You're Measuring Your Career Moves Wrong. Here's What to Track Instead.

I was working on a centralized platform team and I was generally unhappy.

The promotion arrived at the exact moment I started looking for something else. Level 4. Better title. Better bonus trajectory. On paper, the obvious move.

There was also a level 3 studio role with smaller bonuses.

I took the studio job.

A lot of people around me thought that was a mistake. Nobody said it directly. But you can tell. You walk away from a promotion and people look at you the way they look at someone who just turned down a free lunch.

My reason was simple. I decided being happy was more important than being promoted.

The Ruler Everyone Uses

Most career decisions get filtered through two numbers: title and compensation. These are the metrics people compare at dinner tables and in alumni group chats. They are easy to track, easy to compare, and almost entirely beside the point.

Title tells you your rank. It does not tell you whether you are getting better at work you want to do. Compensation tells you what the market assigns to your current position. It does not tell you whether your next five years will build something that makes you more valuable, or just more employed.

The engineers and managers I talk to who feel stuck are usually measuring the right things for the wrong goal. The title might move up and the comp might increase. But the career does not feel like it is building toward anything.

What the Backwards Move Taught Me

The person who backfilled my old role on the platform team is at the same level, years later. Most of my former coworkers eventually left the company. I cannot know for certain what would have happened if I stayed. What I can say is that the trajectory I was on did not look like it was heading somewhere I wanted to go.

The studio was different. The work fit me better. When you are doing work you want to do, you show up differently. You do not have to talk yourself into showing up.

I did something similar again later. My current role was a significant increase in scope and responsibility with no change in title or pay. I had started getting complacent and the opportunity to learn something new seemed like the better trade. The move also opened space for people on my former team to grow into roles they were ready for.

Three years in, I am leading a much larger team. I am still enjoying the learning. Promotions have been discussed and promised.

The Advice I Give College Students

When I talk to university students about careers, I say something that sounds too simple to be useful:

*Do not chase money, chase happy. Happy people do better work, and people who do better work tend to get more recognition.*

This is not an argument against caring about compensation. Compensation matters. This is an argument about what generates it over time.

When I stopped chasing the next promotion and focused on doing the kinds of work I wanted to do, my career accelerated. When I stopped asking permission to do what the business actually needed done, things moved faster. Not more slowly.

The framing I give people when they ask is simple: focus on building skills and doing work you want to do, not on chasing titles. The titles come when they come. The skills stay with you regardless.

The hard part is that this runs directly against how most people measure career momentum. When your peer gets promoted and you moved sideways, the scorecard shows them winning. There is real pressure in that.

Peer groups track promotions. LinkedIn broadcasts levels. What does not appear on LinkedIn is what you are actually getting better at.

The Market Is Already Moving

A 2025 Management Science study from Bidwell and Keller found that lateral moves facilitate career advancement by developing and demonstrating new skills. The effect is especially strong for people who would struggle to get promoted from their current role. The backwards move lets you prove skills you would not have proven staying put.

The promotion market has also tightened significantly. Workday data shows promotions declined in 10 of 11 industries through 2025. The overall promotion rate is down 25% from its 2022 peak. Only 8% of employees were promoted in 2024.

Chasing a promotion in that environment means chasing something increasingly scarce and outside your control.

Sheryl Sandberg made this point in 2013: careers are jungle gyms, not ladders. That framing was useful when it first landed. But it softened the actual reality.

The move can be genuinely backwards: lower title, smaller bonus, less impressive to report at the alumni dinner. And still be exactly right.

What to Ask Before Your Next Decision

Before your next role decision, try replacing the standard filter with a different set of questions.

"Does this role build skills I want to build?" is more useful than "Does this title represent progress?"

"Will I still be engaged in this work six months from now?" is more useful than "Does this comp number beat my current one?"

"What will I be able to do in three years that I cannot do today?" cuts through more noise than most other questions you could ask.

The conventional metrics are not wrong. Title matters. Compensation matters. Neither of them captures what actually generates career momentum over the long run.

The person who took the safe promotion is not always the one who advances. The person who chose work they wanted to do often is.

Monday Action

Write down the two or three skills you most want to build over the next three years. Then look at your current role, or any role you are weighing, and ask honestly whether it builds those things.

If the answer is yes and the title or comp are not what you expected, that is useful information. If the answer is no and the title looks great, that is also useful information.

The comparison you run at the end of that exercise will serve you better than the one most people are running.
