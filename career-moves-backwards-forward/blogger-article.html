Lateral Career Move vs. Promotion: Which One Actually Builds Your Career?

The promotion rate in the United States fell 25% from its 2022 peak. In 2024, only 8% of employees received a promotion. Workday data via Forbes, 2025 shows promotions declined across 10 of 11 industries through 2025.

If your career strategy is built around getting the next promotion, you are betting on something increasingly rare and largely outside your control.

There is a different way to think about career moves. It starts with asking a different question.

Why Lateral Career Moves Drive Promotion: What the Research Shows

A 2025 study published in Management Science by researchers Matthew Bidwell and Erin Keller examined how lateral moves inside organizations affect career advancement. Bidwell and Keller, Management Science, 2025

Their finding: lateral moves facilitate career advancement by developing and demonstrating new skills. The effect is strongest for people who would struggle to get promoted from their current role.

The backwards move creates a skills demonstration the conventional path cannot. That demonstration might come with a lower title, a different scope, or sometimes a pay cut. When you step sideways into a new function, team, or scope of work, you build capabilities that your previous role never required. Those capabilities become visible in ways that staying put never would have allowed.

This runs directly against the instinct most people use to evaluate opportunities. The instinct says: does this move represent progress? Progress, by that definition, means higher title or more money.

The research says something different. Progress means building skills that increase your value over time. A lateral move often does that faster than waiting for the next rung on the ladder.

The Career Comparison Trap Keeping Technical Leaders Stuck

Most career decisions get made with one ruler: title and compensation. These are the numbers people compare at dinner tables and in alumni group chats. They are easy to track, easy to compare, and almost entirely beside the point.

Title tells you your current rank. It does not tell you whether the work will make you more valuable in three years. Compensation tells you what the market currently assigns to your position. It does not predict whether you will be engaged in the work six months from now, or whether that engagement will lead to the kind of output that actually gets recognized.

The engineers and managers I talk to who feel stuck are usually measuring the right things for the wrong goal. The title moves up. The comp increases. But the career does not feel like it is building toward anything.

I have experienced this directly. Twice.

The first time: I was working on a centralized platform team and I was generally unhappy. A promotion arrived at exactly the moment I started looking for something else.

Level 4. Better title. Better bonus trajectory. There was also a level 3 studio role with smaller bonuses.

I took the studio job.

The person who backfilled my old role is at the same level, years later. Most of my former coworkers eventually left the company.

The second time: my current role came with a significant increase in scope and responsibility and no change in title or pay. I had started getting complacent and learning something new seemed like the better trade. The move also opened space for people on my team to grow into roles they were ready for.

Three years in, I am leading a much larger team. The promotions are still being discussed and promised.

The title and comp comparison told the wrong story both times. The skills and engagement comparison told the right one.

The Technical Leader Promotion Gap: What Title-Chasing Misses

Sheryl Sandberg made the point in 2013 that careers are jungle gyms, not ladders. Stanford GSB, 2013 That framing was useful. But it softened the actual reality.

The move does not just have to be sideways. It can be genuinely backwards: lower title, smaller bonus, less impressive on the next resume line. And still be the right call.

The popular version of the advice says lateral is acceptable. The honest version says backwards can be right. There is a meaningful difference between those two positions.

When I talk to university students about careers, I give them something that sounds too simple to be useful: do not chase money, chase happy. Happy people do better work, and people who do better work tend to get more recognition.

The framing I give people when they ask is simple: focus on building skills and doing work you want to do, not on chasing titles. The titles come when they come. The skills stay with you regardless.

This is not an argument against compensation. It is an argument about sequencing. Optimize for skills and engagement first. Recognition tends to follow.

How to Assess a Role's Skill-Building Potential Before You Accept

Most role evaluations stop at comp and title comparison. This one goes further.

Question 1: What skills will I be able to demonstrate in this role that I cannot demonstrate now?

Not "what will I learn." That is too vague. The question is: what will I be able to prove on record that I currently cannot? A role that lets you demonstrate capabilities you have not yet proven is more valuable than one that requires skills you already have but pays better.

Question 2: Will I still be engaged in this work twelve months from now?

Not six months. Twelve. The six-month question is about novelty. The twelve-month question is about fit. If you cannot honestly say yes at the twelve-month mark, the comp bump will not fix the underlying problem.

Question 3: Does this role build new professional networks or organizational visibility I do not currently have?

Lateral moves into new functions, teams, or business areas build cross-functional relationships that staying put cannot. Those relationships create opportunities that do not appear on the formal promotion track.

Question 4: In three years, will this role have made me more valuable, regardless of the title on my business card?

This is the three-year test. Not "will I get promoted." Not "will I be paid more." Will the skills, relationships, and experiences this role builds make me a stronger candidate for anything I choose to pursue in three years?

If the answer is yes and the title is a step down, that is information worth acting on.

The Three Questions That Replace the Standard Career Filter

Before your next role decision, try swapping the comparison most people use for a different set of questions.

"Does this role build skills I want to build?" is more useful than "Does this title represent progress?"

"Will I still want to be here in a year?" is more useful than "Does this comp beat my current number?"

"What will I be able to do in three years that I cannot do today?" cuts through more noise than most other questions you could ask.

The conventional metrics are not wrong. Title matters. Compensation matters. Neither of them captures what actually generates career momentum over the long run.

The person who took the safe promotion is not always the one who advances. The person who chose work they wanted to do often is.

Monday Action

Before your next role conversation, write down the two or three skills you most want to build in the next three years. Then look at the role in front of you and ask honestly: does this build those things?

If yes, the title and comp comparison becomes much less important. If no, the comp is compensating you for something you did not actually want to spend the next three years doing.
