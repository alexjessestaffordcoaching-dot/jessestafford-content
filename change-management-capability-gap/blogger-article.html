How to Lead Organizational Change Without Just Announcing It

A leader I know sent an email to her whole department. New process, effective immediately. She had spent two weeks getting sign-off from three different VPs to make it happen, and she was proud of the announcement itself.

Three months later, almost nobody was following the new process. Not because they had not seen the email. Because seeing the email was never the hard part.

That gap between the announcement and the actual change is not a communication failure. It is the single biggest reason change initiatives fail, and almost nobody names it directly.

The Change Leadership Gap Nobody Talks About

Change management has an entire industry built around it. Kotter's eight steps. ADKAR. Consultants who will walk your leadership team through a framework binder.

None of it explains why the failure rate has stayed roughly the same for decades.

Here is what the data actually shows. Leader-reported preparedness to manage change has nearly halved in five years, from 25 percent down to 13 percent, even as companies spend more on change management training than ever. The frameworks are multiplying. The capability is not.

That is the change leadership gap. Not a missing framework. A missing behavior that no framework, by itself, can install.

The Sourced Fact

DDI's 2026 Global Leadership Forecast, drawn from more than 100,000 frontline, mid-level, and executive leaders, found that only 8 percent of executives are strong at leading through change. The gap is worst at the top, not the bottom: frontline leaders report 15 percent strong, mid-level leaders 30 percent strong, and executives sit lowest of all at 8 percent. Source: DDI, "DDI Data Reveals Only 8% of Executives Are Strong at Leading Through Change" (PR Newswire, 2026)

That number undercuts the common assumption that junior leaders just need more reps before they are ready to run change. The opposite is true. The more senior a leader gets, the more likely their change leadership sits on being good at announcing something, not creating it.

Announcing Change Is Not the Same Skill as Creating It

Here is the distinction almost every change-management framework skips past. Announcing change is visible, rewarded, and low effort. You get a moment where the room is looking at you, and you get credit simply for having addressed the problem out loud.

Creating change is invisible. It means retiring an old process instead of adding a new one next to it. It means changing what actually gets measured and rewarded, not just what gets said in a memo. It means staying present for weeks while the new way is clunky and half-working, instead of walking away the moment the announcement is made.

Roughly 88 percent of major transformations fail to deliver what they promised, according to Bain. McKinsey has cited a 70 percent failure rate on change initiatives for years running, and that number has not meaningfully moved despite a decade of new frameworks designed to fix it. See Mooncamp's compiled change management statistics for the full data set.

The frameworks are not failing because they are poorly designed. They are failing because leaders stop at the part the framework can actually teach: the plan and the announcement. The part after that has to be lived, not templated.

I have written before about how context beats process every time when you are trying to get people to move fast on something new. The same principle applies here. A new process bolted onto an unchanged incentive structure is not context. It is just more paperwork asking people to do the same thing they already did, with an extra step.

Three Questions That Separate Announcing From Creating

Before you count something as change you led, not just change you announced, run it through three checks.

Did I remove something, not just add something? A new process layered on top of the old one is not change. It is more work, with the old habits still fully intact underneath it. If nothing got removed, nothing actually changed.

Did I change what gets measured or rewarded? People keep doing whatever earns them recognition, no matter what the announcement said. If the old behavior still earns the same praise it always did, the new behavior will not survive contact with a busy week.

Am I still in it six weeks later, not just on stage the day of? The announcement is a single moment that takes an afternoon. The real work is what you do in the six weeks after, when the new way is uncomfortable and it would be easier to quietly let everyone slide back to what they already knew.

If the honest answer to all three is no, you made an announcement. That outcome is common enough to be the default, not a personal failure. The leaders who eventually get trusted with the next transformation are the ones who noticed what they'd skipped and closed it, not the ones who never missed it in the first place.

What Changing the Measurement Actually Looks Like

This is the part most change efforts skip entirely, and it deserves its own example.

I once worked with a scrum team that had no real sense of whether their sprints were worth what they cost. So I calculated it. I took the fully loaded cost of the team, roughly 25,000 dollars for a two-week sprint, and brought that number into the retro.

The question was simple: did we produce 25,000 dollars of value to the company this sprint? Not "did we finish our tickets." Not "did we hit velocity." Did the output actually justify the cost.

That single change in what got discussed, moving the conversation from task completion to business value, did more to shift how the team prioritized than any announcement about "focusing on impact" ever could have. Nobody needed a memo about caring more. They needed the actual measurement in the room to change, and once it did, their own conversation changed with it.

That is what changing what gets measured looks like in practice. Not a new KPI dashboard nobody checks. A number, in the room, that changes what the team argues about.
