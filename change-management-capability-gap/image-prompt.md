## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A leader announces a change and treats the announcement itself as the finish line, while the real work, the part that actually makes the change stick, is invisible and happens somewhere else entirely.
**Context box text:** ANNOUNCING THE CHANGE IS NOT THE SAME AS LEADING IT
**Emotional tone:** Direct and focused. This is systems and accountability content, not a people-connection piece. Jesse's expression should be clear-eyed and steady, someone mid-way through unglamorous work, not performing effort for an audience.

## Concept 1 — The System

**Visual metaphor:** A ceremonial lever mounted on a spotlighted podium, wired with a cable that trails off and lies unplugged on the floor, disconnected from the actual machine, a large network of gears and pipes, running in the background.
**First-second read:** A shiny lever sits on a podium under a spotlight, cable dangling loose and unconnected to anything. Off to the side, a man has his hand on a real valve embedded in the working machinery. The viewer reads it immediately: the switch that looks like it does something is not wired to the machine that actually runs.
**Compositional approach:** Jesse is positioned close and deliberate in the lower-left third of the frame, at real scale, working directly at the actual machinery. The ceremonial podium and lever sit in the upper-right background, smaller in the composition despite being visually ornate, cable trailing uselessly to the floor.

### Full Expanded Prompt

COMPOSITION — most important: Wide shot, 16:9. Lower-left third of the frame: Jesse standing close to camera, at true scale, one hand resting on a real valve wheel that is part of a larger network of exposed gears, pipes, and pressure gauges filling the left and center background. Upper-right background, smaller and more distant in the frame: a spotlighted podium holding a large ornate ceremonial lever, a loose cable hanging from its base and coiling unconnected on the floor beneath it, nowhere near the actual machinery. Jesse is looking back over his shoulder toward the podium, not toward camera. Negative space in the upper-left keeps the panel from feeling crowded.

ART STYLE: Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision, not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching. Lines should suggest a technical patent drawing or a well-crafted indie comic panel. Loose, organic watercolor fills, visibly hand-painted. Color intentionally bleeds slightly outside the ink lines or leaves small white gaps at boundaries. Wet-edge textures and transparency variations are required, these are features, not errors. No digital gradients. No glow effects. No perfectly aligned fills. Background: clean white paper texture (#ffffff) with steampunk mechanical elements, objects have exposed mechanics, rendered in charcoal linework as abstract environmental texture. Mechanical background elements suggest systems and structure without being literal or dominant. 16:9 aspect ratio, single panel.

COLOR PALETTE: Primary watercolor wash: #100299 (deep blue). Canvas and negative space: #ffffff. Linework and shadows: #333333. Shadows are a semi-transparent charcoal watercolor wash, never solid black fill. Supporting colors are allowed for depth and warmth but must not compete with the blue/white/charcoal triad. Use a warm brass or amber wash on the podium lever and spotlight to make its disconnection visually obvious against the cooler blue-grey machinery Jesse is working at.

PRIMARY CHARACTER: Man in his early 40s. Short buzz-cut hair. Neatly trimmed short beard and mustache. Brown eyes. V-neck long sleeve shirt in deep blue (#100299) watercolor wash. Charcoal pants. Relaxed, unposed, sleeves pushed up, weight settled into his stance like he has been at this valve a while. One hand on the real valve wheel, expression direct and focused, steady, looking back over his shoulder toward the disconnected podium lever without alarm, just clear-eyed recognition.

SCENE DESCRIPTION: The frame is split between two systems. On the left and center, exposed gears, pipes, and pressure gauges form a real working mechanism, rendered with visible wear, the kind of machine that has been running and needs a hand on it. Jesse stands at its edge, engaged with it. In the upper right, smaller and more distant, a spotlighted ceremonial podium holds a large ornate lever, polished and prominent, with a cable that visibly fails to connect to anything, coiling loose on the floor beneath the podium. No other people in frame. Negative space in the upper left keeps the panel open.

CONTEXT BOX: Uppercase sans-serif text inside a solid white (#ffffff) rectangle with a sharp charcoal (#333333) border. Placed at the extreme bottom of the panel. Text: ANNOUNCING THE CHANGE IS NOT THE SAME AS LEADING IT

TONE GUARDRAILS: This image should feel like two people talking through a real problem in plain language. Not a motivational poster. Not corporate (no handshakes, whiteboards, or team huddles). Not inspirational abstraction. Grounded, human, and specific. The mechanical background supports the scene, it does not compete with it.

In the background, through a doorway, an empty podium and spotlight are still lit from an event that has clearly ended. The viewer reads it immediately: the real work happens in a different room than the announcement, after the applause has stopped, and it takes more than one person.
**Compositional approach:** Jesse and the two colleagues are placed at equal scale and working side by side, a peer dynamic, not Jesse directing them. The announcement stage is smaller, distant, and visible only through the doorway, deliberately deemphasized.

## Concept 3 — The Reframe

**Unexpected angle:** Ironic juxtaposition. A grand ribbon-cutting ceremony, oversized ceremonial scissors, cut ribbon still fluttering, sits at the entrance of a building. Through the open doorway behind it, the building is visibly still under construction, scaffolding and exposed framing, unfinished.
**First-second read:** The ribbon has already been cut, the ceremony is clearly over. But the doorway behind it opens onto a building that is obviously not done. The viewer gets the irony immediately: they celebrated finishing something that is still, very visibly, not finished.
**Why it works:** It renders the entire post's argument in a single incongruous image, the ceremony for completion happened, but the actual structure behind it proves the work was never actually done, which is funnier and sharper than a literal illustration of the same idea.
