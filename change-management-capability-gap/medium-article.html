What's the Difference Between Announcing Change and Actually Leading It?

I have sat in enough all-hands to know the pattern by heart. A VP steps up. New process, new initiative, sometimes a rebrand of the whole way the org works.

Slides. A confident line about culture. Applause.

Six months later almost nothing has actually changed. The same VP is genuinely confused about why.

That confusion is not rare. It is the default outcome. Only 8% of executives report being strong at leading through change, according to DDI's 2026 Global Leadership Forecast.

Five years ago, a quarter of leaders said they felt prepared to manage change. Now it is 13%. The number is getting worse, not better, even as every company claims change management as a core competency.

The Announcement Isn't the Change

Here is the part almost nobody names out loud. Announcing change is the visible, rewarded, low effort part of the job. You stand up, you say the words, and you get credit for having addressed it.

The actual work is invisible. Removing the old process. Changing what gets measured. Staying in the mess while the new way is clunky and not fully working yet.

Nobody claps for that part, so leaders skip straight past it and call the announcement the finish line.

Roughly 88% of major transformations fail to deliver what they promised, according to Bain. McKinsey has cited a 70% failure rate on change initiatives for years, and that number has barely moved despite a decade of frameworks built to fix it. The frameworks are not the problem. Doing the second half of the job is.

More Communication Is Not the Fix

I see leaders who sense something is missing reach for the same lever. Communicate more. Repeat the message.

Run a Q&A session, then another one. Follow the steps in whatever change framework got handed down at the last leadership offsite.

That is not wrong exactly. It just answers the wrong question. I've sat through the extra Q&A sessions leaders add when they think communication is the problem.

They don't fix it, because the team already understood the plan the first time. What people are actually watching for is whether anything about how work gets done, or what gets rewarded, is going to change.

Volume of communication without a visible change in the mechanics reads as noise, not action. I've watched teams clock the exact moment a leader stops mentioning an initiative, and that is when they know the difference between talk and a real shift. Over-communicating an announcement is still just repeating the announcement.

What Creating It Actually Looks Like

A compliance group I worked with was drowning. Every question, every clarification, every update went out as one email blast to more than a hundred people. It cost the team eight hours a day just responding to the noise, and it still was not working. Regulators kept finding gaps because the right information was not reaching the right person.

The easy version of this fix is an announcement. Tell the group to communicate better. Send a memo about email hygiene.

Ask everyone to be more targeted going forward. That version gets a leader credit for addressing the problem without ever touching it.

We did something slower. We retired the email blast entirely and replaced it with a process built on project tracking tools, routing each question straight to the person who could actually answer it. That meant sitting with the group while the new system was half working, fixing what broke, and staying present until it actually held. Context about why the change mattered did more work here than any new rule could have.

It got the team eight hours back every single day. It also led to the company's first ever clean audit from regulators. Not because we announced a new way of communicating. Because we removed the old way and stayed until the new one worked.

Three Questions That Tell You Which One You Did

Before you count a change as led, not just announced, run it through three checks.

Did I remove something, not just add something? A new process bolted on top of the old one is not change. It is more work. If nothing got removed, nothing actually changed.

Did I change what gets measured or rewarded? People keep doing what gets recognized, no matter what you said in the meeting. If the old behavior still earns the same praise, the new behavior will not stick.

Am I still in it six weeks later, not just on stage the day of? The announcement is a single moment. The real work is what you do in the weeks after, when the new way is uncomfortable and it would be easier to quietly let people slide back to the old one.

If the honest answer to all three is no, you made an announcement. That is not a personal failure. It is the default outcome for 92% of leaders. But naming what you skipped is the first move toward closing it, and the leaders who close it are the ones who get trusted with the next transformation.
