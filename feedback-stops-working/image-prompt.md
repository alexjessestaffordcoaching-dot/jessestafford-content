## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A leader keeps giving feedback on the same behavior and it keeps coming back — not because the person is resistant, but because the structural trigger that produces the behavior was never changed. You cannot tell electricity to flow somewhere else; you have to build a different circuit.

**Context box text:** THE BEHAVIOR ISN'T THE PROBLEM. THE PATH IS.

**Emotional tone:** Direct and focused — this is systems/structural content, not an emotional moment. Jesse's expression stays clear-eyed and analytical throughout, a man tracing a circuit rather than managing a feeling.

## Concept 1 — The Evidence

**The artifact:** A real electrical breaker panel, wall-mounted, where one breaker switch shows heavy wear — scorch discoloration and a loose, worn toggle — from being flipped back on over and over, while every other breaker in the panel looks untouched. The post's own metaphor made literal: the same trip, the same reset, the same place, every time.

**Setting:** A real utility closet or garage wall with a mounted breaker panel, the kind every building has.

**First-second read:** One switch in a grid of identical switches is visibly worn and discolored — reads instantly as "this one thing keeps happening here, over and over," before any text is read.

**Compositional approach:** Jesse stands close and deliberate, directly in front of the panel, at human scale, one hand raised near the worn breaker without touching it — arriving at the insight, not fixing it in this frame.

### Full Expanded Prompt

COMPOSITION — most important: Medium-close shot, straight-on, inside a utility closet. A wall-mounted breaker panel fills the center-right two-thirds of the frame, its door open, rows of identical toggle switches visible inside. Jesse stands left-of-center, close to the panel, human scale, angled toward it, one hand raised near chest height toward one specific switch in the third row without touching it. Negative space fills the upper-left corner above his shoulder.

MECHANICAL ENVIRONMENT — second most important: The breaker panel is the scene's one clear mechanical object — each switch shows a visible toggle-and-spring mechanism, with the panel door's hinge visible on its left edge. One switch, third row, is discolored with faint scorch shading around its base and its toggle sits slightly looser than the others, rendered with a subtle worn texture. All switches are otherwise identical and unlettered — no labels, no directory card in the panel door's holder (the holder itself is empty and blank). The charcoal schematic linework occupies the upper-left negative space above Jesse, rendered as blueprint annotation of the worn switch's internal toggle-and-spring mechanism enlarged. The S mark is worked into the schematic as a small cast plate on the panel door's hinge.

Art style: Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision — not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching. Lines should suggest a technical patent drawing or a well-crafted indie comic panel. Loose, organic watercolor fills — visibly hand-painted. Color intentionally bleeds slightly outside the ink lines or leaves small white gaps at boundaries. Wet-edge textures and transparency variations are required — these are features, not errors. No digital gradients. No glow effects. No perfectly aligned fills. Base canvas: clean white paper texture (#ffffff). Single panel.

**Aspect ratio:** 16:9

Color palette: Primary watercolor wash: #100299 (deep blue). Canvas and negative space: #ffffff. Linework and shadows: #333333. Shadows are a semi-transparent charcoal watercolor wash — never solid black fill. The charcoal schematic layer stays unpainted linework. Supporting colors are allowed for depth and warmth but must not compete with the blue/white/charcoal triad.

Primary character: Man in his early 40s. Short buzz-cut hair. Neatly trimmed short beard and mustache. Brown eyes. V-neck long sleeve shirt in deep blue (#100299) watercolor wash. Charcoal pants. Relaxed, unposed — never stiff or formal. Standing close to the panel, one hand raised near the worn switch without touching it, expression clear-eyed and focused — tracing the pattern, not fixing it yet.

Scene description: A utility closet, single bare bulb overhead rendered as a soft light wash. The open breaker panel fills most of the right frame, rows of identical toggle switches, all unlettered. One switch, third row, shows faint scorch discoloration and a slightly loose toggle. The panel door's directory-card holder is empty and blank. Jesse stands close, hand raised toward that one switch. Negative space fills the upper-left above him.

Not inspirational abstraction. Grounded, human, and specific. The setting is a real place. ## Concept 3 — The Reframe

**Unexpected angle:** Environmental inversion. Trade the electrical metaphor for its plumbing cousin — water finds the path of least resistance too. Show a basement or utility pipe run where one joint has been patched over and over, layers of repair tape and clamps stacked on the same spot, while an alternate, unused pipe route sits nearby, ready to carry the flow a different way.

**Setting:** A real basement or utility room with exposed pipe runs along the wall and ceiling.

**First-second read:** One pipe joint wrapped in multiple layers of mismatched tape and clamps, clearly repaired many times, sitting right next to a clean, unused length of pipe — reads instantly as "they keep patching the same spot instead of rerouting it."

**Why it works:** Switching from electricity to water keeps the same "path of least resistance" physics from the post but forces a second look — the viewer has to notice it's a different mechanism telling the same story, which is exactly the moment of recognition the post is asking for.

An unused, clean length of alternate pipe runs parallel a few feet to its left, clearly connectable but not yet connected, a capped valve at its end. Jesse stands left-of-center, human scale, looking between the patched joint and the unused alternate route. Negative space fills the upper third of the frame above the ceiling pipes.
