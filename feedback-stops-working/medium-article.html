What Do You Do When Feedback Stops Working?

Quick Bridge: When the same behavior keeps coming back after feedback, most managers conclude the person is resistant or unwilling to change. The real problem is structural. The path that produces the behavior was never redesigned, and no amount of feedback changes that. You leave this post with a different question to ask and a different place to look.

---

I had a direct report who had visible negative reactions to new information. A change in scope, a surprise announcement, something that affected their team. Those reactions landed in front of whoever was in the room.

I gave feedback more than once. The conversations went well. Two weeks later, the same pattern.

I spent a long time trying to figure out what was wrong with them.

That was the wrong question.

---

If feedback alone could change a repeated behavior, it would have worked the first time.

What took me longer to understand is that people follow the path of least resistance by default. Not because they are resistant or broken. Because that is how behavior works.

If a condition in the environment keeps creating the same trigger, the behavior keeps returning. The feedback is not the circuit. The structure is.

Robert Fritz wrote about this idea in the 1980s and James Clear brought a version of it into the mainstream with Atomic Habits. Most leaders apply it to their own habits. Very few apply it to their teams.

McKinsey puts the failure rate of change initiatives at 70 percent. The reason cited most often is employee resistance. But resistance is rarely the real cause.

The environment was never actually changed. Leaders gave better instructions and called it a redesign.

---

Why the Standard Feedback Loop Keeps Failing You

Here is what the standard feedback loop looks like for most managers.

You notice a behavior. You give feedback, the person acknowledges it, and things improve briefly. A few weeks pass. The behavior returns.

You give more feedback. You start to wonder if they are actually listening. You start thinking about their attitude. You start counting conversations toward a future performance plan.

The behavior came back because the path came back.

You did not change the trigger. You did not change the condition. You changed the instruction. The path of least resistance still runs through the same place.

Electricity does not stop flowing because you asked it to go somewhere else. You have to build a different circuit.

What Circuit Design Looks Like

The change I made with that direct report was simple. I stopped expecting them to manage the reaction before it happened. I started changing when and how information reached them.

I made a point to share things with them ahead as much as possible. Then let them overreact while I was still there and get it out of their system.

That was the circuit. Information arrives early. The reaction happens in front of me, privately.

By the time the next team meeting comes, the reaction is spent. They show up settled.

I did not tell them to react differently. I changed the path the information traveled.

That is circuit design. Map the behavior back to its structural trigger. Then change the trigger.

There are three moves that make this concrete.

Find the condition, not the personality. What consistently precedes this behavior? Information arriving late is a condition. A metric that creates the wrong incentive is a condition.

A process that requires the harder thing by default is another. Personality is not something you can change with a structural intervention. Conditions are.

Change the condition. If information arriving late triggers a defensive reaction, change when it arrives. If a meeting format reliably produces conflict, change the format.

If a report has to go around three people to get a straight answer, shorten the path. Make the right behavior the path of least resistance.

Add friction to the old path. This is the part most people miss. Circuit design sounds like making good behavior easier.

That is half of it. Sometimes you also have to make the old behavior harder.

I once changed the goals for a QA team I had just taken over. Their goals had been individual metrics they fully controlled. I changed them to team-level metrics that required them to work differently with the rest of the game team.

They pushed back hard. They said it was not fair, that there was nothing they could do to make the new goals work. What I recognized was grief, not resistance. They were mourning the way it used to work.

The behavior changed. Not because I convinced them. Because the old path was gone.

Adding friction to the old path is not punishment. It is completing the circuit.

The Question Worth Asking

If you have given feedback on the same behavior more than three times and it keeps returning, stop asking what is wrong with the person.

Start asking what the structure keeps producing.

What condition in their environment makes the current behavior the easiest option? What would have to change to make the better behavior the easier path?

This is not about lowering your expectations. It is about shifting your target. Most of the time, the behavior you keep seeing is not a character flaw.

It makes sense given the path in front of that person. The path is doing exactly what paths do.

Building a circuit means asking: where is the current actually going, and what would I have to change to redirect it?

This connects directly to a frame I have written about before: context changes behavior more reliably than process does. When you redesign the information flow, the timing of conversations, or the structure that produces the trigger, you are changing context, not rules. What to Do This Monday

Pick one behavior you have given feedback on more than twice. Write down the last time it happened. Ask yourself: what was the trigger? What condition came before it?

What is the path of least resistance that keeps producing this?

You do not need a management theory to answer that question. You need fifteen minutes and a willingness to stop diagnosing the person and start looking at the path.

What part of the structure keeps producing this?
