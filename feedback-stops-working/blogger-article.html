Why Your Feedback Keeps Failing (And What to Build Instead)

Seventy percent of organizational change initiatives fail to achieve their goals. The reason most commonly cited is employee resistance.

That number is from McKinsey research on change management. Most managers accept it as a frustrating fact of working with people. (Change Management Statistics, mooncamp.com)

But resistance is not the real explanation. The environment that produced the behavior was never changed. Leaders swapped the instruction and left the structure in place.

Your team does not resist change because of their personalities. They follow the path of least resistance. If the path has not changed, the behavior will not change either.

---

Why the Standard Feedback Loop Fails Engineering Managers

The feedback loop most managers run looks like this.

You spot a behavior that is causing problems. You bring it up in a 1-on-1. You are clear, specific, and fair. The behavior improves for a few weeks.

Then it returns. You give more feedback. The cycle repeats.

Most managers run this loop for months before recognizing that the loop itself is the problem. Feedback addresses behavior after the fact. It does not address the condition that produced the behavior.

Robert Fritz wrote about this in his work on structural tension in organizations: the path of least resistance is determined by structure, not by the intentions of the people in it. Without changing the underlying structure, change efforts produce temporary improvement, and then the behavior returns. (Path of Least Resistance, robertfritz.com)

James Clear made a version of this accessible in Atomic Habits: "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." Most readers applied this to their personal habits. Very few applied it to how they manage their teams. (Power of Environment, jamesclear.com)

The engineering managers who stop the feedback loop are not better at feedback. They are better at asking what in the environment keeps producing the behavior they want to change.

---

The Environment Design Shift That Changes Engineering Manager Communication

Here is the shift: stop treating behavior as a choice to be corrected and start treating it as a signal from the environment.

When a direct report keeps doing the same thing after repeated feedback, the question is not "why does this person keep doing this?" The question is: what in their environment keeps producing this?

I had a direct report who had visible negative reactions every time new information arrived. A scope change, a surprise announcement, anything that touched their team without warning. Those reactions landed in front of whoever was in the room.

I gave feedback more than once. The conversations went well. The pattern returned.

What I changed was not my message. I changed when and how information reached them. I made a point to share things with them ahead as much as possible. Then let them have the reaction in front of me, privately, so it was out of their system before it could reach the team.

By the time the next team meeting came, they showed up settled. I did not change the person. I changed the path the information traveled.

That is environment design. You are not fixing the behavior. You are redesigning the structure that produces it.

What Leaders Get Wrong: The Additive Bias

Here is why this shift is hard to make.

Behavioral scientist Leidy Klotz published research in Nature showing that humans have a cognitive bias toward addition. When we want to improve a situation, our first instinct is to add something: more feedback, more check-ins, more rules, more oversight.

The instinct to solve a problem by removing or redesigning something requires deliberate effort. Adding is the default. Subtracting is not. (The Power of Less, dameleadership.com)

For engineering managers, this pattern plays out constantly. A direct report misses a handoff, so you add a check-in meeting. Someone keeps talking over others in standups, so you add a norm. A team member overreacts to new information, so you add another feedback conversation.

All additions. None of them changed the structure.

The environment design move is often a subtraction or a substitution, not an addition. What condition could you remove? What meeting could you change?

What information flow could you shorten? What approval step is creating the wrong incentive?

The leaders who redirect behavior without burning out are not doing more. They are doing something different.

Three Moves to Stop Repeating the Same Feedback Conversation

Once you are looking at the structure instead of the person, three moves become available.

Find the condition, not the personality. What consistently comes before this behavior? Information arriving late is a condition. A metric that measures the wrong outcome is a condition.

A process that makes the correct behavior harder than the incorrect one is another.

Personality is not something you can redesign. The environment is.

Change the condition. If information arriving late triggers a defensive reaction, change when it arrives. If a meeting format reliably produces conflict, change the format. If a direct report has to navigate three layers to get a straight answer, shorten the path.

Make the behavior you want the easier option.

Add friction to the old path. This is the move that most environment-design advice misses. Sometimes you have to make the old behavior harder, not just make the new behavior easier.

I once changed the goals for a QA team I had just taken over. Their previous goals had been individual metrics they fully controlled. I changed them to team-level metrics that required them to work differently with the rest of the game team.

The team pushed back hard. They said it was not fair, that there was nothing they could do to make the new goals work. What I recognized was grief, not resistance. They were mourning the way it used to work.

The behavior changed. Not because I convinced them. Because the old path was gone.

Adding friction to the old path is not punishment. It is completing the circuit.

---

What to Do This Monday

Pick one behavior you have given feedback on more than twice.

Write down the last time it happened. Ask yourself: what was the condition that came before it? What is the structure that keeps producing this behavior?

Then ask the harder question: what would I have to remove, change, or redesign to make the behavior I want require less effort than the behavior I am trying to stop?

You do not need a management framework. You need fifteen minutes and a willingness to look at the structure instead of the person.
