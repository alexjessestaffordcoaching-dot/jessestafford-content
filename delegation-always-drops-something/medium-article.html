---

What Actually Happens to the Work You Delegate

I found out in a skip-level 1-on-1.

A few months earlier, I had delegated a weekly status reporting process to my engineering manager. It was eating up two to three hours of my time each week. She was a strong leader and more than capable of owning it.

What I did not know: she was already deep in a platform migration. She handed the reporting task to one of her engineers to put together each week. That engineer was already running behind on a documentation backlog from the previous release.

The documentation quietly dropped. Nothing broke immediately. Nothing surfaced.

Three months later, I was sitting across from that engineer in a skip-level conversation. I asked what had picked up unexpectedly over the past few months.

She told me about the weekly reporting process.

I asked what she had set aside to make room for it.

That is when I found out about the documentation backlog. The same documentation I had assumed was current. I had delegated something, felt the relief of it leaving my plate, and moved on.

The work had moved too. It just moved somewhere I stopped watching.

---

The Physics of Delegation

Here is what delegation actually does.

You delegate because you are out of time. The person you hand work to is already busy. Because they are already busy, they drop something lower-priority or pass the work further down.

And so it goes until the chain runs out of people. At that point, something has to fall out the bottom.

Delegation does not remove work from the organization. It moves the work to someone who has less authority to push back on it and less standing to say anything when they get overloaded.

The total amount of work in the system stays roughly the same. It just concentrates somewhere you are no longer watching.

This is not a failure of the person you delegated to. It is not a planning mistake. It is how capacity works inside any organization.

Every team is already carrying something. When new work arrives without a conversation about what gives, something gives quietly. And creates stress that not everything is getting done.

The relief you feel after handing something off is real. But it is your relief, not the system's.

A Different Kind of Accountability

Most writing on delegation focuses on the handoff. Give context, set clear expectations, check in regularly, and build autonomy over time.

If you are still working out how to hand things off without burning your team out, that is the right starting point. I wrote more on that here: [How to Delegate Without Burning Out Your Team].

This post starts where that one ends.

Even when you delegate well, the work still moves. The cascade still happens. What changes is not whether something gets dropped, but whether you know what got dropped.

That is a harder question. Not many leaders stop to ask it.

It means accepting that handing something off does not end your relationship with it. You have to follow the work further than your direct report. And you have to recognize that something you set in motion probably landed on someone who had no standing to say "I do not have room for this."

This is not an argument against delegation. The alternative, holding onto work until you cannot function, creates a different kind of problem. I wrote about that one too: [You Created the Dependency Trap].

Under-delegation and delegation both create problems. The difference is which ones you can see.

The goal is not to delegate perfectly. The goal is to understand what you are trading.

Once you accept that something will always move down the chain, the job changes. You are not trying to prevent the cascade. You are trying to stay aware of it. And you are trying to make sure what falls out the bottom is the thing you would have chosen to let go, not the thing that dropped because no one knew how to surface it.

---

Following the Work Down

The skip-level 1-on-1 is one of the few tools that lets you see the end of the chain.

When you talk to your direct reports, you hear what is happening at their level. You do not hear what has been absorbed below them. The skip-level is where that changes.

A few questions that surface cascade work:

"What picked up this month that you did not plan for?"

"What has had to take a back seat to make room for it?"

"Is there anything that has been quietly falling behind that I should know about?"

"If your manager asked you right now what was at risk, what would you say?"

These are not audit questions. People rarely volunteer capacity problems unprompted. Saying "I am overloaded" to their own manager can feel like saying the manager made a bad call.

The skip-level creates more distance. With that distance comes more honesty.

If you have never run a structured skip-level conversation before, I wrote a full guide: [A Guide for Skip Level One-On-Ones].

Once you hear the answer, you decide what to do with it. Sometimes what fell out was the right thing to fall out. A documentation backlog that slipped for one sprint may be genuinely lower-priority than the work that replaced it.

The conversation gives you the information. You still make the call.

Other times, what fell out was not fine. The cascade landed on something that actually mattered, and nobody surfaced it because nobody knew how to say "I cannot absorb this." In those cases, the skip-level gives you the chance to redirect before the cost becomes visible to everyone else.

---

The engineer I mentioned was not complaining. She had absorbed the extra work, made it fit, and moved on.

She was not burned out. She was not resentful. She was doing what people do when work lands without a conversation about capacity: she made it work and dropped something quieter.

I was the one who had set that in motion months earlier.

That is the part most delegation advice does not prepare you for. The cascade does not feel like a problem while it is happening. It feels like a successful handoff. The cost shows up later, somewhere else, on someone who did not have the standing to question it.

The skip-level is not a performance management tool. It is a visibility tool. It shows you what your team is carrying because of decisions you made and gives you the chance to decide if that is the right trade.

---

Monday Action

Before your next handoff, have a different conversation than usual. Ask the person receiving the work: "What do you have capacity for right now, and what might have to give?"

You do not need a full analysis. You just need that conversation to happen before the cascade starts instead of three months after.
