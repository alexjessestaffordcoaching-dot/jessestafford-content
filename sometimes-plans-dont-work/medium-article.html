How to Navigate Your Career When It Doesn't Go According to Plan

You are probably better at your work than you are at making sure the right people can see it.

That is not a criticism. Technical leaders are trained to build things and solve problems. Nobody trained them to make that work visible to the people who have any say in where their career goes next.

The leaders who get stuck are often not the ones with the weakest skills. They are the ones whose work is hardest to see from the outside. Their managers do not know what shipped last quarter. Their skip-level cannot name what they have been working on. The senior leaders making decisions about promotions and opportunities have never had a reason to know their name.

Good work, consistently invisible, does not move careers. Visible work does.

---

Why Technical Leaders Go Invisible

The instincts that make technical leaders effective in their work are the same ones that make them invisible in the organization.

They wait until something is done before mentioning it. Saying something is "in progress" feels unprofessional. It invites questions they cannot answer yet, and it feels like taking credit before it is earned.

They assume good work will be noticed. If the outcome is real, the recognition will follow. They trust the meritocracy to sort it out.

They stay inside their technical context. Explaining what they built in nontechnical terms feels like dumbing it down, or overselling something they are not sure anyone else will understand.

Each of these instincts is reasonable in isolation. Each of them creates the same problem: a manager who cannot tell you what their best engineer is working on, and a technical leader who cannot understand why their career is not moving.

---

The Part You Cannot Control

Careers are roughly 25% luck and 75% you. The luck component is what you cannot control: your projects, your managers, your organizational environment.

Your strongest project gets cancelled before launch. Your biggest advocate gets promoted to a different team. A reorg puts three years of solid work under a new VP who has never heard your name. The team you helped build gets cut in a round of layoffs that had nothing to do with performance.

These things happen to people doing excellent work, constantly. They are not personal. They are the 25%.

You cannot eliminate them. What you can do is operate in a way that puts you in position to take advantage of luck when it shows up, and to hold your ground when it does not. That is the 75%.

---

Show Your Work

The most important behavior in the 75% is showing your work. And most technical leaders are not doing it.

Showing your work is not self-promotion. It is translation. Your technical work is invisible to anyone who does not share your context. You understand what you built and why it matters. The people who have any say in your career often do not. Your job is to close that gap, consistently and intentionally, before something goes wrong.

The manager who asks what you are working on is not micromanaging. They are blind to work that was not shared with them in a context that made sense to them. That is what this practice fixes.

It has a specific sequence.

Step 1: Share your plans.

This is the step most technical leaders skip entirely. Before a project begins, share what you are working toward and how you plan to work through it.

Not a full briefing. A forward-looking statement: "We are starting the authentication refactor this sprint. The goal is to close the security gap legal flagged. Here is the order we are going to tackle the pieces."

Your manager cannot advocate for something they do not know is in motion. Your skip-level cannot route opportunities your way if they do not know what you are building toward. The update before the work starts is often more valuable than the one at the end.

Step 2: Update, update, update.

As the work moves, translate it into terms that matter outside your technical context.

Not "we completed the database migration." Instead: "We shipped the migration. Query times dropped 40%, which means the product roadmap feature that was blocked on performance can move now."

Not "we finished the authentication refactor." Instead: "We closed the vulnerability legal flagged. No customer-facing risk from that surface anymore."

Translating completed work is half the practice. The other half happens before the work is done.

While a project is still in motion, your manager is carrying questions they will not always ask out loud: What is going well? What is not going as expected? Where could you use help? What is the next step?

Answer them before they have to ask. That is what keeps your name in the conversation while the work is happening, not just when it ships.

Step 3: Share when done, and what is next.

When something ships, say so clearly. Then name what you are picking up next.

Most technical leaders announce completion. They do not connect it to what comes next. That break in continuity means your manager has to reconstruct your workload from scratch every time they need to speak on your behalf.

Close the loop. Then immediately open the next one. That is how your name stays in the conversation over time.

---

Understand the Environment You Are In

These behaviors work inside any environment. How you apply them depends on the environment you are actually in.

Where is the company growing right now? Where is headcount being held flat or reduced? Which projects are getting real resources this year, and which ones are quietly moving into maintenance mode? Who has actual influence over the decisions that will affect your career in the next 12 months?

This information is not hidden. It is in all-hands meetings, in what leadership is talking about, in which teams are posting roles and which are not. Most technical leaders are not actively gathering it.

What the environment tells you is not how things should work. It is what is actually true right now. The choices that look surprising from the outside almost always come from someone who was paying close attention to what was actually in front of them.

---

Act Accordingly

I was on a centralized platform team. The work was solid. The bonuses were better than average.

I was not happy.

A studio role opened up. The level was lower. The pay was smaller. On every conventional measure, it was the wrong move.

I took it. Because I understood my environment. The platform team was not going to change. My unhappiness was not temporary. Not how it should work. What was actually true.

The person who took my old role is still at the same level, years later. Most of my former coworkers eventually left the company. I cannot tell you it would have turned out the same way for me. But I made that decision based on what the environment was telling me, not on what the career ladder said I was supposed to want next.

That is acting accordingly. Understanding your situation clearly enough to make a choice that reflects what is actually true, even when it does not match the expected path.

If the company is reducing headcount in your area and you are keeping your head down doing good work, acting accordingly means making sure the right people know what you have been building. Good work that nobody sees does not count when decisions get made.

Build the relationships before you need them. Share your work while you are doing it. Be in the environments where opportunities form. That is how the 75% stays active even when the 25% is working against you.

---

Monday Action

Write down three things you have delivered or worked on in the last 30 days that you did not explicitly talk to your manager or skip-level about. Do not assume they know what you are doing.

Pick one. Send a brief update today. Frame it as information, not an accomplishment: "Wanted to let you know what happened with [X]."

Bring it up in your next 1-on-1. Name what you are working on next.

That is showing your work steps 2 and 3 in action. That is the 75% doing its job.
