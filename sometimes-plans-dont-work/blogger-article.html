Quick Bridge: You are probably better at your work than you are at making sure the right people can see it. This post explains why that gap exists, how it keeps technical leaders stuck, and what a consistent Show Your Work practice actually looks like. Three steps. What to do differently starting Monday.

---

Why the Work You Are Doing Is Not Getting You Where You Want to Go

Technical leaders are trained to build things and solve problems. They are not trained to make that work visible to the people who have any say in where their career goes next.

That is not a character flaw. It is a structural gap. It has real career consequences.

The leaders who get stuck are often not the ones with the weakest skills. They are the ones whose work is hardest to see from the outside. Their managers do not know what shipped last quarter. Their skip-level cannot name what they have been working on. The senior leaders who make hiring decisions have never had a reason to know their name.

Good work, consistently invisible, does not move careers. Visible work does. Even work that is sometimes messy.

---

Why This Happens to Technical Leaders Specifically

The instincts that make technical leaders effective in their work are the same ones that make them invisible in the organization.

They wait until something is done before mentioning it. Saying something is "in progress" feels unprofessional. It invites questions you cannot answer yet, and it feels like taking credit before it is earned.

They assume good work will be noticed. If the outcome is real, the recognition will follow. They trust the meritocracy to sort it out.

They stay inside their technical context. Explaining what they built in nontechnical terms feels like dumbing it down, or overselling something they are not sure anyone else will understand.

Each of these instincts is reasonable in isolation. Each of them creates the same problem: a manager who cannot tell you what their best engineer is working on, and a technical leader who cannot understand why their career is not moving.

---

The 25/75 Framework for Career Outcomes

Before getting to the practice, here is a framing that matters.

Careers are roughly 25% luck and 75% you. The luck component is what you cannot control: your projects, your managers, your organizational environment. Your strongest project gets cancelled before launch. Your biggest advocate gets promoted to a different team. A reorg puts three years of solid engineering work under a new VP who has never heard your name.

These things happen to people doing excellent work, all the time. They are not personal. They are the 25%.

You cannot eliminate them. What you can do is operate in a way that puts you in position to take advantage of luck when it shows up, and to hold your ground when it does not. The three behaviors below are how you work the 75%.

---

The Three-Step Show Your Work Practice

Showing your work is not self-promotion. It is translation. It is taking work that is invisible by default and making it legible to the people who need to understand it.

It has a specific sequence.

Step 1: Share your plans.

This is the step most technical leaders skip entirely. Before a project begins, tell the people who matter what you are working toward and how you plan to work through it.

Not a full briefing. A forward-looking statement: "We are starting the authentication refactor this sprint. The goal is to close the security gap legal flagged, and we expect to ship by end of quarter."

Your manager cannot advocate for something they do not know is in motion. Your skip-level cannot route opportunities your way if they do not know what you are building toward. The update before the work starts is often more valuable than the one at the end.

Step 2: Update, update, update.

As the work moves, translate it into terms that matter outside your technical context.

Not "we completed the database migration." Instead: "We shipped the migration. Query times dropped 40%, which means the product roadmap feature that was blocked on performance can move now."

Not "we finished the authentication refactor." Instead: "We closed the vulnerability legal flagged. No customer-facing risk from that surface anymore."

Translating completed milestones is half the practice. The other half happens before the work is done.

While the work is in motion, your manager is carrying questions they will not always ask out loud: What is going well? What is not going as expected? Where could you use help? What is the next step?

Answer those before they have to ask. The manager who shows up in a 1-on-1 asking what you are working on is not micromanaging. They are blind to work that was not shared with them in a context that made sense to them. Proactive updates fix that.

Step 3: Share when done, and what is next.

When something ships, say so clearly. Then name what you are picking up next.

Most technical leaders announce completion. They do not connect it to what comes next. That break in continuity means your manager has to reconstruct your workload from scratch every time they need to speak on your behalf.

Close the loop. Then immediately open the next one. That is how your name stays in the conversation over time.

---

Read the Room Before You Act

These behaviors work inside any environment. How you apply them depends on the environment you are actually in.

Where is the company growing right now? Where is headcount being held flat or reduced? Which projects are getting real resources this year, and which ones are quietly moving into maintenance mode? Who has actual influence over the decisions that will affect your career in the next 12 months?

This information is not hidden. It is in all-hands meetings, in what leadership is talking about, in which teams are posting roles and which are not. Most technical leaders are not actively gathering it.

What the environment tells you is not how things should work. It is what is actually true right now. The choices that look surprising from the outside: the lateral move, the role that seemed like a step back. Those choices almost always come from someone who was paying close attention to what was actually in front of them.

---

What Acting Accordingly Actually Looks Like

I was on a centralized platform team. The work was solid. The bonuses were better than average.

I was not happy.

A studio role opened up. The level was lower. The pay was smaller. On every conventional measure, it was the wrong move.

I took it. Because I understood my environment. The platform team was not going to change. My unhappiness was not temporary. Not how it should work. What was actually true.

The person who took my old role is still at the same level, years later. Most of my former coworkers eventually left the company. I cannot tell you it would have turned out the same way for me. But I made that decision based on what the environment was telling me, not on what the career ladder said I was supposed to want next.

That is acting accordingly. Understanding your situation clearly enough to make a choice that reflects what is actually true, even when it does not match the expected path.

---

Monday Action

Pick one project or deliverable that you are currently working on that you have not specifically talked to your manager or skip-level about.

Send them a brief update today, Step 1 or Step 2 of the sequence above. Frame it as information: "Wanted to give you a heads-up on where we are with [X]."

Make a note to do it again when that project ships. And name what you are picking up next.

That is the 75% working. Start there.
