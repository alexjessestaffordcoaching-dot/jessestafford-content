Why Good Employees Get Labeled Bad Communicators (And What to Do Instead)

Quick Bridge: If you have a strong performer on your team who somehow keeps getting called "difficult to communicate with," this post is for you. Nobody is naturally good at communicating, and the leaders who figure that out first stop wasting their best people on a label they never earned. What follows is the exact shift that turns a "bad communicator" back into your strongest performer, and the specific moves that make it happen.

A leader I worked with had his cursor sitting over the phrase "communication issues" in a performance review draft. He had typed it, deleted it, and typed it again.

The employee behind that phrase was one of his best engineers. Terse in meetings. Slow to respond to Slack. The kind of person other people on the team quietly complained about.

He almost left it in the review. Then he asked himself a question that changed the whole conversation: had he actually changed how he delivered information to this specific person, or had he been sending the exact same message, the exact same way, to everyone on his team?

He had not changed anything. Every person on that team got the same rapid-fire verbal updates, the same drop-in conversations, the same "quick question" energy.

The engineer he was about to flag needed something different. Written context first. Time to think it over. A scheduled conversation instead of an ambush at his desk.

Once the leader started delivering information that way to that one person, the "difficult" label disappeared inside of a month. Nothing about the engineer changed. The leader changed how he delivered the message, and the engineer he had almost written up in a performance review started showing up as one of the sharpest voices in the room.

Why Good Employees Get Labeled Bad Communicators

Here is the part almost nobody says out loud when they hand out that kind of label. Nobody is naturally good at communicating. Not the executive who sounds confident in every meeting. Not the engineer who explains a system cleanly in three sentences.

Every one of them learned to talk to people who already talk like them. Somewhere along the way, that learned habit got mistaken for a natural gift, and the people who never had the chance to learn it that way got labeled as the problem.

None of this is helped by how most people get handed a leadership role in the first place. 82% of managers are promoted without any real leadership training, communication included (Zippia, Leadership Statistics). Judging someone else's communication is easy to do when you were never taught how to communicate on purpose either. Plenty of leaders are running on whatever came naturally to them, and calling anything different a deficiency.

That mislabeling has a real cost. It shows up as a note in a performance review. It shows up in who gets skipped for a stretch assignment because they are "hard to work with." It shows up as a talented person quietly deciding the extra effort of translating themselves for a leader who will not meet them halfway is not worth it anymore.

The Difference Between a Communication Style and a Performance Problem

The mistake underneath all of this is confusing an inability to communicate with an inability to perform. They are not the same problem, and treating them as the same problem punishes the wrong person.

A quiet, terse engineer and a checked-out, disengaged one can look identical from across the room. So leaders default to the version of communication that is easiest to judge: does this person sound the way I expect a strong communicator to sound? That standard was never fair. It only felt objective because it happened to match whoever was doing the judging.

One study puts a number on exactly how wide that gap is. The Niagara Institute surveyed nearly 2,000 professionals across 52 countries on workplace communication. Just over half rated their own communication style as firm, composed, and confident. Roughly the same share of people on the receiving end of those same messages described them as indirect, passive-aggressive, or overly intense (Niagara Institute, Communication Styles in the Workplace Statistics).

Read that again. About half the room believes their message is landing exactly the way they intend it to. The other half is not experiencing it that way at all.

Nobody in that split is lying about their own experience. They are just talking to people who do not process information the same way they do, and most of them have no idea the gap exists.

That gap is not a character flaw. It is what happens by default when nobody has ever taught a leader to check.

How to Stop Confusing Communication Style With Communication Skill

The fix is not a personality quiz. It is a small set of habits any leader can start practicing this week.

Change the channel before you change the person. Before deciding someone communicates poorly, move the message into a different format and see what happens. Take the terse email and turn it into a five-minute call. Take the rambling verbal update and ask for a short written summary instead.

If the message suddenly lands once the channel changes, the person was never the problem. The delivery was.

Replace the label with the behavior. "Communication issues" in a review tells nobody what to do differently. Instead, name the specific moment: what was said or not said, in what setting, and what you actually needed from them at the time.

A vague label protects nobody and fixes nothing. A specific behavior gives a real person something to work with.

Audit your own delivery before you audit theirs. Spend a week just noticing the words you choose when you talk to your team, and which ones land with a negative charge versus a positive one. Then start swapping them out deliberately.

This is not about managing someone else's style. It is about managing yours first, so you are not asking for adaptability you have never practiced yourself.

What DISC and Myers-Briggs Get Wrong About Workplace Communication

Most workplace communication training hands out a personality framework. Take the quiz, get labeled "high D" or "introverted," and move on. These tools are not wrong to exist, but they get used wrong constantly.

The problem is what happens after the label gets assigned. Once someone is "high D" or "an introvert," the friction they cause gets explained once and then nobody does anything else about it. The label becomes a permission slip instead of a starting point.

It tells you why the mismatch exists. It does not tell you what to actually do differently tomorrow.

A style framework is only useful if it changes behavior on both sides of the conversation, not just the side wearing the label. If a personality quiz result has ever been used to end a conversation about communication instead of starting one, that quiz did the opposite of its job.

There is a real objection worth naming here. Some leaders push back that constantly adapting delivery to every person on the team sounds exhausting, even a little fake. It is not the same thing as changing who you are.

You are still saying exactly what you mean. You are just saying it in a way the specific person in front of you can actually receive it.

This Week's Move

Pick one person on your team whose communication style has been quietly getting on your nerves. Before your next 1-on-1, write down exactly what you need from them, then deliver it through their preferred channel instead of yours. Written first if they usually get a rushed verbal update. A short call instead of a long email chain if writing has never been where they process best.

Then watch what happens to the part of them you had already decided was hard to work with.
