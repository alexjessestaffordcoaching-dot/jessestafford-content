Why Your Best People Keep Getting Labeled "Bad Communicators"

Quick Bridge: If you have ever quietly decided someone on your team is "just a bad communicator," this is the post that changes that. Nobody is naturally good at communicating, not the confident ones and not the quiet ones. The fix is not a personality framework or a style quiz. It is learning to deliver information the way the other person receives it, so you stop mistaking a delivery mismatch for a performance problem.

Early in my career, someone pulled me aside and told me I was too blunt. Too direct. Not politically correct enough for the room I was in.

I heard it as a verdict on my ability. It was not. It was a mismatch between how I talked and who was listening.

Now I work at some of the highest levels in my organization, and that same directness is an asset. Nothing about my ability changed between then and now. My audience did, and eventually I learned to read the room instead of just talking at it.

Here is the part nobody tells you when they are handing out that kind of criticism. Nobody is naturally good at this. Not one person.

Not the confident-sounding VP. Not the engineer who somehow explains a system in three sentences instead of thirty.

Both of them learned to talk to people who already talk like them, and then called that learned habit "being a good communicator."

Here is proof it is not just you. In one study, 52% of professionals rated their own communication as firm and confident. 48% of the people receiving those same messages described them as indirect, passive-aggressive, or overly intense.

Half the room thinks they are landing the message. The other half is not experiencing it that way at all. Nobody in that split is lying. They are just talking to people who do not process information the way they do.

The Mistake I See Leaders Make Constantly

I worked with a leader who was about to write off one of his strongest performers as "difficult." The engineer gave one-word answers in meetings, sent terse emails, and never volunteered context unless asked directly. Other people on the team found him hard to work with. The leader was ready to note it in the next review as a performance concern.

I asked him one question first. Had he changed how he delivered information to this specific person, or was he sending the same message the same way to everyone on the team?

He had not changed anything. He talked to this engineer exactly like he talked to the extroverted product manager two desks over, the one who processed information out loud in real time. The engineer needed the opposite. Send the context in writing first, give him time to think, then talk.

Once the leader adjusted his own delivery, the "difficult" engineer started showing up in meetings with sharp, specific input. He had been fine the whole time. The leader had been delivering to an audience of one person while writing a message for a different one.

That is the mistake. Confusing an inability to communicate with an inability to perform. They are not the same problem, and treating them as the same problem punishes the wrong person.

Why This Keeps Happening

You cannot spot this by watching output. A quiet engineer and a checked-out one look identical from across the room. Leaders default to the version of communication that is easiest to measure: does this person sound the way I expect a strong communicator to sound?

That standard was never fair. It only felt objective because the person doing the judging was measuring everyone against himself.

DISC, Myers-Briggs, all the communication-style quizzes people hand out in workshops, they get treated the same way. Somebody gets labeled "high D" or "introverted" and it becomes a permission slip. The label explains the friction once and then nobody does anything else about it.

A style label is not an excuse to stop adjusting. It is a starting point for figuring out how to adjust.

What Actually Helps

Now.

Ask what the medium is doing to the message, not just what the person said. Before you decide someone communicates poorly, change the channel. Move the terse email into a five-minute call. Move the rambling verbal update into a written summary first.

If the message suddenly lands, the person was never the problem.

Separate the label from the review. If you find yourself about to write "communication issues" in someone's performance review, stop and name the specific behavior instead. What did they actually say or not say, in what setting, and what did you need instead? A vague label protects nobody and fixes nothing.

Deliver information the way the other person receives it, not the way you would want to receive it. I did this with my own team once, deliberately. I spent weeks just listening to the words other people chose, noting which ones landed with a negative charge and which did not.

Then I turned that same attention on myself and started swapping out my own language. I was not managing anyone else's style. I was managing mine.

Expect to keep doing this, not to finish it once. Adapting delivery is not the same as changing who you are. Some leaders push back here, worried that flexing to every audience is exhausting, even a little fake.

It is not. Changing your tone for a meeting is not the same as changing your values for one. You are still saying the same thing. You are just saying it in a way the other person can actually hear.

The Monday Move

Pick one person on your team whose communication style has been quietly annoying you. Before your next 1-on-1, write down exactly what you need from them and try delivering it in their preferred channel instead of yours. If they usually get a rambling verbal update from you, send it in writing first. If they usually get a rushed email, ask for five minutes on a call instead.

Then watch what happens to the part of them you had already decided was "hard to work with."
