## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A leader is about to write off one of his strongest engineers as a "bad communicator," when the real problem is that he has been delivering the same message the same way to everyone, and this one person needed the context in writing first, with time to think.
**Context box text:** CHANGE THE CHANNEL BEFORE YOU CHANGE THE PERSON
**Emotional tone:** Direct and focused. This is a decision-and-judgment post, not a warmth-and-connection one. Jesse's expression reads as alert and deliberate, caught mid-realization — not smiling, not stressed. In Concept 2 the same tone plays as steady attentiveness rather than the big "quick question" energy the scene is built to contrast against.

## Concept 1 — The Evidence

**The artifact:** The half-typed performance review, still in the machine, with one key jammed before it reaches the page. The post's story ends with the leader "ready to note it in the next review as a performance concern" — the review draft is the physical trace this situation leaves, and the jammed typebar is the message physically failing to land.
**Setting:** A real workspace at the end of the day — a desk with a mug, a stack of papers, a desk chair pushed back.
**First-second read:** A man at a typewriter has stopped typing because one key is stuck halfway between the keys and the paper. The message is jammed before it arrives — nothing about the machine is broken except the path this one letter is taking.
**Compositional approach:** Jesse close and deliberate, seated at the desk just left of center, the typewriter at hand height with the frozen typebar catching the eye at frame center. His hands are withdrawn from the keys. Environment organized around his position — this is the clarity/decision posture, held at the moment of hesitation.

### Full Expanded Prompt

**COMPOSITION — most important:** Wide shot, 16:9. A real desk at the end of a workday, seen slightly from the side. The primary character sits just left of center, close to the frame, a manual typewriter on the desk directly in front of him at hand height. A sheet of paper is rolled into the platen, half filled with typed lines. One single typebar is frozen mid-strike, caught halfway between the key basket and the paper, its linkage arm visibly taut; every other typebar rests flat in the basket. The character's hands have pulled back from the keys and hover just above them. To the right of the typewriter sits a squared-off stack of identical printed sheets. Generous white negative space fills the upper-left of the frame and the wall plane behind the desk. Do not fill the frame.

**MECHANICAL ENVIRONMENT — second most important:** Objects and their working mechanism: (1) the manual typewriter — exposed fan of typebars, the segment and type-guide they pass through, the platen roller with its visible ratchet and knob, two ribbon spools turning on their spindles, the carriage-return lever standing up on the left; the live mechanism on show is the one typebar stalled halfway to the platen with its connecting link stretched, while the rest sit at rest. (2) A desk drawer standing half-open on visible metal slide rails. (3) A gooseneck desk lamp with a visible spring in its neck, switched off. The charcoal schematic linework sits in the negative space of the upper-left wall plane and the margin above the desk: a blueprint-style exploded annotation of the typebar-and-segment linkage, thin call-out lines pointing down toward the type-guide. It stays on the wall in the negative space — it does not cross the desk or the figure. The S mark is worked into that schematic as the round reference-callout bubble at the end of the linkage annotation, rendered in charcoal linework.

**Art style spec:**
Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision — not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching. Lines should suggest a technical patent drawing or a well-crafted indie comic panel. Loose, organic watercolor fills — visibly hand-painted. Color intentionally bleeds slightly outside the ink lines or leaves small white gaps at boundaries. Wet-edge textures and transparency variations are required — these are features, not errors. No digital gradients. No glow effects. No perfectly aligned fills. Base canvas: clean white paper texture (#ffffff). Single panel.

**Aspect ratio:** 16:9

**Color palette:**
Primary watercolor wash: #100299 (deep blue). Canvas and negative space: #ffffff. Linework and shadows: #333333. Shadows are a semi-transparent charcoal watercolor wash — never solid black fill. The charcoal schematic layer stays unpainted linework. Supporting colors are allowed for depth and warmth but must not compete with the blue/white/charcoal triad. Here: a muted warm grey on the typewriter body and a soft member of the ochre family on the desk surface, both kept well under the blue in visual weight.

**Primary character:**
Man in his early 40s. Short buzz-cut hair. Neatly trimmed short beard and mustache. Brown eyes. V-neck long sleeve shirt in deep blue (#100299) watercolor wash. Charcoal pants. Relaxed, unposed — never stiff or formal. Seated at the desk, weight settled back in the chair, both hands drawn back and hovering just above the keys rather than typing. Head tipped slightly down toward the jammed typebar. Expression alert and focused, caught mid-realization — brow lightly drawn, not smiling, not distressed, simply stopped.

**Scene description:**
A real workspace at day's end. The desk holds the typewriter, the squared stack of identical printed sheets to its right, a plain ceramic mug, and the half-open drawer. The sheet in the platen carries typed lines and, near the last line, the two words the jammed key was in the middle of striking. The stack of identical sheets is blank and unlettered — same page sent to everyone, shown by sameness, not by text. The desk chair, the mug, and the lamp are all unlettered. Behind the desk the wall is mostly open white paper with the charcoal linkage schematic in the upper-left. One clear focal element: the typewriter with its single stuck key. Negative space in the upper-left is intentional and stays open.

**Context box:**
Uppercase sans-serif text inside a solid white (#ffffff) rectangle with a sharp charcoal (#333333) border. Placed at the extreme bottom of the panel. Text: CHANGE THE CHANNEL BEFORE YOU CHANGE THE PERSON

**One in-frame text element (quote verbatim):** the sheet in the platen reads `COMMUNICATION ISSUES` and nothing else legible. Every other surface — the stacked sheets, the mug, the drawer, the lamp — is blank and unlettered.

**Tone guardrails:**
This image should feel like two people talking through a real problem in plain language. Not a motivational poster. Not corporate (no handshakes, whiteboards, or team huddles). Not inspirational abstraction. Grounded, human, and specific. The setting is a real place. The charcoal schematic supports the scene from the negative space — it does not float across the middle of the frame and it does not compete with the scene.

**Setting:** An open-plan office floor, a few desks visible, ordinary and lived-in.
**First-second read:** A manager has stopped by someone's desk to talk, and the person he is talking to is pulling back rather than meeting the energy — while two desks over the same approach is landing fine with someone else.

## Concept 3 — The Reframe

**Unexpected angle:** The absent element, set somewhere unexpected. A pneumatic-tube mail room: Jesse feeds identical sealed capsules into a bank of brass delivery tubes and five of them whisk a capsule away — but the sixth tube is clear and empty, its valve shut. The message for that one person never left the station. It reframes "bad communicator" as "unrouted message": he is communicating constantly, energetically — it just is not reaching one person.
**Setting:** An old-building pneumatic-tube dispatch room — a real place someone could walk into, brass tubes rising from a cast manifold, a belt-driven air pump at the base.
**First-second read:** A man is sending the same capsule down every tube in a bank, and one tube is empty. One person is getting nothing — and it is the channel, not the sender, that is stopped.
**Why it works:** The post's whole argument is that the leader is doing the work; it is landing everywhere except with one person. Five identical capsules in transit and one dead tube makes that legible in a glance, and the nostalgic mechanical setting turns an abstract idea — delivery channels — into something physical and obvious.
