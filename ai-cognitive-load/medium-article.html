Why Does Your Team Feel More Burned Out After Adopting AI?

Quick Bridge: A few months after your team adopted AI tools, the same people are more tired, redoing more work, and quieter going into the week. Each of them told you the rollout made them faster, so the strain is hard to place.

My team picked up AI coding tools this year. It drastically increased what we ship. I am not arguing with the speed numbers.

What I have noticed happening is where the time went. We spend more of it now planning the nuanced details of how a feature should actually work, and less of it writing code. The work did not shrink. It moved.

And it landed on people differently. My senior developers are energized. Their decision-making time turns into working software faster than it used to, so the part of the job they already liked got a bigger payoff. My junior developers are spending more time and more energy than before. They do not have the experience yet to define good software, and the tool does not hand them that.

A lot of engineers online are talking about how much time they now spend reviewing AI output. On my teams the harder problem is a little different. It is keeping quality high when the people submitting the work are not yet skilled at steering AI to a good result.

And still, the same thing kept nagging at me: if the output went up, why did the mood go down?

The easy work was also the rest

Think about what the routine tasks actually were: clearing small tickets, wiring up a simple test, formatting a deck, writing boilerplate you had written a hundred times before. None of it was satisfying, but none of it was draining either. Those were the low-stakes stretches where your brain got to coast for a while between the hard calls.

Take them away and the day does not get shorter. It gets denser. You are making more decisions per hour, hitting more novel problems back to back, and carrying a new job on top of it: checking work you did not write and only half trust. Engineers already have a phrase for AI code, "almost right, but not quite," and almost-right is the most expensive kind to catch.

A 2024 Upwork survey of 2,500 workers found that 77 percent of people using AI at work said it had added to their workload rather than reduced it, with a good chunk of the new time going to reviewing and correcting what the tools produced.

Lisanne Bainbridge saw the shape of this back in 1983, in a paper called "Ironies of Automation." Automate everything you know how to automate, and what you leave the human is the set of tasks nobody could figure out how to automate. Those are the hard ones. Then you hand that same person a monitoring job on top, and human attention is bad at monitoring. Forty years later it describes an AI coding assistant almost exactly.

Software development runs on thought energy, and there are only so many hours a day a person can run at high focus before it costs them. That ceiling did not move when the tools showed up. What sat under it did.

The load also moves up

Here is the second thing, and I am feeling it myself more each week. When a team executes faster, it reaches its decision points and its organizational hurdles faster too. Questions that used to come up once a month now come up once a week: approvals, cross-team alignment, whether we are even allowed to build it the way we want to.

The organization has not caught up to the new pace, so projects sit and wait on a broad decision the team was ready for weeks earlier than anyone planned. I end up spending more of my time helping teams work through the corporate nuance it takes to get a stuck project moving again.

It is an old idea: speed up a step that was never the bottleneck, and the work just stacks up in front of the step that is. Typing code was rarely the bottleneck. Deciding what to build, and getting everyone to agree to ship it, usually was. AI sped up the first one and made the second one impossible to ignore.

The teams feel it as friction. They can suddenly move faster than they are allowed to, and that is its own kind of tired.

What a healthy version looked like, before

Years ago, well before we used AI tools widely, I ran a small study with a team of five. In-office, eight-hour days, two-week sprints. We tracked every hour, focused versus not focused, for months, and ran little experiments along the way.

About 4.5 hours of focused work a day was the ceiling. Any more than that and people started showing burnout signs. They were happiest at around 3.5. The rest of the day went to helping each other, learning something new, meetings, hallway conversations. All of it was work. None of it was head-down.

That was not slack in the system. That was the design working. The focused hours were capped by everything around them. AI clears out a lot of what used to fill those other hours, so the focused hours quietly stretch to fill the space, and nobody actually decided that.

I am not saying keep busywork around so people have something easy to do. That is nostalgia. The routine work is not coming back, and you would not want it back. The job is to build the day around its absence on purpose.

How to design around it

The fixes here are structural, and they belong to the leader. Trying harder inside the same day is not one of them.

What I have started doing in planning is treating the deep-thinking part of the day as its own budget, separate from the workday. I am not arguing for a six-hour day. Most places are not ready to see that, and it is not the point. The point is that the deep, high-judgment work was always a smaller number than eight hours. Three to four, in my old study. Plan around that number, and fill the rest of the day with the review, the pairing, the learning, and the lighter work that used to happen on its own.

The one I would fix first is the wait on decisions. If the team can now build faster than the organization can decide, my job is to close that distance. I look at the sprint ahead and find the decisions and approvals the team is going to run into, and I try to get them lined up and agreed before the code is ready, not after it is blocked. I also watch how many open decisions are sitting on any one person, and treat that like an overloaded ticket count, something to bring down.

The review load people complain about is really a standards problem underneath. When someone who is still learning to drive AI to a good result is shipping work, "I will just check it myself" does not hold up for long. It works better to put the definition of done in writing, put the bar for good in writing, and then coach to it in code review and one-on-ones, instead of catching the same thing ten times.

The last two are about seeing the strain and protecting against it. Almost every individual signal after a rollout points the same way: people say they are faster, and the seniors mean it. Watch the team-level signals instead, the rework rate, the churn, how review time is trending, whether people are quieter going into the week. And put some recovery back on the calendar deliberately, because the small breaks between hard problems are gone. No-meeting blocks, and a lighter load during a heavy decision stretch, are not perks now. They are what keeps the focused hours usable.

What to do Monday

In your next planning session, count the decisions and judgment calls each person is carrying into the week, not just the tasks. Then bring that number down the way you would bring down an overloaded ticket count. If one person is holding six open calls that only they can make, that is the overload, even when their task list looks normal.
