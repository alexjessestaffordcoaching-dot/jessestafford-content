## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A team adopts AI coding tools, every individual reports feeling faster, and a quarter later the same team is more tired and shipping more rework, because the routine low-stakes work that used to be recovery time between hard decisions is gone.
**Context box text:** THE EASY WORK WAS ALSO THE REST
**Emotional tone:** Direct and focused. Jesse is working a real problem, brow slightly furrowed, mouth set, eyes on the thing in front of him. Not worried, not performing. Concept 3 shifts a half-step toward calm-and-grounded as he takes stock of a climb.

## Concept 1 — The Evidence

**The artifact:** A team task wall where the DONE column is packed and overflowing while a middle column has jammed solid behind a single hand-lettered WAITING card. This is the physical trace of the blogger opening: sprint work finished by Wednesday, then the team sat because the decisions and approvals had not caught up.
**Setting:** A team bullpen. Wall-mounted magnetic whiteboard on a swing-arm bracket, sticky notes and magnetic cards, a low credenza beneath it.
**First-second read:** One column of the board is stuffed full and the work has stopped moving; a man stands in front of it holding a marker, looking at the jam.
**Compositional approach:** Jesse center-lower, slightly small against the board, cards and sticky notes clustering toward him from the jammed column as if handed over. Not in command of it. Absorbing it.

### Full Expanded Prompt

**COMPOSITION — most important:** 16:9 single panel. A team bullpen. A wall-mounted magnetic whiteboard fills the upper two-thirds of the frame, tilted very slightly on its swing-arm mount. The board is divided into vertical columns by charcoal marker lines. The left column is densely packed with small blank rectangular cards, several overlapping and peeling off the edge. The center column is jammed solid with blank cards, and at its head sits one slightly larger card, hand-lettered in rough charcoal capitals reading "WAITING". The right column is almost empty. Jesse stands in the lower-center of the frame, rendered a little smaller than the board so the wall dominates, a dry-erase marker in his right hand held loosely at his side, his body angled toward the jammed center column, looking up at it. Three blank sticky notes float just off his left shoulder as if just handed to him. Negative space along the top margin and upper-right corner.

**MECHANICAL ENVIRONMENT — second most important:** The whiteboard hangs on an articulated steel swing-arm with an exposed elbow pivot and visible tension spring where it bolts to the wall stud, lower-left of the board. Along the bottom lip of the board runs a marker tray with a small sliding clip that rides an exposed rail. The credenza beneath has one drawer open on a visible ball-bearing slide. The charcoal schematic linework sits in the upper-right negative space and the top margin: a blueprint-style plan of a kanban flow with clean arrows moving left to right, drawn as unpainted annotation over the wall plane, contrasting the stalled real board. The S mark is cast into the flat steel mounting plate of the swing-arm bracket, lower-left, in charcoal relief.

**Art style spec:** Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision — not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching. Lines should suggest a technical patent drawing or a well-crafted indie comic panel. Loose, organic watercolor fills — visibly hand-painted. Color intentionally bleeds slightly outside the ink lines or leaves small white gaps at boundaries. Wet-edge textures and transparency variations are required — these are features, not errors. No digital gradients. No glow effects. No perfectly aligned fills. Base canvas: clean white paper texture (#ffffff). Single panel.

**Aspect ratio:** 16:9

**Color palette:** Primary watercolor wash: #100299 (deep blue). Canvas and negative space: #ffffff. Linework and shadows: #333333. Shadows are a semi-transparent charcoal watercolor wash — never solid black fill. The charcoal schematic layer stays unpainted linework. Supporting colors are allowed for depth and warmth but must not compete with the blue/white/charcoal triad. Here: a muted warm grey for the whiteboard frame and credenza, a thin ochre wash on the WAITING card.

**Primary character:** Man in his early 40s. Short buzz-cut hair. Neatly trimmed short beard and mustache. Brown eyes. V-neck long sleeve shirt in deep blue (#100299) watercolor wash. Charcoal pants. Relaxed, unposed — never stiff or formal. Direct and focused expression, a slight furrow, taking the measure of the jam.

The board's columns are marked only by charcoal divider lines — no column headers except the single hand-lettered "WAITING" card. Every other card and sticky note is blank. The window blinds behind are half-drawn. Negative space held along the top and upper-right for the schematic. Do not fill the frame.

## Concept 3 — The Reframe

**Unexpected angle:** Environmental inversion. "All peaks and no valleys" made literal as a stairwell whose landings have been removed — one continuous steep flight with nowhere to stop. The rest between efforts is gone; the climb did not get shorter.
**Setting:** An interior office stairwell — steel stringers, painted block wall, a single landing door.
**First-second read:** A staircase that should have flat landings has none; it just keeps going up, and a man partway up has stopped to look back down at where a landing should be.
**Why it works:** It turns an argument about the shape of a workday into a place the viewer can feel in their legs. The missing landings are the missing recovery time.

### Full Expanded Prompt

**COMPOSITION — most important:** 16:9 single panel. An interior concrete-and-steel office stairwell seen from a three-quarter angle. A single unbroken run of stairs climbs from lower-left to upper-right and continues past the top of the frame, with no landings anywhere in the run — the flights that would normally break at a flat landing instead join directly into the next pitch. Jesse stands about a third of the way up, one hand on the steel handrail, his body turned to look back down the run, past the viewer, toward where a landing should be. He is mid-scale, clearly a person on a real staircase, not dwarfed. One closed steel door is set into the wall beside him with no landing in front of it, its threshold opening straight onto a step. Negative space fills the upper-left corner and the wall plane above the rail.

**MECHANICAL ENVIRONMENT — second most important:** The stair is carried on exposed steel stringers with visible bolted connections at each splice, and the handrail is mounted on brackets with exposed fasteners every few feet. The lone door has a surface-mounted hydraulic door closer with a visible arm and elbow linkage at its top. A wall-mounted pipe with a gate valve runs up alongside the stair. The charcoal schematic linework sits in the upper-left negative space and along the wall plane above the handrail: a blueprint stair elevation drawn correctly, with flat landings marked at every half-flight, as unpainted annotation — the "designed" version hanging over the landing-less real one. The S mark is worked into that schematic as the drawing's corner title-block mark, upper-left.

Supporting colors are allowed for depth and warmth but must not compete with the blue/white/charcoal triad.
