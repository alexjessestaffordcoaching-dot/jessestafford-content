How to Know When to Stop Pushing Your Team (And What to Do Instead)

Quick Bridge: If your team feels slow and your instinct is to add more pressure, this post is for you. Constant pushing can be the friction point, not the solution. This piece gives you a clear decision framework to tell when your team actually needs more urgency from you and when the most effective move is to step back, verify direction, and trust the momentum.

---

A manager I was mentoring came to me frustrated. His team wasn't moving fast enough. Deadlines were slipping. He had added more check-ins, more requests, more urgency.

The team was starting to burn out. He was burning out too. And none of it was helping.

He was certain the problem was his team. They weren't doing enough. If he could just get them moving, everything would sort itself out.

I told him: "You do not always have to be rowing the boat, as long as it is still pointed in the right direction."

He got quiet. That was not the answer he expected.

---

The Assumption That Gets Leaders Into Trouble

There is an assumption that gets baked in early: leadership means forward motion. The team should always be moving, and you are what keeps it moving.

When progress feels slow, the instinct is to add pressure. Add a meeting. Add a deadline. Add urgency to the next conversation.

The logic is: if things are slow and I do more, things will go faster. That logic feels correct. It is almost always wrong.

It looks like you care. It looks like you are working hard. Meanwhile, the team burns out and the work slows down.

There is even a name for this leadership style. Goleman called it pacesetting in his research, and it consistently produced the worst long-term team performance of any style he studied.

The problem is not that you are pushing. The problem is that you are pushing regardless of whether more speed is actually needed.

That is a different problem, and it requires a different question.

---

What to Ask Before You Add Pressure

When I caught myself adding pressure without evaluating whether it was necessary, I started using one question to slow down before I added anything.

What is the actual business need right now?

If there is a real need, something specific is at risk. A deadline matters, a gap needs to close, or the business cannot do what it needs to do. The follow-up is: what would we say to the team, or change about their work, to actually address that?

Not more urgency for its own sake. A specific conversation about a specific need.

If there is no urgent need, verify direction and let it run. The work is in motion, the direction is right, and nothing critical is at risk. Make sure the boat is still pointed where it needs to go. Then stop rowing.

This is not the same as coasting. You are not closing your eyes. You are making a deliberate call: the momentum is good, the direction is right, and adding pressure right now creates noise, not speed.

The manager I was mentoring had never asked the first question. He had been adding urgency on instinct, not on evaluation.

When we walked through it together, he realized his team was actually on track. There was no business need for more speed. He had been creating friction because stillness made him uncomfortable.

---

What Checking the Compass Actually Looks Like

Stepping back does not mean stepping away. It means shifting from adding pressure to reading the signals.

Here is what I look for when I evaluate whether to push or let it run:

  • Are people finishing what they started, or cycling through half-done work?
  • Are 1-on-1 conversations about the work itself, or about managing my expectations?
  • Is the team asking for direction, or asking me to slow down the stream of new asks?
  • Are people energized after syncs, or relieved when they end?

Pace is a symptom. These questions get at the cause.

A team moving slowly on paper but finishing things and talking about real work does not need more from you. The other team has a different problem. Pushing harder is not what fixes it.

The distinction matters because the response is completely different in each case.

---

The Annual Evaluation Practice

Once a year, I do a more deliberate version of this check. I review the full weight I have placed on my team.

Not just active projects. The implicit asks too. The things I flagged as "look into this when you get a chance."

The expectations I set without the resources to back them up. The priorities that have not been explicitly removed even though newer ones have landed on top of them.

That list is almost always longer than I expect.

The reason I take this seriously is something I discovered with one of my teams years ago. We tracked focused versus non-focused work hours for months.

Around 4.5 hours of focused work a day was the ceiling before burnout signs appeared. The team was happiest at 3.5 hours. The remaining time went to collaboration, learning, and conversations. All work, just not focused output.

When I find the load is over capacity, I make explicit what is actually highest priority. I have that conversation with the team so they know where to focus.

And I quietly retire the things that do not belong on the list anymore. Not with a big announcement. Just by stopping asking about them.

A team running at full sprint for a quarter and then collapsing delivers less than a team that runs at a consistent pace. The managers I coach who struggle most with this are usually uncomfortable with stillness.

Busyness feels like contribution. Stopping feels like failing. But a team moving well does not need more from you. It needs you to protect the momentum they already have.

---

Monday Action

Before you add anything to your team's plate this week, ask one question: what is the actual business impact if this does not happen in the next two weeks?

If you cannot answer that clearly, hold the ask. Verify direction. Let the momentum carry.

The question will still be there next week. You will be in better shape to bring it once you can answer it.
