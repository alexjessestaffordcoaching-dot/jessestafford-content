Does an AI Notetaker Belong in Your 1-on-1s?

Quick Bridge: Your direct reports are still showing up to their 1-on-1s with you. What is changing is how much of themselves they bring into the room once they know something else is listening too.

Before I was certified as a coach, recording client calls was just part of the training. Standard practice. Every client agreed to it before we started. I recorded them so my supervisor could review my work and tell me what I was missing.

What I noticed had nothing to do with my coaching. Clients gave shorter answers once the recording started. Less emotional depth. The same person, the same question, and somehow a smaller answer than the one I knew was in there.

I changed the setup. Instead of a built-in tool like Zoom's recorder sitting visibly on the call, I switched to a handheld voice recorder I set next to my speakers.

Same recording. Same consent. Less visible apparatus. The answers got longer again.

Nothing about the agreement changed. The client still knew they were being recorded, still knew why, still had said yes. What changed was how present the recording felt in the room. That gap between "you agreed to this" and "you forgot this was happening" is the whole story.

Consent is not the same as honesty

Right now, an AI notetaker is quietly becoming a fifth person in meetings that were built for two. Most of the time, nobody asked permission first. Even where somebody did ask, that is where the real problem starts, not where it ends.

Read AI, working with researcher Rebecca Hinds, analyzed speech patterns across nearly 160,000 meetings and found that the room changes the moment a notetaker joins: individual contributors start talking almost as much as their managers, and quieter voices speak up more. That part is a real gain. But equal airtime is not the same as equal honesty, and nobody has measured what people are choosing not to say once they know the room is on the record.

That should not surprise anyone who has ever watched someone talk differently once a camera turns on. But a 1-on-1 is the one meeting where the entire point is the opposite of careful. It is supposed to be the place where a direct report tells you the thing they would not say in a group. The moment they have to think about who else might read the transcript, and when, and why, that conversation quietly becomes a performance instead of a conversation.

I would not use an AI notetaker in my own 1-on-1s. Not because the tool is bad, but because 1-on-1s need room for the difficult conversation, and most people will hold back the second they suspect there is clear evidence of what they said. It is not malice. It is fear.

Some of your team members will consent to the notetaker and mean it. They already treat the relationship as transactional, and a transcript works fine for them. But plenty of people will consent out of obligation to their job, then quietly adjust what they say in the room to avoid anything that reads as career-limiting later.

You will not be able to tell the two groups apart from the transcript. It will look identical either way, and that is exactly the problem: the tool cannot show you what it is costing you.

There is a version of this that has already gone further than most leaders realize. Some managers are using AI to score the sentiment of a 1-on-1, then referencing that score in a later conversation. I had not heard of that pattern until I went looking for it, and once you have, it explains itself in one sentence: the person across from you now has to manage not just what they say, but how a machine will grade the way they said it.

A notetaker genuinely helps the person who cannot fully listen while also writing everything down, and it frees up attention that used to go to scribbling notes instead of the conversation. That part is real. What nobody is managing is the cost sitting underneath it, and that is the actual problem.

What actually restores the honesty

Getting consent is the legal minimum, not the fix. A signature does not make someone forget there is a second audience. If you are serious about keeping a 1-on-1 honest, the work is behavioral, not procedural.

Name the second audience out loud. If you use any kind of recording or notetaking tool, say plainly who can see the notes, how long they are kept, and what happens to them. Silence about the artifact is what makes people fill in the worst-case version themselves.

Ask yourself what the tool is actually for. A notetaker that only captures action items is a different animal than one that scores tone or flags sentiment, and if you cannot explain in one sentence why it needs to exist in that specific meeting, it probably does not belong there.

Watch for the shorter answer, not just the missing one. The chilling effect rarely shows up as someone refusing to talk. It shows up as an answer that is thirty seconds instead of three minutes, and if someone's 1-on-1s have quietly gotten more clipped since a tool got introduced, that is the signal, not a quiet week. I noticed the same thing from the other side of the recorder: a client did not stop talking, they just stopped saying the parts that mattered.

You do not actually need AI to solve this. I switched from a visible tool to a handheld recorder and got longer, more honest answers back, with the exact same consent in place. A notebook and your own memory still work just as well for the conversations that matter most.

Nothing from the record should ever get quoted back at someone later, out of context, in a performance conversation or otherwise. Separate the record from the relationship, because the moment someone believes a transcript could be used against them down the road, you have recreated the exact fear you were trying to remove.
