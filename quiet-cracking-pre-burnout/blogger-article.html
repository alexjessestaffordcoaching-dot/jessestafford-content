High Performer Burnout: The 1-on-1 Question Engineering Managers Are Not Asking

The exit interview said she had been exhausted for over a year.

You had no idea. As far as you knew, she was your most reliable person. She delivered every project. She never missed a deadline.

She never said anything was wrong.

Three months after the exit interview, you still had not replaced her.

This is not a story about one employee. It is a pattern. Every manager who has lost a high performer unexpectedly has heard some version of it.

The details change. The outcome does not.

The question is not why it happened. The question is why nobody knew it was coming.

---

Engineering Burnout: Why It Hides Longest in Your Best Performers

High performers are the last people managers worry about in a 1-on-1. They deliver. They meet every expectation. When you have something hard, you give it to them because you know it will get done.

That is also what makes them the most at risk.

They absorb more work because they are reliable. Nobody expects them to say no. They are often the first person a team leans on when capacity gets thin, and the last person a manager thinks to check on beyond the work.

A recent CNBC report described quiet cracking this way: unlike quiet quitting, it is not a choice. (Source: CNBC, August 2025) Quiet quitting is a deliberate withdrawal of effort. Quiet cracking is slow collapse under sustained pressure.

Quiet cracking does not look like disengagement from the outside.

It looks like someone doing their job. And doing it well. Right up until they hand in their notice, or stop showing up mentally, or finally say they have been struggling for a long time.

You had every opportunity to catch it. You just did not have the right question.

The Quiet Cracking Signals That Show Up in 1-on-1s First

Performance data cannot detect quiet cracking early. The output is still there. But conversations can detect it, if you know what you are looking for.

Here is what shows up in a 1-on-1 before the numbers change.

The person who used to send you updates before meetings now waits to be asked. The perfectionist starts submitting work they would have rejected six months ago. The one who always drove the conversation in your 1-on-1 starts giving flat answers to everything.

"Fine." "It is okay." "Things are good."

The person who always had a strong opinion on how to solve a problem starts shrugging. "Whatever you think."

None of these signals will show up in a dashboard. You will not find them in a performance report. You will only find them in a conversation. And only if your 1-on-1 format creates space for this kind of answer to surface.

If your 1-on-1s are mostly status updates and project check-ins, they will not surface it. (See: You're having status meetings, not 1-on-1s for why the format matters.)

A 2025 Psychology Today study of over 1,000 workers found that 55% were maintaining expected performance output while experiencing significant internal distress. Those workers were 6.2 times more likely to develop clinical burnout than their peers. (Source: Psychology Today, August 2025)

A separate TalentLMS survey from March 2025 found that 54% of American workers reported experiencing the kind of sustained unhappiness that drives quiet cracking, with 20% experiencing it frequently. (Source: TalentLMS research via Circles, 2025)

The number that stands out is not the 55%. It is the 6.2 times more likely to develop clinical burnout. These workers are not burned out yet.

They are still delivering. But the gap between where they are and where burnout begins is much smaller than their output would suggest.

And the ones with the most to lose by saying so are the ones you trust the most.

The 1-on-1 Question Technology Managers Are Not Asking

Most 1-on-1s with high performers stay close to the work. You talk about projects, blockers, timelines. For most of the year, that is appropriate.

But there is one question that changes the conversation.

"What is one thing you would put down for a few weeks if you could?"

This is not the same as "how are you?" or "how is your workload feeling?" Those questions produce the answer the high performer thinks you want to hear. They have been delivering for you for years. They will tell you what they think you need to hear.

This question is different. It asks for something specific. It asks them to actually identify one real thing they would hand off if they could. And it creates a space that a direct question about stress never does.

Listen for what happens before the answer.

Is the answer flat and fast? "Nothing, I'm good," in a way that does not match the energy you see. Or does it come slowly, like they have been sitting on it for a while and finally have somewhere to put it.

If you get a flat "nothing," try this: "Really? Not even one thing?" Say it lightly. Make space. The second answer is almost always more honest than the first.

You are not diagnosing burnout. You are creating a moment where the honest answer can surface. That is the entire job of the question.

The person who pauses before answering has been carrying something longer than you know. You do not need to fix it in the moment. Sometimes the most useful next step is simply: "That is good to know. Let's look at the project load and come back to this on Thursday." And then actually come back on Thursday.

When a High Performer Finally Tells You Something: What to Do Next

When a high performer actually answers honestly, the moment is easy to mishandle. Some managers make it a bigger deal than it needs to be. Others say something vague and never follow through. Either response closes the door on the next honest conversation.

Here is what actually helps.

The most important thing: do not treat the honest answer like a red flag. If a high performer finally tells you something real and you turn it into an emergency, they will not tell you something true again. Acknowledge what they said: "That is good to know." Keep the energy where it started.

Name one specific next step. "Let's see what we can do" is not a follow-through. "Let me look at next month's load and come back to you on Thursday" is.

High performers track whether you heard them. A specific next step is the evidence that you did.

Make a small adjustment in the next two weeks. One thing changed quickly does more for trust than a large change promised for the next quarter. The adjustment does not have to be dramatic. It just has to be real.

Check in two weeks later. Not to assess their recovery. To close the loop.

Try: "We talked about the project load a couple weeks ago. How is that looking now?" The signal that you came back is what makes the next honest conversation more likely to happen.

The goal is not to solve the root cause in one 1-on-1. The goal is to make the next honest conversation easier to have. That is what retention actually looks like with high performers.

Monday Action

Before your next 1-on-1 with your highest performer, take a minute and make a guess. If they could put one thing down for a few weeks, what would they name?

Write it down. Then ask the question. Compare what you guessed to what they actually say.

The gap between your guess and their answer is exactly where quiet cracking hides.
