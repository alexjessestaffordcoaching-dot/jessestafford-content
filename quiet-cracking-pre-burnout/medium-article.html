How to Catch Burnout in Your Best People Before It Shows Up in Their Work

One year I was given a large team and not enough organizational support to run it.

I burned out fast. For almost two full months at the end of that year, I was not doing a good job.

I attended the meetings that mattered most. I did the required work. I showed up for the travel. But I was not bringing myself to any of it.

My output was still meeting the minimum bar. On paper, nothing looked broken.

Nobody asked me the right question.

Years later, during the pandemic, I built forecasting spreadsheets to model my team's attrition for the next two to three years. What I found confirmed what I had already lived.

The people most likely to leave were my high performers. Not the ones I worried about. Not the ones creating problems. My best people.

The ones most likely to stay were the steady performers who valued stability and were not looking for anything else.

That spreadsheet did not feel like a forecast. It felt like a mirror.

---

The Performer Who Never Makes You Worry

There is a specific person on your team you probably do not think much about during your 1-on-1s.

They deliver. They never miss. When you have something hard, you hand it to them. They will get it done.

You are proud of them. In your 1-on-1, most of the conversation is about the work because things are going well.

What you are not doing is asking how they are actually doing. Because the output says they are fine.

I came across a study last year that confirmed what I had already lived. Over a thousand workers surveyed, and 55% of them were maintaining expected performance output while experiencing significant internal distress.

Not burned out. Not checked out. Still delivering. The same study found those workers were 6.2 times more likely to develop clinical burnout than their peers.

Your high performer is not immune. They are more at risk.

They absorb more work because they are reliable. Nobody expects them to say no. They never have.

They get recruited. They outgrow roles before they say anything. The traits you reward in them are exactly what accelerates the cracking.

This is different from quiet quitting. Quiet quitting is a deliberate choice to pull back effort. Quiet cracking is not a choice.

It is collapse under sustained pressure. And it is harder to spot because they still look fine.

By the time you see it in their output, you are already in the aftermath.

---

The Signals That Show Up Before the Numbers Change

If you run your 1-on-1s like status meetings, you will miss quiet cracking entirely. A 1-on-1 that stays on the work cannot tell you what is happening to the person. (If that distinction feels familiar, I wrote about the format problem here: You're having status meetings, not 1-on-1s.)

Here is what shows up in conversation before performance changes.

The person who used to send you updates before meetings now waits to be asked. The perfectionist starts submitting work they would have rejected themselves. The one who used to drive your 1-on-1 conversations gives you flat answers.

"Fine." "It is okay." "Things are good."

The person who always had a preference starts shrugging. "Whatever you think."

I know what this looks like from the inside. During those two months, my output was technically fine. I attended the right meetings and delivered what was expected.

Nobody would have found me on a performance report.

But someone asking the right question in a 1-on-1 might have found me.

These signals appear in conversation months before they appear in the metrics. Your 1-on-1 is the only place you can catch them early. But you need to ask a different kind of question than the ones you are probably asking right now.

---

Ask This in Your Next 1-on-1

Here is the question.

"What is one thing you would put down for a few weeks if you could?"

Not "Are you okay?" Not "How is your workload feeling?" Those questions get you the answer they think you want to hear.

They have been delivering for you for years. They will tell you what they think you need to hear.

This question is different. It is specific. It asks them to identify one real thing they would actually put down. And it creates an opening that a direct question about stress never does.

Listen for what happens before the answer.

Is the answer flat and fast? Like "nothing, I'm good," said in a way that does not match the energy underneath it. Or does it come slowly, like they have been sitting on it for a while and finally have somewhere to put it.

If you get a flat "nothing," try this: "Really? Not even one thing?" Say it lightly. Make space. The second answer is almost always more honest than the first.

You are not running a survey. You are not diagnosing burnout. You are creating a moment in the conversation where the honest answer can actually surface. That is the job of the question.

The person who pauses before answering, or who names something specific without hesitation, has been carrying that thing longer than you realize. You do not need to fix it in the moment. Sometimes the most useful next step is: "That is good to know. Let's look at whether there is anything we can adjust." And then follow through.

---

Where Your Attention Actually Needs to Go

Your eye goes to underperformance. That is where the visible problems live. You track the person on the performance plan. You spend time on the one who is missing deadlines.

Those are real problems.

But the person most likely to resign on you is the one you were proud of. The one you trusted with everything hard. The one you never thought to worry about.

Learning to read the people around you is not mind reading. It is paying attention to their patterns. Their energy. How they engage with you compared to two months ago.

The data I saw during the pandemic told me who was likely to leave. But it could not tell me who was quietly cracking right now. It could not intervene. Only a conversation could do that.

Your 1-on-1 is where you find out. If you ask the right question.

---

Monday Action

In your next 1-on-1, ask your highest performer: "What is one thing you would put down for a few weeks if you could?"

Not your lowest performer. Not the person you are already worried about. Your best person. The one you never think twice about.

Ask it. Then stop talking. Listen for what is in the silence before the answer.
