Quick Bridge: "Force multiplier" is no longer a compliment your manager pays you in passing. It is now an explicit line on senior and staff engineering leveling rubrics at major tech companies. This post breaks down what that criterion actually asks for, why the work behind it is invisible by design, and the specific narration habits that get it credited before someone else's launch takes the win.

The Technical Visibility Gap Between Multiplier Work and Multiplier Credit

I once spent the better part of a year in meetings that had nothing to do with my own team's roadmap. My job that stretch was to learn how localization worked at my company from beginning to end, then negotiate a completely different process with every internal team and outside vendor that touched it.

I did not write the software that ultimately automated most of the steps. My own team did that part. My job was defining the new process, negotiating it across departments that had never had to agree on anything before, and evaluating what the savings would actually be once it worked.

We reduced localization costs by 90 percent, company-wide.

And if you had looked at my calendar that year, you would have seen almost nothing that looked like output. Meetings. Negotiations. A lot of listening.

What "Technical Leader Promotion" Rubrics Actually Ask For Now

That kind of work has a name in 2026 leveling frameworks, and it is not a soft one anymore. Engineering ladders at Google, Meta, Amazon, and Intuit all name some version of "multiplier" as an explicit, scored criterion, separate from individual execution. Staff and above, the expectation flips from doing the work yourself to increasing what everyone around you can do.

That should have made my localization year easier to defend at review time. It also would have, if I had ever narrated it as it happened instead of letting the 90 percent number speak for itself two years later.

Here is the trap in the new rubric language. Naming "multiplier" as a criterion does not change how the work looks day to day. It still produces no pull request, no launch, no line in a sprint report. The industry gave the invisible work a name before it gave anyone a way to make it visible.

Why Calibration Committees Can't See Multiplier Work

Multiplier work leaves no pull request, no launch, no line in a sprint report. It shows up in someone else's output. When calibration time comes, the reviewer sees the launch and the person who shipped it. The person who cleared the path three weeks earlier has no artifact to point to.

This is not a fairness problem. It is what happens when a rubric names an outcome but never names how to make it visible. The rubric changed. The habit of narrating the work did not catch up.

The Sourced Fact

The research backs up why individual output metrics were always the wrong lens for this kind of contribution. The SPACE framework, published in ACM Queue by Nicole Forsgren and colleagues, explicitly warns that activity metrics like commit counts or ticket closures are a poor standalone proxy for productivity. Activity shows that work happened. It does not show whether the work mattered, and it says nothing about work that shows up in someone else's output instead of your own. Source: Forsgren, Storey, et al., "The SPACE of Developer Productivity," ACM Queue, 2021.

That finding matters here because most calibration conversations still default to individual output as the easiest thing to measure, even when the criterion on the page says "multiplier." If the metric people reach for by habit cannot capture multiplier work, the only fix is narrating it directly, in language a calibration committee can repeat without you in the room. Levels.fyi's standard engineering ladder framework shows this pattern across companies: the further up the ladder, the more the scoring shifts toward influence you cannot point to with a single deliverable.

How to Show Your Work When the Work Isn't Yours to Point At

This is where the Show Your Work method earns its keep, applied to a problem it was not originally built for. The framework has always been about proactive communication before someone has to ask "what are you working on?" Multiplier work needs the same proactive habit, aimed one level further out, because there is no artifact to point a manager toward later.

Say what you removed, not what you did. "I helped the payments team" is not a sentence a manager can repeat in a room. "I removed the schema dependency that was costing the payments team two days a sprint" is. Name the specific obstacle and the specific thing its removal made possible.

Narrate before the credit gets assigned. If you know a launch is coming that your earlier work enabled, say so before the launch happens, not after someone else's announcement has already claimed the moment. "I intend to spend this sprint on the deploy pipeline that has been slowing the mobile team down" puts your name on the record while it is still true.

Log it weekly, not from memory. A one-line note, once a week, of who you helped and what changed for them turns into an entire calibration case by review season. I cannot reconstruct a quarter of unblocking work from memory. Almost nobody can.

Translate the mechanism into what the business sees. My localization project only became a story people remembered once it had a dollar figure attached to it. If your multiplier work has a number behind it, whether that is hours saved, cycle time reduced, or people who no longer need you, say the number early and often, not only when someone finally asks.

Why "Avoid Glue Work" Advice Doesn't Work Anymore

The most cited advice on this exact topic goes the opposite direction. Tanya Reilly's essay "Being Glue" argues that coordination and mentorship work, what she calls glue work, is a trap before you have the title to protect you. Her advice, in short: do less of it until you are senior enough that avoiding it will not tank your career.

That advice made complete sense in a world where multiplier contribution was invisible to the scoring system, not just invisible to the naked eye. In 2026, it is not invisible to the scoring system anymore. Multiplier is a named line on the rubric at the companies most likely to employ the readers of this post.

Avoiding the work does not avoid the scrutiny. It just means you are being scored against a criterion you deliberately did less of.

The real fix was never avoidance. It is narration, done consistently enough that the work stops disappearing the moment someone else ships. LeadDev's coverage of getting credit for invisible work at the staff-plus level makes the same case from a different angle: the engineers who get credit are not doing less of the invisible work, they are the ones who found a way to keep it visible to the people deciding.

One caution worth naming directly. Not everyone who talks in every meeting and leaves an opinion everywhere is a multiplier. Real multiplier work leaves something behind: a doc someone still references, a process that does not need you the second time, a person who can now do what only you could do before.

If a quarter of "helping" left nothing durable behind, narrating it will not fix that. There was nothing there to narrate.

Monday Action

Open your calendar from the last two weeks. Find one meeting, one code review, one conversation where you removed something blocking someone outside your immediate team. Write two sentences: what you removed, and what it made possible. Send those two sentences to your manager today, before whatever it unblocked gets credited somewhere else.

What is the last thing you did that made someone else's work possible, and did anyone besides you know it happened?
