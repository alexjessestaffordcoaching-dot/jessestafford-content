What Does "Force Multiplier" Actually Mean at Promotion Time?

Quick Bridge: If you spent the last quarter making your whole team better and walked into a calibration meeting with nothing to point at, you did not do the wrong work. You did the right work without leaving a record. This is how you fix that.

For two years, I spent hours every week grooming backlogs, meeting with internal customers, and marketing what my six-person team was building. We produced a huge amount of value. We got real visibility across the company.

Almost none of it happened because of what my team did at their desks. It happened because of what I did in every room they weren't in.

Then my role expanded and I got promoted. My schedule filled up with a bigger team and bigger problems, and the work I had been doing for my original team just stopped. Nobody picked it up.

Nobody even knew it needed to happen, because they had never seen it happen. That team fell into mild obscurity for years.

I call that my biggest leadership failure, but not because the work was low value. It was some of the highest-leverage work I have ever done. The failure was that I never showed anyone it was happening.

The work was not the problem. The silence around the work was the problem.

Multiplier Is Now a Line Item, Not a Compliment

"Force multiplier" used to be something a manager said about you in passing. It is now a named box on the leveling rubric. Senior and staff engineering ladders, tech leader promotion criteria, even executive scorecards ask some version of the same question: did this person make the people around them better, not just their own output?

That should be good news. It puts a name on the exact kind of work I did for years without telling anyone. But naming the criterion did not fix the underlying problem.

Multiplier work is invisible by construction. Unblocking a team, mentoring someone through a hard stretch, building the guardrail that keeps three other projects from breaking: none of that produces a deliverable with your name on it. It shows up in someone else's output.

The instinct when you realize this is to stop. Protect your calendar. Ship your own thing so you have something to point at. That advice is not wrong exactly. It is just aimed at the wrong target.

You do not need to do less multiplier work. You need to stop treating it as something that speaks for itself.

How to Narrate Work That Isn't Yours to Point At

Name the mechanism, not just the outcome. "I unblocked the payments team" tells your manager nothing they can act on. "I removed the schema dependency that was costing the payments team two days every sprint, they shipped a week early because of it" gives them a sentence they can repeat in a room you are not in. Say the specific thing you removed and the specific thing it made possible.

Send the note before the credit gets assigned, not after. When you know a deliverable is about to ship because of something you cleared weeks earlier, say so before it lands. "I intend to spend this sprint fixing the deploy pipeline that has been slowing the mobile team down. Expect their release cadence to speed up starting next month." Now the connection exists on record before anyone else's launch announcement erases it.

Keep a running list, not a memory. I still cannot recall the specific projects I unblocked in any given quarter unless I wrote them down at the time. A one-line entry, once a week, of who you helped and what changed for them, turns into your entire calibration case by the time review season starts.

Watch for the fake version of this work. Not everyone who talks in every meeting and leaves opinions everywhere is a multiplier. Real multiplier work leaves something behind: a doc someone still uses, a process that did not need you the second time, a person who can now do what only you could do before. If nothing durable is left behind, narrating it will not fix that, because there is nothing there to narrate.

This is not the "avoid glue work until you have the title to protect you" advice you may have heard before. That advice made sense when nobody was scoring for it. It stopped being complete advice the moment multiplier became an explicit line on the rubric.

You cannot avoid your way to a promotion criterion. You have to be seen clearing it.

Your Monday Action

Pick one thing you did in the last two weeks that made someone else's work possible. Write one sentence naming what you removed and what it enabled. Send it to your manager this week, before whatever it unblocked gets credited to someone else.

What is the last thing you did that made someone else's work possible, and did anyone besides you know it happened?

You're great at the work. Let's make you impossible to ignore.
