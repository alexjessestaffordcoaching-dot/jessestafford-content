You're Creating Arsonists

In twenty years of coaching technology leaders, I have heard hundreds of variations of the same sentence: "I have no idea why everything is always on fire."

I know why. You probably do too.

The fires are not random. They are manufactured. Not always on purpose, and not by people who want to make your life harder. They are manufactured by people who have learned exactly how your organization responds and who are following the path that works.

You are not creating arsonists deliberately. You are creating the conditions where manufacturing urgency is the most effective strategy available.

---

Why Your Team Learned to Manufacture Urgency

In 2001, MIT researchers Nelson Repenning and John Sterman published a paper with one of the most accurate titles in the history of organizational research: "Nobody Ever Gets Credit for Fixing Problems that Never Happened."

Their core finding: organizations systematically underinvest in prevention because firefighting is visible and prevention is not. Managers reward what they can observe.

What they can observe is the rescue. The person who prevented the crisis leaves no trace. The person who saved the launch gets named in standup.

The math is direct. An engineer who flags a dependency risk six weeks before launch and watches it get addressed quietly gets nothing. An engineer who flags it the morning of the deadline and stays up through the night to save the launch gets thanked in front of the team, mentioned in the retrospective, and referenced in the all-hands.

Both engineers solved the same problem. One of them will be remembered.

[Source: Repenning & Sterman, California Management Review, 2001. mitmgmtfaculty.mit.edu/jsterman/nobodyevergetscredit/]

Every time you only respond to fires, you run a training session. The lesson: urgency is the only currency that works here.

Your team is rational. They are paying attention. They are making decisions accordingly.

The Three Patterns You Are Probably Already Seeing

Not everyone on your team adapts to the same environment in the same way. Some stop flagging things early because early flags go nowhere. Some have discovered that requests with a deadline attached move faster. Some have found that escalated requests get same-day responses when direct ones wait for three days.

All three are responding to the same underlying signal. But they express it differently.

The one who stops flagging early sees the problem weeks before it becomes a crisis. They watch it develop and bring it to you when it has become unavoidable.

Not early enough to prevent the disruption. Just in time to solve it under pressure.

This person is not trying to sandbag you. They have just stopped sending early flags because early flags did not change anything. The last time they raised a concern in planning, it sat on a list until it was already on fire. They stopped going that route.

The one who names a deadline attaches urgency to requests that did not have a hard date before. "The client is expecting this by Friday." The Friday requirement may have started as a rough target and became a hard deadline because that is what got traction in your organization.

The next time you receive a deadline you did not know about, ask where it came from. Sometimes it is real. Sometimes it was a soft target that got sharpened because sharpened deadlines move faster.

The one who escalates everything has found that escalated requests get responses and direct messages sometimes wait. They copy the VP not because the VP needs to be involved, but because that is the path that works. This is not aggression. It is adaptation.

All three patterns are rational. All three are a direct response to an environment that only moves when things are burning.

---

What This Is Costing Your Team

Here is what this pattern costs in practice.

DORA's 2024 State of DevOps research found that rework rates in low-performing engineering teams can run as high as 40%, compared to below 5% for elite performers. That means low-performing teams are spending nearly half their delivery effort fixing what went wrong after the fact, instead of preventing it before it shipped.

That is not a skills gap. It is an incentive gap.

[Source: DORA 2024 State of DevOps Report, dora.dev]

Low-performing teams are not staffed by less capable engineers. They are staffed by engineers who have learned that fixing things after they break is what gets noticed, while preventing the break generates no signal at all. The recognition goes to the rescue. The prevention is invisible.

When everything that gets named in a retrospective is a save, and nothing that prevented a save from being necessary ever gets named, you are teaching your team what to optimize for. They are listening.

---

Changing the Leadership ROI on Prevention

You cannot fix this by talking to the person who waited too long. The behavior is a symptom. The system is the cause.

Name prevention out loud. When someone flags a risk early and it gets handled before it becomes a fire, say so. At retro, at standup, in a message to the team. Use the same words you would use to recognize a rescue.

"I want to name that this could have been a crisis last week. It was not because Sarah flagged it in planning." Say it the same way you would thank the person who worked through the night.

Ask when they first noticed. Build this into how you receive problems. When someone brings you something urgent, ask one question before you move into solve mode: when did you first notice this?

The answer tells you something important about the gap between when problems surface and when they get addressed. A genuine surprise looks different from something that has been developing for weeks.

Remove the friction from early flags. If escalation works and a direct message does not, there is a response gap somewhere. A request is sitting unanswered. A decision that should have been made in a one-on-one is still waiting. Find it and close it. The workaround will keep finding you until you do.

---

The Difference Between a Firefighting Culture and a Prevention Culture

A firefighting culture is not built on purpose. It is built by default.

Leaders who are overwhelmed prioritize what is on fire. Overwhelmed leaders reward what they responded to. Their teams learn what the rewards are for. The culture that emerges is one where urgency is manufactured because urgency is what gets a response.

A prevention culture is also built by default. But the default behaviors have to change first.

Recognition has to be explicit. Early flags have to get real responses. "When did you first notice this?" has to become a standard part of how you receive problems, not an occasional coaching move.

The difference is not heroism. It is habit.

One team I worked with committed to naming one prevention in every retrospective. Not a formal ceremony. Just a name said out loud, the same way they named the person who saved the launch.

By the fourth month, early flags were coming in without prompting. The team had learned that prevention was worth doing.

They had not changed. The incentives had.

Monday Action

This week, when someone brings you a problem, ask when they first noticed it.

The time between "I noticed it" and "I told you" is what urgency is costing your team. The longer that stretch, the more expensive your fires will be.

Then find one person who prevented a problem quietly this week. Name it at standup or in a message to the team. Use the same words you would use to recognize a rescue.

You do not have to change everything at once. Change what you recognize first.
