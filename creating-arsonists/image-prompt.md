## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A leader realizes that by only recognizing the last-minute rescue, she has taught her team to let problems grow into crises instead of flagging them early.
**Context box text:** THE RESCUE GETS NAMED. THE PREVENTION NEVER DOES.
**Emotional tone:** Direct and focused — this is systems and accountability content. Jesse's expression is level and attentive, the look of someone running an honest audit of his own team. Not stern, not warm. Working something out.

## Concept 1 — The Evidence

**The artifact:** A team recognition board — a wall-mounted corkboard where the Friday-night deploy save is pinned front and center with fresh marker around it, while the early warning card from three weeks earlier is still pinned in the lower corner, half-covered by newer notices. This is the physical trace the article's story leaves: the flag was on the wall the whole time, it just got papered over. It is specific to this post — the engineer who "noticed something about three weeks ago" and said nothing because "early flags did not go anywhere."
**Setting:** The corner of an open-plan office kitchen/breakout area — the wall where team notices actually get pinned.
**First-second read:** A man lifts a stack of newer papers aside on a team board and uncovers an old warning card that was there all along.
**Compositional approach:** Jesse close and deliberate, standing at the board slightly right of center, one hand lifting the newer notices, the old card revealed under them. The board fills the right two-thirds of the frame; Jesse is at human scale in front of it, not posed beside it.

### Full Expanded Prompt

**COMPOSITION — most important:**
16:9 single panel. The setting is the corner of an open-plan office breakout area: a laminate counter with a coffee machine at the far left edge, a wall-mounted cork-and-magnet notice board filling the right two-thirds of the frame, a window with the blinds half-drawn on the back wall. Jesse stands just right of center, close to the board, at full human scale, seen from a three-quarter back angle so the viewer sees both his face in profile and what his hands are doing. His left hand lifts a bulldog-clipped stack of newer printed notices away from the board surface; his right hand rests near an index card pinned low in the board's bottom-left corner — curled at the edges, older, a hand-drawn warning triangle on it. The center of the board carries a single fresh sheet with a hand-drawn star and a marker box around it, clearly the notice everyone added to. Negative space runs along the top of the frame and the upper-left above the counter — do not fill it. The charcoal schematic sits there.

**MECHANICAL ENVIRONMENT — second most important:**
Objects and their working mechanism: the notice board's spring-steel bulldog clip at top center, jaws open, coil pivot visible; brass push-pins with exposed screw shanks holding the corner cards; a wall clock high on the back wall with its gear train and escapement visible through a clear face (hands present, no numerals). The charcoal schematic linework sits in the negative space along the top margin and the upper-left corner above the coffee counter: an unpainted blueprint annotation of the bulldog clip's coil-spring pivot and a pin-and-cork cross-section, drawn like patent figures over the wall plane. The S mark is cast into the lower-right corner bracket of the notice board's aluminium frame.

**Art style spec:**
Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision — not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching. Lines should suggest a technical patent drawing or a well-crafted indie comic panel. Loose, organic watercolor fills — visibly hand-painted. Color intentionally bleeds slightly outside the ink lines or leaves small white gaps at boundaries. Wet-edge textures and transparency variations are required — these are features, not errors. No digital gradients. No glow effects. No perfectly aligned fills. Base canvas: clean white paper texture (#ffffff). Single panel.

**Aspect ratio:** 16:9

**Color palette:**
Primary watercolor wash: #100299 (deep blue). Canvas and negative space: #ffffff. Linework and shadows: #333333. Shadows are a semi-transparent charcoal watercolor wash — never solid black fill. The charcoal schematic layer stays unpainted linework. Supporting colors are allowed for depth and warmth but must not compete with the blue/white/charcoal triad.

**Primary character:**

Relaxed, unposed — never stiff or formal. Here: standing close to the notice board, three-quarter back angle, left hand lifting a clipped stack of papers aside, right hand near the curled corner card he has just uncovered, head tipped down toward it, expression level and focused — working out how long it has been there.

## Concept 3 — The Reframe

**Unexpected angle:** Ironic juxtaposition plus the absent element. An office lobby trophy cabinet full of identical rescue trophies — little cast figures fighting fires — with one empty trophy stand front and center where the prevention would go. We literally build monuments to firefighting and leave no stand for the fire that never started.
**Setting:** An office building lobby, in front of a glass-fronted display cabinet.
**First-second read:** A trophy case packed with firefighting trophies has one conspicuously empty stand in the middle of it.
**Why it works:** The reader stops because the case makes the argument physical — recognition is a shelf, and there was never a place on it for the thing that didn't happen.

Dead center of the middle shelf is one empty trophy stand: a bare metal peg on a base, with a clean unblemished ring on the shelf around it where dust never settled. Jesse stands close to the glass, left of center, at full human scale, looking directly at the empty stand; his faint reflection shows in the pane. The frame is composed so the empty stand and Jesse's line of sight are the focal axis. Negative space runs along the top of the frame above the cabinet and down the right-hand wall; the charcoal schematic sits there.
