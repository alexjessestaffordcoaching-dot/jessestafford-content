Quick Bridge: Are you tired of every situation being an emergency on your team? The people driving those emergencies are not trying to make your life harder. They have learned that urgency is the only currency that gets a response in your organization.

You're Creating Arsonists

I was talking to a leader I coach a few weeks ago. She mentioned in passing that one of her engineers had stayed up most of Friday night to save a deployment that was in trouble. She had thanked him in Monday standup in front of the whole team. It was the kind of recognition that actually lands.

I asked her one question: when did this start?

She was not sure, so she went back and asked him. He paused for a moment, then said he had noticed something about three weeks ago. A dependency that looked like it might be a problem. He had kept an eye on it, figured it would work itself out.

Three weeks. The late night was not the crisis. The crisis was three weeks earlier, when he saw the risk coming and said nothing.

He is not a bad person. He is not careless. He just stopped flagging things early because early flags did not go anywhere. They got acknowledged, put on a list, and revisited when something more pressing came up.

He did not decide to wait. He drifted toward what worked.

---

You Have Probably Seen This Already

Something like this shows up in almost every team I work with.

Maybe someone brings you a problem that feels urgent, and when you dig in you realize it has been building for a while. Or you get a deadline you did not know existed. The client needs this by Friday, someone says, and you cannot figure out where Friday came from.

Or everything ends up escalated, copied to a VP, flagged as a priority, because a direct message sits in an inbox for three days and an escalation gets a same-day response.

None of these people are doing this on purpose. They are not sitting around thinking about how to manufacture urgency.

They have just figured out what works. When something gets flagged early and ends up on a list, the team learns something. When a three-alarm problem gets the full room and a decision that day, they learn something else.

Nobody has to say it out loud. They are watching what moves decisions and what does not. Over time, they stop doing the things that do not move anything.

---

What the Recognition Gap Is Teaching Your Team

The last three times someone on your team got recognized in front of the group, who was it?

Was it the engineer who flagged a dependency risk six weeks before launch? The person who caught a scope problem in planning before it became a delivery crisis? Or was it the person who worked through the night to save a deployment and got thanked in standup Monday morning?

The rescue is visible. The prevention is invisible. The crisis that did not happen leaves no applause, no story, no mention in the retrospective.

Your team is watching who gets named. They are learning what is worth doing.

If the only recognizable contribution is showing up at the last minute when something is falling apart, the natural drift over time is toward being available for that moment. Not by creating problems on purpose. Just by not going out of their way to surface things early when early has never been worth anything.

Urgency is the only currency that has worked. They figured that out without anyone telling them.

---

This Is Not About Blaming Your Team

You are not doing this deliberately. You respond to crises because crises need responses. You recognize the people who worked hard because they deserve it.

The problem is not that you do those things. The problem is that prevention never gets the same room.

I see this come up often in the technology leaders I work with. Not because they are negligent. Because they are overwhelmed.

When fifteen things are competing for your attention and three of them are already on fire, the other twelve wait. The other twelve are also watching what happens to the three that are burning.

The lesson gets written without anyone saying it out loud.

---

How to Change It

You cannot fix this by talking to the person who waited three weeks. The behavior is not the problem. The environment is.

Name prevention out loud. When someone flags a risk early and it gets handled before it becomes a fire, say so. At standup, at retro, in a message to the team. Use the same words you would use to recognize a rescue.

"I want to name that this could have been a crisis last week. It was not because Marcus flagged it in planning." Say it the same way you thanked the engineer who worked through the night.

Start asking when they first noticed. Build this into how you receive problems. Before you go into solve mode, ask one question: when did you first notice this?

It is not an accusation. It is information. The time between "I noticed it" and "I told you" is what urgency is costing your team.

Make early flags worth sending. If an escalation gets a same-day response and a direct message sits for three days, something is not getting answered. Find it. Close it. The workaround will keep finding you until you do.

---

Your Monday Action

This week, when someone brings you a problem, ask when they first noticed it.

Then look for one person who prevented something quietly. Name it out loud. The same way you would name the rescue.

You are not rewarding arsonists on purpose. But if prevention never gets named, urgency will stay the only currency that works.
