How to Make Sure Your Team Actually Reads Your Updates

A client had built the best status dashboard I had ever seen.

Every project tracked. Dependencies mapped. Color-coded by risk level. Updated every Friday by 9am without fail.

She had even pinned the link in Slack. Her team was still asking the same questions every Monday.

"What is the status on the API migration?"

"Did we decide on the timeline for launch?"

"Is the database work blocked or not?"

The answers were all there. She knew that. She was frustrated. And when she told me her team was not reading the updates, I asked her one question.

"What happens that tells them when they should?"

She did not have an answer. She had a dashboard. Those are not the same thing.

---

The Difference Between Filing and Communicating

There is a distinction that changes everything about how you think about sharing information with your team.

Making information available is filing. Delivering it is communication.

When you update a dashboard, wiki, or status doc, you have created a place where the information lives. That is genuinely useful work. But it is not communication until something causes that information to reach someone at a moment when they can engage with it.

Filing requires people to go looking. Communication requires them to receive.

Think about what your team is doing when they are not in your update. They are heads-down on their own work.

Sprint goals. Their own deadlines. Their own blockers.

Your dashboard is not competing with their laziness. It is competing with their focus.

They are not avoiding your status doc. They are doing their jobs. And "go check the shared folder" is nowhere on that list.

---

The Burden You Did Not Mean to Shift

When you build a system that makes information available and call it communication, the burden of staying informed has quietly transferred to the people who have the least time and the least incentive to go looking.

You spent the time organizing the information. They now have to spend time finding it. Most will not.

Not because they are disengaged. Because they are prioritizing, and finding your update is not on the list.

I have been on the other side of this.

As a senior leader, I have had people tell me to check the status page or read through an email chain to find what I needed. My reaction was always the same. Make me read through a long email history to find your update, and you are wasting my time. Send me the update, and you are not.

Those two things feel similar. They are not.

When you deliver information, the person receives it. When you organize it, they receive an opportunity to find it. Only one of those happens reliably.

That is the shift worth making. Not "how do I build a better dashboard" but "how do I make sure the information actually arrives."

---

The Default When No Signal Comes In

Here is the pattern underneath the pattern.

If someone does not know what is in your status doc, they have no reason to look at it. They are not avoiding it. They do not know what they are missing. That is the whole problem.

I have watched this play out regularly. People default to assuming that if they do not know what you are doing for them, you are probably not doing anything for them. Not out of bad faith. It is just the natural inference when nothing is coming in.

Your weekly update sends no signal. It sits there, current and thorough, waiting for someone to come find it. Nobody does because nothing tells them to.

This is where the system breaks down. And the fix has nothing to do with the tool you built.

---

What a Triggering Event Actually Is

The fix is not a better dashboard. It is not a prettier wiki or a Slack reminder to "go check the status doc."

The fix is a triggering event. A recurring moment when someone has bandwidth, context, and a reason to engage with your information at the same time.

Here is what that looks like in practice:

A short email with one highlight and a link. Not the full dashboard. One sentence: what changed this week and why it matters, with a link to the full update.

The email is the trigger. The dashboard is the depth. People read the sentence and decide whether they need to click through.

A standing agenda item in a meeting that already happens. Thirty seconds in the weekly standup. One thing from the status doc that the whole team should know.

You are not reading the entire update aloud. You are creating the moment that makes the dashboard feel relevant to people who would otherwise never open it.

Put it on their calendar. For longer-horizon updates, create a monthly calendar item with the link already in the body. When it lands on their schedule, that is the signal.

They see it coming, open the doc before the meeting, and arrive with context instead of questions.

None of these require rebuilding the system you already have. The content is there. You are adding the delivery mechanism. That is the only thing that was missing.

---

The Repetition Math

I have told this to managers more times than I can count. People need to hear something seven times before they retain it. But that saying leaves out the most important part. It is seven times per person.

If you lead a team of ten, you are not repeating your message seven times. You are repeating it seventy times.

That sounds exhausting until you realize a triggering event handles most of that work automatically. One short email reaches ten people at once. One agenda item surfaces the update in a meeting you are already running.

You do one thing. It reaches everyone.

You are not doing seventy separate actions. You are creating one recurring delivery mechanism that reaches each person, consistently, on your schedule rather than theirs.

That is the difference between filing and communicating.

---

The Question to Ask This Week

Look at your current update systems. Dashboard, wiki, status page, weekly report.

For each one, ask: what happens to tell someone when they should look at this?

If the answer is "nothing," that is why people are not checking it. The content is fine. The trigger is missing.

---

Monday Action

Pick one update your team should have seen but probably has not.

Do not add anything to the dashboard. Do not reformat it. Add a trigger instead.

Send one short email: what changed, why it matters, and a link to the full details. That is it. Track what questions stop coming the following week.
