How to Re-Engage as a Manager When Nobody Else Will

Quick Bridge: A day off has stopped helping, and the job still does not pull you forward. You can change that without waiting on your company, with three moves to your work, your calendar, and your network, starting this week.

If you are searching for how to re-engage as a manager, start with the number. Global manager engagement fell from 27% to 22% in a single year, according to Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace report. Managers used to be the most engaged people in the building. Now they are only about as engaged as the people they lead, and most of them are still attending every meeting.

If you are inside that number, you already know what it looks like. The work gets done. The calendar is full. Monday arrives and you feel nothing about it, good or bad.

There is a lot of advice about this, and most of it is addressed to companies. Fix the middle-manager experience. Cut the administrative load. That advice is fair, and it is also out of your hands this week. This post is about the part that is in your hands.

How to Re-Engage as a Manager: Start With Work You Enjoy

The first choice costs nothing. You decide what you want to be doing.

Ask which parts of your work you would miss if they disappeared. When I did this, the list was short: analyzing process and mentoring others. Those were also the two things that first pointed me toward management.

Once you have the list, build your week toward it. I did a lot of self-learning, and I structured my teams so I could spend more time on the parts I prefer. I also stopped chasing the next promotion and tried to be happy at work. Everything else followed from that.

Keep it small and daily. Make sure there is something you enjoy in every workday, even if it is one hour. It does not matter how bad the rest of the day got if that hour was good. When there is room for something bigger, take it. I recently spent a couple of hours a day digging into quality reports and building scripts to automate the output, and it gave me something to make real progress on. What I have found is that I feel worst when I cannot see progress, so a project I can move forward every day does more for me than a day off.

Put Your Best Work on the Calendar by Name

A calendar is the only place a priority survives. If the work you chose is not on it, someone else will fill the space.

Think about the shape of a typical week: meetings stacked from nine to five, and the work you care about squeezed into the evening. The fix has two parts. Name the block, then clear room for it.

Name every block with the exact work you plan to do. Mine reads "Work on the data report for quality." It does not read "Focus time." I also block time every day for one-on-ones and for thinking, and I name those too. The one-on-ones keep me connected to the people I lead. The thinking time keeps me from walking into every meeting already behind, because it is where I decide what I want out of the week before the week starts deciding for me. A named block is a decision you already made. A calendar is a list of decisions. Leave a block open and someone else makes that one.

Clear room every week. On Monday morning, open your calendar before your inbox. Decline or remove any meeting that does not line up with your priorities for the week. Pick one priority for each day and keep what you value.

When someone asks for a slot you already blocked, you do not owe them a long explanation. Say, "I have something else requiring my time then," and offer a different time. It stays friendly, and the decision stays with you. It will not work for every meeting, and some you cannot decline. The block's name makes the rest easier, because you know what the time is for.

Why Managers Need Mentors Outside Their Chain

Your calendar is only as good as what you know to put on it. That part you cannot see alone.

My mentors have come from inside and outside the company, some formal and some just a good working relationship. What they gave me was a wider view: the business needs behind decisions, a different way to frame a problem, and, most of all, a look at how things happen in rooms I was not in.

That last one matters when you feel stuck. If you cannot see why a decision was made, it feels like it was done to you. Someone who has been in the room can tell you what was said.

Choose someone above your level who is not in your chain of command. You can say things to them that you cannot say to your team or your boss. A peer manager at another company works too, as long as they have been in rooms you have not. For more on owning your own load, see It's Not Your Manager's Stress, It's Yours. For what unspoken expectations do to the people carrying them, see The Hidden Cost of Workplace Assumptions.

What if the Problem Is the Company?

Sometimes it is, and that is worth saying plainly.

Team size, mandates, and reorganizations are real drains. Reporting on the 2026 Gallup data puts global employee engagement at its lowest level since 2020 and names falling manager engagement as the strongest driver of that decrease. When engagement falls across a whole group, the cause is rarely one person's attitude. A named calendar block will not shrink a span of control or undo a mandate.

Sort it by asking who owns the drain. If the drain is about how your time is spent, who you talk to, or which part of the job you are leaning into, those are choices you can make now. If the drain is about headcount, pay, or direction, that is a conversation to have with the people who own those decisions, and a mentor outside your chain is often the best person to help you plan it.

The line that helped me: it is all choice, and that includes choosing which fights are yours. Other people can influence your priorities. You still own how you show up and how you spend your time. You cannot do this alone, and that is not because someone is stopping you. Work does not happen alone, and careers are built on teams too.

Monday Action: Three Small Moves

Do these before you open your inbox.

1. Write down the one part of your job you would miss most. Block an hour for it this week and name the block with the exact work. 2. Look at the next five days and decline or remove one meeting that does not fit. 3. Message one person outside your chain: "I would value thirty minutes of your view on how decisions get made above my level. Could we find a time?"

You do not need a sabbatical for any of it, and nobody has to give you permission.

If you could take one meeting off your calendar this week to make room for work you like, which meeting would it be?
