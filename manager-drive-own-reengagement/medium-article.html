How to Re-Engage Yourself as a Manager When Nobody Else Is Going To

Quick Bridge: You are putting in the hours and feeling nothing, and you have been waiting for someone else to change that. You can start getting your interest back this week with three choices that are yours to make.

I got to a point where I really was not enjoying work. I was putting in about three or four hours a day and doing the bare minimum to get my tasks done.

From the outside, nothing looked wrong. My tasks got done, and a full calendar looks a lot like engagement. From the inside, I was just running the paces, and there was nothing in the day I was looking forward to.

If that sounds familiar, you have probably been waiting for something to change. A new boss, or a company that finally fixes the middle-manager experience. Either would help. Neither is in your hands this week.

What keeps most of us waiting is a sentence. We put too much pressure on ourselves and let other people add more, because managers are supposed to be available, supposed to attend, supposed to absorb it all. Finish that sentence and ask who decided it. Nobody did. Waiting feels safe, because if you never choose, you never have to own the result. It is a choice, and getting out of that stretch took three of them.

It started with the work that gives me energy, moved to my calendar, and ended with the people outside my chain who could show me what I could not see from my own desk. Each one comes with a small move for Monday.

Start With the Work That Gives You Energy

The first choice costs nothing. You decide what you want to be doing.

I sat down and asked which parts of my work I actually enjoyed. For me it was analyzing process and mentoring others. Those were the same two things that had pointed me toward management in the first place.

Once I named them, I rebuilt around them. I did a lot of self-learning, and I structured my teams so I could spend more time on the parts I prefer. I stopped chasing the next promotion and tried to be happy at work. The rest followed.

Now I make sure there is something I enjoy in every workday, usually mentoring or coaching someone. It does not matter how bad the rest of the day got if at least an hour of it was good.

Sometimes it is a bigger project. Not long ago I took on the quality metrics and reporting for my teams. I spent a couple of hours a day in reports and databases, building scripts to automate the output, and I came out with data we could tell a clear story with. I used it as reset time, because it gave me something to make progress on every day.

Knowing the work you want is the easy part. Getting it to survive a week of meetings is the real problem, because work you do not protect gets traded away one meeting at a time.

Put It on Your Calendar by Name

Work you enjoy only counts if it has a place to live, and the calendar is the only place that holds. There are two parts: name the block, then clear room for it.

You know the week I mean. Meetings stacked from nine to five, and the work you care about squeezed into whatever is left of the evening. By Wednesday you are counting what is left of the week.

I block time every day for three kinds of things: one-on-ones, time to think, and any project I want to work on directly. What matters is the name. I name every block with what I plan to do, and I never leave it open. Mine reads "Work on the data report for quality." It does not read "Focus time."

A named block is a decision you already made. An open block gets filled by the first request that shows up.

The second part is a weekly clear-out. Every Monday morning I open my calendar and decline or remove any meeting that does not line up with my priorities for the week. I pick one priority for each day, and I keep the things I value on the schedule.

When someone asks for a slot I have already blocked, I say, "I have something else requiring my time then." Then I offer a different time. That keeps it friendly, and it keeps the decision with me.

It will not work for every meeting. Some you cannot decline. But the name on the block makes the rest easier, because you know exactly what the time is for.

Your calendar is only as good as what you know to put on it. That part you cannot see alone.

Get People Outside Your Chain

I have had many mentors over the years, inside and outside the company, formal and informal. They changed four things for me:

  • The bigger picture
  • The business needs behind decisions
  • Different ways to think about a problem
  • How things happen in rooms I was not in

That last one matters most when you feel stuck. When you cannot see why a decision was made, it feels like it was done to you. Someone who has been in the room can tell you what was said, and the decision stops feeling like weather.

Pick someone above your level who is not in your chain of command. You can say things to them that you cannot say to your team or your boss.

You cannot do this alone. That is not because someone is stopping you. Work does not happen alone, and careers are built on teams too. How you choose to show up decides which options you have.

Other people can influence your priorities, and some of what drains you does come from the system, like team size, mandates, or reorganizations. A calendar block will not fix those. But you still own how you spend your time and who you spend it with.

Monday Try This

Three small moves, one for each choice. Do them before you open your inbox.

1. Write down the one part of your job you would miss if it disappeared. Block an hour for it this week, and name the block with the exact work. 2. Look at the next five days and decline or remove one meeting that does not fit. Say, "I have something else requiring my time then." 3. Message one person outside your chain: "I would value thirty minutes of your view on how decisions get made above my level. Could we find a time?"

That is the whole list. You do not need a sabbatical, and nobody has to give you permission.
