## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A manager looks at a week where every slot has been filled by someone else's requests and sees that one slot, with a name on it, is the only one that is theirs.
**Context box text:** A NAMED BLOCK IS A DECISION YOU ALREADY MADE
**Emotional tone:** direct-and-focused. Jesse's expression is level and deliberate, the look of someone who has just made a decision.

## Concept 1 — The Evidence

**The artifact:** The week planner itself, almost every tile identical and blank, with exactly one tile carrying handwritten work. A calendar is what this situation leaves behind.
**Setting:** A manager's office with a wall-mounted week planner opposite the desk.
**First-second read:** A full week of identical empty tiles, with one that has a name.
**Compositional approach:** Planner fills the right two-thirds. Jesse stands lower-left, close to the board and deliberate, a short arm's length from it.

### Full Expanded Prompt

**COMPOSITION — most important**
Wide shot, eye-level. The week planner fills the right two-thirds of the frame. Jesse stands in the lower-left third, medium-small in the frame, a short arm's length from the board, looking at it. A desk with a closed laptop sits at the far left edge. The viewer reads a packed week first, then the one tile that is different.

**MECHANICAL ENVIRONMENT — second most important**
A wall-mounted week planner: five vertical day columns on a pale board, each column a pair of steel rails holding sliding rectangular tiles. Each tile has a visible spring clip, a small roller on its back edge, and a pivot pin; tiles slide up and down the rails. Tiles are unlettered unless stated. Nearly every tile in all five columns is the same blank pale-blue tile, stacked end to end with no gaps. One tile in the third column is a deeper blue and carries one line of handwritten marker text, exactly: WORK ON THE DATA REPORT FOR QUALITY. That tile's spring clip is visibly seated and its roller sits in the rail groove. Unpainted charcoal schematic linework in the upper-left wall plane above the planner: a gear train and a cam-and-follower drawn like blueprint annotation, and a rail-profile cross-section near the top-right corner. The S mark is worked into the center of the cam.

**Art style spec**
Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision — not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching. Lines should suggest a technical patent drawing or a well-crafted indie comic panel. Loose, organic watercolor fills — visibly hand-painted. Color intentionally bleeds slightly outside the ink lines or leaves small white gaps at boundaries. Wet-edge textures and transparency variations are required — these are features, not errors. No digital gradients. No glow effects. No perfectly aligned fills. Base canvas: clean white paper texture (#ffffff). Single panel.

**Aspect ratio:** 16:9

**Color palette**
Primary watercolor wash: #100299 (deep blue). Canvas and negative space: #ffffff. Linework and shadows: #333333. Shadows are a semi-transparent charcoal watercolor wash — never solid black fill. The charcoal schematic layer stays unpainted linework. Supporting colors are allowed for depth and warmth but must not compete with the blue/white/charcoal triad.

**Primary character**
Man in his early 40s. Short buzz-cut hair. Neatly trimmed short beard and mustache. Brown eyes. V-neck long sleeve shirt in deep blue (#100299) watercolor wash. Charcoal pants. Relaxed, unposed — never stiff or formal. Standing, weight even, arms relaxed at his sides, expression level and deliberate. He is looking at the one named tile, not at the viewer.

**Scene description**
A manager's office with a window along the left wall, a plain desk with a closed laptop, and the week planner on the wall. The only lettering in the frame is the single tile text quoted above. Every other tile, the laptop lid, and the window are blank and unlettered. Negative space is intentional: the left wall plane and the area above the planner stay open for the schematic.

**Context box**
Uppercase sans-serif text inside a solid white (#ffffff) rectangle with a sharp charcoal (#333333) border. Placed at the extreme top or bottom of the panel. Text: A NAMED BLOCK IS A DECISION YOU ALREADY MADE

**Tone guardrails**
This image should feel like two people talking through a real problem in plain language. Not a motivational poster. Not corporate (no handshakes, whiteboards, or team huddles). Not inspirational abstraction. Grounded, human, and specific. The setting is a real place. The charcoal schematic supports the scene from the negative space — it does not float across the middle of the frame and it does not compete with the scene.

## Carousel

**Shared setting:** Jesse's office with the wall-mounted week planner opposite the desk.
**Shared mechanism family:** Sliding tiles on steel rails with spring clips, rollers, and pivot pins; gears and cams in the charcoal schematic.

**Deck read-through:**
1. You are running the paces. | The work gets done and the calendar is full. Nothing in the week pulls you forward.
2. Managers are supposed to... | Be available. Attend everything. Absorb it all. Nobody decided that.
3. I was doing the bare minimum. | At one point I put in three or four hours a day. From the outside, nothing looked wrong. Then I asked what I enjoyed.
4. It is all choice. | Others can influence your priorities. You still own how you show up and how you spend your time.
5. 1. Choose the work. 2. Name the block. | Pick the part of the job you would miss. Block an hour for it and write the exact work on the tile.
6. 3. Find someone outside your chain. | Decline one meeting that does not fit. Then ask someone above you, outside your chain, for thirty minutes.
7. Finish the sentence yourself. | What is your "managers are supposed to..." sentence, and what would change if you treated it as a choice?

**Cold-read check:** (a) yes: by slide 2 the problem is clear, a full calendar with no pull and an automatic script about what managers owe. (b) yes: slide 3 gives the story in one line (minimum effort, nothing looked wrong, then a question) and slide 4 states the turn that it is a choice. (c) yes: slide 5 and 6 give three specific moves (block an hour named with the exact work, decline one meeting, ask one person outside the chain for thirty minutes). (d) yes: each slide follows the one before it and none depends on the picture to make sense.
