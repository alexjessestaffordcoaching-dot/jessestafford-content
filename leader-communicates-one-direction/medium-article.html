Your Team Isn't Just the People Who Report to You

Quick Bridge: You have gotten good at asking your manager for what you need, and good at handing work down to your team. The direction nobody trained you for is sideways, to the team that doesn't know they have reason to care what you are working on. That is where the ask either bounces up your chain and disappears, or never gets made at all.

---

"Can you tell me how a manager from the studio side is going to help make compliance work better?"

That was the first thing the compliance leader said when we sat down, before hello, before anything else.

A senior leader at my company had asked me to go work with our compliance group and help them communicate with the studios more effectively. I did what seemed reasonable. I found out which leader on that team I should talk to, scheduled a meeting, sent an outline of what I wanted to cover, and showed up on time. Her first sentence told me I had skipped the part that actually matters.

I paused and backed all the way up. I introduced myself properly, told her what my role actually was and what I had been asked to help with, and explained why I thought my experience might be useful here. Then I stopped talking about me and started asking about her work. How it moved through her team, where it jammed up when another department was involved, what the whole thing felt like from her seat.

By the end of that hour we were designing a fix together. But I nearly lost the room in the first thirty seconds, because I had walked in treating a cross-team project like a calendar invite instead of a relationship I had not built yet.

You Learned to Talk Up and Down. Nobody Taught You Sideways.

Think about where you have actually practiced communication as a leader. Almost all of it runs vertically. You put real effort into 1-on-1s and feedback with your team, and just as much into keeping your own manager informed and ready for your skip-level. Up the chart, or down it.

Now think about the last time a project depended on a team that did not report to you and did not report to anyone you report to. Different department, different goals, different vocabulary, and no particular reason to move for you. You have probably got one of those asks open right now, the one you sent up your chain a few weeks ago that is still sitting somewhere between your manager and theirs. It is the one direction that never shows up on your calendar, so it is the one you never practice.

In a study of more than 7,600 managers, Donald Sull and his co-authors found that only 9 percent said they could count on colleagues in other functions all the time. Around 80 percent said the same about their own boss and their own direct reports.

We practice the two directions that show up in the calendar, and we stay weak in the one that does not. The leaders who get things done at a senior level are not smarter than everyone else. They just work the sideways connections that most people leave alone.

The Org Chart Is How HR Thinks About Your Company. It Isn't How Work Gets Done.

Here is the belief underneath it. Somewhere along the way most people absorbed the idea that the org chart is a set of rules. If you need something from another team, you go to your boss, your boss goes to their boss, and the request travels up one side, across the top, and back down the other while everyone stays in their box. That is playing telephone through your manager, and the message loses something at every hop.

The org chart is how HR reasons about the company. It tells you who runs performance reviews for whom. It does not tell you who actually needs to talk to solve the problem in front of you. People trap themselves inside those lines like they are walls, when all they really are is reporting relationships that were never built to carry the actual work.

Let me be clear about the obvious objection, because this is not going over anyone's head. I am not telling you to skip your manager or spring a surprise on them. I am telling you not to route a peer-level conversation through three people who will each lose a piece of it on the way.

None of this is the comfortable option. Reaching out to a peer you have never met, about a project they did not ask for, feels worse in the moment than handing the problem to your manager and letting the chain carry it. That discomfort is most of the reason people default to the chain in the first place. It is still the wrong default, and the rest of this post is how I work around it.

How to Open the Conversation

Start at your own level, then work up or down from there. If you are a first-level manager, talk to the first-level manager closest to the project you want to collaborate on. If you are a director, talk to the director closest to it. You are not escalating first and you are not starting the project in silence. You are getting the person nearest the work to help you shape how the two teams should fit together.

Before you say a word about what you need, understand what they think their job is. You cannot show up and tell someone what they do, because they know more about it than you do. Ask how work moves through their team, where it jams when another department is involved, and what they are measured on. Then frame your project in their language and in terms of how they already see their work. Show up with your own assumptions instead and you start the whole thing on the wrong foot.

There is also a tone to get right. You have no authority to give this team an order, so if the ask lands like one, that is what they will push back on. The same facts, offered the way a colleague shares what they know, get you a partner instead of an argument.

Show your work in every direction, not just up. Tell your boss the plan, and do not ask permission for it. Here is how that sounds. "I am going to go work with compliance to make this email situation easier to manage. I will start by talking to the manager who sends the email out. What is your take?" It is not a permission slip and it is not starting without their input. It gives your manager a real chance to tell you something you are missing before you have spent the effort, and sharing your plans that way is a lot of what makes someone look like a leader.

What Happened With Compliance

Once I understood how issues actually reached that team, the picture was obvious. When a regulator or a test lab flagged something, compliance had no map of who owned what inside the studios. Their process had been built back when the company was small enough that everyone knew everyone.

What was left of that process was a single email to more than a hundred people, asking someone to claim the issue. By the time I saw it, that email got ignored by default, because surely someone else would pick it up.

We replaced it. Issues went into a tracking tool and got routed to the specific person who needed to act. That change handed back about eight hours a day of one person's time that had been going to email triage, and it led to the first stretch in company history with no complaints from regulatory agencies.

None of that happened because I had authority over compliance, because I had none. It happened because I spent the first hour learning what they were accountable for and talking about the work the way they saw it.

If you want the case for why these sideways relationships decide your career, I made it separately in "Your Peers Control Your Success." This post is the part that comes after you already believe it, the part about how you actually open and run the work.

What to Do Monday

Pick one project on your plate right now that depends on a team you do not control. Find the one person on that team, at your own level, who sits closest to the work. Not their boss, not your boss, the person actually doing it.

Before you reach out to them, tell your manager the plan. Not as a permission request. As a heads-up. "Here is who I am talking to and why. Anything you would add before I go?" You are not asking for approval, you are keeping them in the picture before you move, not after.

Then send a short note to the peer asking for thirty minutes to understand how work moves through their team, because you have something coming that will touch them and you would rather learn their side before you plan around it. Do not bring your ask yet. Learn first.
