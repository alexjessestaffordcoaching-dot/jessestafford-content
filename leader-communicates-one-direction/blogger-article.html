How to Work Across an Org Chart You Don't Control

Quick Bridge: You can get a yes from your manager and you can get movement from your team. The place you get stuck is the group sitting beside you on the org chart, the one that owes you nothing. That is where your projects quietly stall.

---

You are three weeks from a deadline and the one piece you do not control has not moved.

You sent the request to your manager. Your manager mentioned it to their manager. Someone said they would raise it with the other group. Nobody told you no. Nothing happened either. Now you are in the worst spot a leader can be in. You are accountable for the result, and you have no direct line to the people who actually decide whether it gets done.

This is the pattern I see most often when a strong leader keeps missing dates that are not really about their own team. It is not a planning problem. It is a communication problem, and it runs in a direction we almost never practice.

Why Technology Leader Communication Breaks Down Sideways

Think about where you have actually practiced communicating as a leader. You run 1-on-1s with your team. You give feedback. You keep your own manager informed. You prepare for your skip-level. Every one of those is vertical, up the chart or down it, and you practice them every single week.

Now think about the last time a project depended on a team that did not report to you and did not report to anyone you report to. Different department, different goals, different vocabulary, and no particular reason to move for you. That direction never shows up on your calendar, so you almost never practice it. Then a deadline lands, the dependency is sideways, and you are working a muscle you have not trained.

The cost is not just a missed date. Leaders stuck in this spot start compensating in ways that make things worse. They duplicate work their own team can control. They let commitments to other groups slide because chasing them feels like begging. They escalate, which buys one result and burns trust for the next three asks.

Cross-Functional Coordination Keeps Breaking Down, and It Is Getting Worse

This is not a personal failing. It is a well-documented pattern.

The sourced fact: In a study of more than 7,600 managers across 262 companies, Donald Sull, Rebecca Homkes, and Charles Sull found that only 9 percent of managers said they could rely on colleagues in other functions and units all the time. About half said they could rely on those colleagues most of the time. Compare that to the roughly 80 percent who said they could count on their own boss and their own direct reports. The reliable relationships run vertically. The unreliable ones run sideways. (Source: Why Strategy Execution Unravels, Harvard Business Review, March 2015.)

The pressure has only increased since then. As organizations flatten and run more of their work through cross-department projects, the number of people you need and cannot command keeps growing. DDI's Global Leadership Forecast work names influence without formal authority as a core development need for mid-level leaders, and separate research on collaborative overload shows that when lateral coordination has no system behind it, 20 to 35 percent of the useful cross-team work ends up concentrated in 3 to 5 percent of people. Those people either become the bottleneck or burn out.

The Org Chart Is How HR Thinks About Your Company. It Is Not How Work Gets Done.

Here is the belief underneath the whole problem. Somewhere along the way most people absorbed the idea that the org chart is a set of rules. If you need something from another team, you go to your boss. Your boss goes to their boss. The request travels up one side, across the top, and back down the other, and everyone stays in their box.

That is playing telephone through your manager, and the message loses something at every hop.

The org chart tells you who runs performance reviews for whom. It does not tell you who actually needs to talk to solve the problem in front of you. People treat those lines like walls when all they really are is reporting relationships, and reporting relationships were never built to carry the actual work.

To be clear, this is not about going over anyone's head. It is not skipping your manager or springing a surprise on them. It is refusing to route a peer-level conversation through three people who will each lose a piece of it on the way.

How to Open a Cross-Team Conversation Without Authority

Start at your own level, then work up or down from there. This is the line I give my managers. If you are a first-level manager, talk to the first-level manager closest to the project. If you are a director, talk to the director closest to it. You are not escalating first and you are not starting in silence. You are getting the person nearest the work to help you shape how the two teams should fit together.

Understand what they think their job is before you say a word about what you need. You cannot show up and tell someone what they do, because they know more about it than you do. Ask how work moves through their team. Ask where it jams when another department is involved. Ask what they are measured on. Then frame your project in their language and in terms of how they already see their work. Show up with your own assumptions instead and you start on the wrong foot.

Deliver the ask as information, not as a directive. You have no authority to give this team an order, so if the ask lands like one, that is what they will push back on. The same facts, offered the way a colleague shares what they know, get you a partner instead of an argument.

Show your work in every direction, not just up. Tell your boss the plan, and do not ask permission for it. It sounds like this. "I am going to work with compliance to make this email situation easier to manage. I will start with the manager who sends the email out. What is your take?" That is not a permission slip and it is not starting without input. It gives your manager a real chance to catch something you are missing, and sharing plans that way is a lot of what makes a person look like a leader.

How a Compliance Team Killed Its 100-Person Email

A senior leader at my company once asked me to work with our compliance group and help them communicate better with the studios. I found the right leader on that team, scheduled a meeting, and sent an agenda. Her first sentence when we sat down was, "Can you tell me how a manager from the studio side is going to help make compliance work better?"

That told me I had walked in before doing the part that matters. So I backed up. I introduced myself, explained what I had been asked to help with, and then spent most of the hour asking about her world. How issues reached her team. Where the process jammed. What it felt like from her seat.

Here was the situation. When a regulator or a test lab flagged something, compliance had no map of who owned what inside the studios. Their process had been built when the company was small enough that everyone knew everyone. What was left of it was a single email to more than a hundred people asking someone to claim the issue. By the time I saw it, that email was ignored by default, because surely someone else would take it. Compliance was getting complaints about response times and had no way to fix it except escalating to senior leadership until someone forced a team to respond.

We designed the replacement together and ran it through the whole lifecycle. Issues moved into a tracking tool and got routed to the specific person who needed to act. That change handed back about eight hours a day of one person's time that had been going to email triage. It also led to the first stretch in company history with no complaints from regulatory agencies.

None of that happened because I had authority over compliance. I had none. It happened because I spent the first hour learning what they were accountable for and talking about the work the way they saw it.

Signs You Are Stuck Communicating in One Direction

You do not need a diagnosis for this. A few honest questions will tell you where you stand.

  • You can name your skip-level's top three priorities, but not your closest peer's.
  • Every request to another team goes out through your manager, not from you.
  • You only talk to leaders in other departments when you need something from them.
  • You find out about other teams' changes after they ship, not before.
  • When a cross-team dependency slips, your first move is to escalate, because you do not have another one.

If three or more of those land, the skill to build is not assertiveness. It is the level-to-level habit above.

Your Monday Move

Look at everything on your plate and find the one dependency most likely to slip. Not the biggest project, the shakiest handoff.

Find the person who owns that piece on the other team, at your own level. Not their manager. Not yours.

Before you reach out to them, tell your manager the plan. Not a permission request. A heads-up. "Here is who I am talking to and why. Anything you would add before I go?"

Then send a short note to the peer asking for thirty minutes to understand how work moves through their team, because something is coming that will touch them and you would rather learn their side before you plan around it. Do not bring the ask yet.
