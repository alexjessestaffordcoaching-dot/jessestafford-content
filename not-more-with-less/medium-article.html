I'm Not Asking for More with Less. I'm Asking You to Work Differently.

Quick Bridge: When your team shrinks, people do not need another speech about resilience. They need to know what work is actually coming off the table.

I stood in front of my team about three weeks after we had lost three people. Two had been reorg'd out and one had given notice, and the backfill request never got approved. I had been thinking about what to say for days, because I already knew what they were going to hear the second I started talking, regardless of the actual words I chose.

Someone finally broke the silence. "So are we just doing their work now too?"

I said no, and watched no one believe me. I understood why. I had heard leaders say "no" in that moment before, and I had watched it turn into yes, not because they meant to mislead anyone, but because they had not yet decided what work would change.

I had seen it happen on other teams. The "no" becomes a delayed yes that shows up about six months later.

What I actually meant was something more specific than that. Not the same process running on fewer people working harder to carry it, but a different process, because the one we had was designed for a headcount that no longer existed.

That sounds like a technicality. It is not. When a leader does not name what actually changes after a headcount loss, the team names it themselves, and they are rarely more generous in that interpretation than you would want them to be. The silence always gets filled with something, and it is almost never the thing you expected.

What the Team Is Already Calculating

What I have come to understand about the weeks after a team loses people is that everyone still at the table is already running the math before you say anything. Not out loud, but constantly in the background. If five people used to carry this work and there are now two of us, what does next Monday actually look like?

They are not being dramatic. They are being accurate. The tickets keep coming in. The reviews still need to happen.

The same processes that were designed for a larger team do not scale down cleanly, and the longer nobody says what changes, the more the silence gets filled with whatever assumption the remaining people have been waiting to hear confirmed: same deliverables, fewer hands, called something else.

In a 2025 TalentLMS survey of 1,000 U.S. employees, 54% said they were experiencing some degree of what the company calls quiet cracking, a persistent erosion of satisfaction that may not show up in performance right away. That language may be new, but the feeling is not.

The math that keeps not adding up in a teammate's head, the one where the work does not fit the available time no matter how they model it, is often where that strain starts. The question is whether you name what changes before they stop waiting for you to.

What an AI Tool Does Not Fix

I hear leaders reach for this one regularly, and I understand why: hand the smaller team new software and treat the gap as handled. The math looks like it should work. The tools are genuinely faster in some places.

But what consistently happens is that whatever efficiency the tool creates becomes the new normal within a month or two. The work does not get lighter. The expectations absorb the gain and reset upward. I have watched this happen enough times to be careful with the promise: a tool saves time, then the saved time quietly becomes an argument for raising the output target.

The headcount gap does not get smaller. The pace expectation does.

"Work differently" is not code for "work the same way but faster because the tools improved." It only means something real if you can point at a specific part of the old process and say: this piece, we are not doing it that way anymore. That is the conversation most leaders skip.

The Conversation That Changes It

What I told my team that day was less a speech and more permission to stop doing one specific thing the way it had always been done.

The most useful move after a headcount loss is to name one piece of the old process that no longer fits, out loud, in language specific enough to act on. Not "we need to find efficiencies." Something a person could write down and do differently by Monday. "We are no longer reviewing every ticket manually. We are sampling twenty percent and trusting the automated checks for the rest." Or: "The status meeting that used to run ninety minutes is going to be thirty, because we cannot staff it the way we used to."

There is a reason specificity matters here more than you might expect. A general direction to be more efficient asks people to figure out on their own which part of their job to let go of, and people almost never drop the right thing when left to decide alone. They drop the thing that feels least defensible, not the thing that creates the most drag. You have to name the thing.

The second part of this, the part that makes the ask feel honest rather than hollow, is cutting something real from the list. If you introduce a new method but keep every deliverable unchanged, you have not changed the process. You have added a step, and the team will notice quickly. When they do, they will conclude, correctly, that "work differently" was just the more sophisticated framing of what they were already expecting.

If you are not sure what to cut, ask them. They usually have an answer before you do. They have been quietly working around the part that stopped making sense weeks ago, waiting to see whether you would notice.

Monday

Before your next team meeting, identify the one process step that no longer makes sense at this headcount. Not a general goal. The actual thing that changes.

Say it in the meeting. Tell them what they can stop doing, not just what you want them to start doing faster.

That is how you begin rebuilding trust after a headcount loss: not by asking people to believe your reassurance, but by showing them what will actually be different on Monday.

What is on your team's plate right now that made sense at the old headcount and does not make sense at this one?
