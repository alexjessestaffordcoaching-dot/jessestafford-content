Should You Stay in Management When the Pressure Never Lets Up?

Quick Bridge: The pressure in the middle never fully lifts, and at some point the load has been heavy long enough that you start wondering whether staying in management makes sense. You can sort what you can affect from what you can only influence and make that decision with a clear head.

The message came down: we are returning to the office.

Not everyone on my team was glad about it, and I had my own view on the timing. That left me with a job I did not ask for: stand in front of my team and explain the decision as the right way forward.

It was not my call, and my team was looking at me to make sense of it. Standing there, I could feel how much easier it would have been to say what I actually thought.

If you have been in that spot, you have probably had the next thought too. I should just get out of management. In the DDI Global Leadership Forecast 2025, 40% of stressed leaders said they had considered leaving leadership to protect their well-being.

Before you decide, sort what is yours to affect and what you can only influence. Then work out where your frustration goes and who hears it. After that, check what your days need to look like for you to stay.

Sort the Load Before You Decide

The question I use is short. What choices can I make to affect the outcome?

Run it on the mandate and you end up with two lists. I cannot change the mandate, so that goes on the list of things I can only influence, and I do try to influence it. How I explain it to my team is a choice I can make, so that goes on the list of things I can affect.

If I can only influence something, I temper what I expect from it. I still push. I just do not let the outcome set my stress level.

The same approach works on a structural load. At one point I had 11+ direct reports and was responsible in some way for 20+ projects. It was not possible to stay on top of all of it. What I could affect was which ball to drop, so I dropped the one with the least impact and kept my team first.

I did not have this approach in the year I was handed a large team with no organizational support to run it day to day. I ran out of road quickly, and by the end of that year I was not working much for almost two months, going only to the most important meetings and doing the travel the work needed. I needed that time to recover. The two months away gave me a clearer view: the load had been structurally unsustainable from the start, not a sign that I was in the wrong role. I came back to this question to catch it earlier.

The two lists do not remove the load. They tell you where to spend yourself, because what you can affect is where your effort pays off, and I am still learning to stop spending effort on the other list.

Some of what is on that list is not even real yet. It is your assumption about what your boss or your team expects, not something they have actually told you. Find out before you carry it.

Vent Up, Explain Down

That mandate sits on the influence list. As managers, we can vent to peers and up the org chart. Never down.

I told my team why I support the return to office, what it means to me and how we work, and how it supports our goals. I kept my opinion on the timing out of it.

Holding back was annoying. If you do the same, it can feel like pretending, and it is still the job. My team heard the real reasons. They did not hear my frustration, because that would only have handed them something else to carry.

To my boss, I say what I am carrying, and I do it all the time. I tell them where the pressure is sitting, what the team's reaction has been, and where I am having to hold the line. They get it because they have been there. They helped me see the business need and what would get us both to the next step.

Check Your Days Once a Year

I have seriously thought about stepping back from leading, and I would want to do it in a completely different career capacity. I do not have a tidy answer. The thought has not gone away.

What keeps me in is the joy of watching other people get their career success and improve, which is the part I would miss most.

At the start of every year I take some time to recenter on one question: what do my days need to look like for me to be successful at this job and to enjoy it? Both have to be there in some capacity. Neither will be 100%, but you need both.

The check cannot always wait for January. Some weeks are just lots of little dings and challenges, none of them big, but enough that I start to wonder if I really want to go to the next meeting. When that happens I run the check again, and I make sure there is at least one hour in the day I enjoy, usually mentoring or coaching someone.

Your days are a list you can write. It fits on the same page as the two lists below.

Monday Try This

Take one page and write two lists.

Under what you can affect, put things like how you explain a decision to your team, which project you drop, and whether you tell your boss what you are carrying.

Under what you can only influence, put things like the decision itself, headcount, and the timing of a rollout.

Pick one item from the influence list and say this to your boss: "Here is what I see as the priority on this, and here is what I think you are expecting. Where am I off?" Their answer shows you where you can shift.

At the bottom of the page, write one sentence about what your days need to look like for you to succeed at this job and still enjoy it.

What is one thing you are carrying right now that you can only influence?
