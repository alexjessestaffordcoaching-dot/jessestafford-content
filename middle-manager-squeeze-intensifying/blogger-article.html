How to Handle Pressure as a Middle Manager (Without Burning Out)

Quick Bridge: Three tools middle managers lean on when the pressure builds and the stay-or-go question arrives: a way to sort what is yours to handle and what is not, a rule for where frustration belongs, and one question to ask yourself once a year.

Most managers do not plan to question the role. They take it, grow into it, and then somewhere in year three or four the thought arrives: maybe I should step back from this.

The moment usually has a trigger. For me, it was a mandate that came down through the org: full return to the office, no exceptions. I had my own view on the timing. I found myself in a position I had not signed up for: standing in front of my team, explaining a decision I did not make as the right way forward.

I have talked to a lot of managers who have been in a version of that spot. The mandate changes. The decision is never yours. Your team is looking at you to make sense of it. And somewhere in the background the question is forming: is this the job I want?

In the DDI Global Leadership Forecast 2025, 40% of stressed leaders said they had considered leaving leadership to protect their well-being. The number tracks. The question is not whether the pressure will arrive. It is what to do when it does.

Here is what I have found.

Tool 1: Sort the Load Into What You Can Affect and What You Can Only Influence

The question I use is simple: What choices can I make to affect the outcome?

Run it on any source of pressure and you end up with two lists.

I cannot change the return-to-office mandate. That goes on the influence list, and I still try to influence it. How I explain it to my team is a choice I can make. That goes on the affect list.

If I can only influence something, I temper what I expect from it. I still push. I just do not let the outcome set my stress level.

The same approach works on a structural load. At one point I had eleven or more direct reports and was responsible in some way for twenty or more projects. There was no way to stay on top of all of it. What I could affect was which ball to drop. I dropped the one with the least impact and kept my team first.

Before I thought this way, I ran out of road. In one role I was handed a large team with no organizational support to run it day to day. By the end of that year I was doing only the most important meetings and the required travel and not much else. I needed the time to recover. When I came back, I had one clear realization: the load had been structurally unsustainable from the start, not a sign I was in the wrong job.

This approach does not make the load lighter. It tells you where to spend yourself, because what you can affect is the only place your effort actually pays off.

One more thing: some of what is on the influence list is not even real yet. It is your assumption about what your boss or your team expects, not something they have actually told you. Find out before you carry it.

Tool 2: Vent Up. Explain Down.

Middle managers can vent to peers and up the chain. Never down.

When I stood in front of my team, I told them why I support the return to the office, what it means for how we work, and how it fits our goals. I kept my opinion on the timing out of it.

Holding back was annoying. If you have been there, you know it can feel like pretending. But there is a difference between what your team needs to hear and what you are carrying. When you vent down, you hand your team something they cannot act on. That is on you, not the decision.

My team heard the real reasons. They did not hear my frustration.

To my boss, I say everything. I tell them where the pressure is sitting, what the team's reaction has been, and where I am having to hold the line. They get it because they have been there. They helped me see the business need and what would get us both to the next step.

The vent goes up. The explanation goes down.

Tool 3: What Do Your Days Need to Look Like?

Here is the question that keeps me in the job.

What do my days need to look like for me to be successful at this job and to enjoy it?

Both have to be present in some capacity. Neither will be 100%, but you need both.

I ask myself this at the start of every year and take it seriously. I have seriously thought about stepping back from leading. I would do it in a completely different career capacity if I did. I do not have a tidy answer. The thought has not gone away.

What keeps me in is watching other people reach their career goals and improve. That is what I would miss.

This question cannot always wait for January. Some weeks are just a series of small dings, none of them big, but enough that I start to wonder if I really want to go to the next meeting. When that happens, I go through it early and make sure there is at least one hour in the day I enjoy, usually mentoring or coaching someone.

When the check keeps coming back the same way for months and neither condition can be met — not even in some capacity — that is information worth taking seriously.

Monday Action:

Take one page. Draw a line down the middle.

Left side: what you can affect. How you explain a decision to your team. Which project you drop. Whether you tell your boss what you are carrying.

Right side: what you can only influence. The decision itself. Headcount. The timing of a rollout.

Pick one item from the right side and say this to your boss: "Here is what I see as the priority on this, and here is what I think you are expecting. Where am I off?"

At the bottom of the page, write one sentence about what your days need to look like for you to succeed and still enjoy the work.

That is the whole framework on one page.

What do your days need to look like this week for this job to still make sense?
