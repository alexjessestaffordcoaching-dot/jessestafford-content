## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A leader changes a team's goals, and the pushback that follows ("this isn't fair," "there's nothing we can do") is not resistance — it's grief over losing the ability to tell, on their own, whether they're doing their job well.

**Context box text:** THAT'S NOT RESISTANCE. THAT'S GRIEF.

**Emotional tone:** Calm and grounded — this is a high-pressure, easy-to-mishandle moment (a leader sitting with someone else's loss instead of defending a decision). Jesse's expression stays steady, attentive, unhurried. Not warm-and-smiling, not clinical.

## Concept 1 — The Evidence

**The artifact:** The private spreadsheet. The post names it directly — a team member still tracking the old individual metric (defects-per-day) by hand, on their own, weeks after the company stopped measuring it. It is the physical trace of someone quietly refusing to let go of the way they used to know they were good at their job.

**Setting:** An open-office QA desk, late in the day. The team member's monitor is on; a new team-level metrics dashboard is printed and taped to the cubicle wall behind them.

**First-second read:** Someone is still keeping their own private scoreboard, and their leader has noticed without saying anything about it yet.

**Compositional approach:** The desk and monitor sit slightly right of center at human scale — this is the team member's moment, not Jesse's. Jesse is seated on a rolling stool just inside the cubicle threshold, off to the left, present but not looming over the screen.

Jesse sits on a low rolling stool just inside the cubicle opening, lower-left third of the frame, smaller in scale than the desk setup, leaning slightly forward with forearms on his knees, watching without reaching for the screen. **Setting:** A quiet office break corner — two chairs angled toward each other near a window, not a formal meeting room.

**First-second read:** This is a listening conversation, not a performance review — the chairs, the body language, and the absence of a table between them read as "he's just here to hear this."

**Compositional approach:** The two chairs are turned at an angle to each other rather than directly opposite, breaking the confrontational face-off read. Jesse and the team member are close to equal scale — peer-height dynamic, not Jesse standing over a seated employee.

## Concept 3 — The Reframe

**Unexpected angle:** The absent element. Instead of showing the new team dashboard being put up, show the empty, unfaded rectangle on the wall where an individual achievement certificate used to hang — the literal shape of what was taken away, still visible, right next to what replaced it.

**Setting:** A real hallway or break-room wall lined with a few framed items — the kind of wall every office has.

**First-second read:** A blank, brighter rectangle on an otherwise sun-faded wall reads instantly as "something used to be here" — paired with a new plaque nearby, it reads as replacement, not just absence.

**Why it works:** It makes the loss physically visible without showing anyone grieving — the wall itself carries the emotional weight, and Jesse's stillness in front of it does the rest.
