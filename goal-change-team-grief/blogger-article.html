What Technology Leaders Get Wrong When Their Team Resists Change

"This is not fair."

If you have changed a team's goals, restructured how success is measured, reorganized a team, changed a process, or taken over a team with a different direction, you have heard this. Or some version of it.

"There is nothing we can do to make these goals work."

"We cannot control the outcome."

Every one of those complaints sounds like feedback on your decision. You defend the decision. You explain the rationale again. The complaint comes back the following week.

You start reading it as stubbornness. A culture problem. A performance issue waiting to happen.

Here is what it actually is: grief.

And the leaders who do not know that are solving the wrong problem every time they step into a 1-on-1.

---

Why Teams Push Back on Change, and What They Actually Mean

To understand the complaint, you first have to understand what goals actually do for people.

Goals are not just performance targets. They are how professionals answer the question "Am I doing my job well?" Every person on your team has a way of self-assessing. A metric they track privately, a standard they hold themselves to, a signal that tells them whether today was a good day at work or a bad one.

When you change the goal, you do not just change the finish line. You change their answer to that question. When the new goal depends on factors outside their individual control, the signal gets cut off. They can no longer tell, on their own, whether they are winning.

That is not a minor adjustment. That is a loss. And "this is not fair" is the language people reach for when they are in grief, not when they are making a careful logical argument about goal design.

Every time I have taken over a team or changed the high-level goals they were working toward, I have seen this pattern. Most recently, I took a QA team whose individual metrics covered things entirely in their control. Defects they wrote, QA duration when a game was handed to them on schedule. I shifted them to team-level metrics: customer-reported defects, whether the entire game team delivered on time.

The complaints were immediate and consistent: "It is not fair. There is nothing we can do." The framing was about fairness and control. The thing underneath was loss.

The Grief Framework: What Change Actually Triggers

Change management literature has used the Kubler-Ross grief model for decades. Denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. HR frameworks cite it. Leadership development decks use it as a visual.

What most of that content gets wrong is the specificity.

The existing frameworks apply grief to organizational change in the abstract: the company is restructuring, the strategy is pivoting, roles are changing. The more useful and more precise observation is this: the grief is triggered by losing the ability to self-measure. Not change in general. The specific thing that tells a competent person whether they are still competent.

That specificity matters because it tells you what to address in the 1-on-1. You are not managing grief about change in the abstract. You are managing grief about a lost feedback system.

According to Gartner data reported by Apollo Technical, only 38% of employees were willing to support organizational change in 2022, down from 74% just six years earlier. That 36-point drop happened during a period of accelerating change, not decreasing change. Organizations got faster at changing goals while getting worse at helping people through what those changes actually cost. That is where leaders lose people. (Source: Apollo Technical, Change Management Statistics)

A 2025 peer-reviewed study in Leadership and Organization Development Journal validates that grief, not just resistance, is the correct lens for understanding employee responses to organizational transitions, particularly when identity and competence are tied to existing processes. (Source: Fondrevay / Wiley, Understanding the Employee Grief Cycle in Organizational Transitions)

The Prosci change management framework estimates that projects with excellent change management practices are six times more likely to succeed. The difference between excellent and average is almost always in how leaders handle the individual emotional response, not how the change gets communicated from the top. (Source: Prosci, Applying the Kubler-Ross Change Curve to Change Management)

What to Do Differently as a Technology Leader

The instinct when you hear "this is not fair" is to defend the decision. You explain the business rationale. You show them why the new metrics are better. You demonstrate that the goal is reasonable.

None of this works. Not because the explanation is wrong, but because you are solving the wrong problem.

What they need first is to feel heard. Not fixed. There is a difference between those two things, and it usually takes a few rounds of the same conversation to find it.

These three questions work well in 1-on-1s after a significant change:

"What was your favorite part about how we used to measure your work?" "What does doing a good job look like to you under the new goals?" "Where do you feel like you have the most control right now?"

These questions do two things. They name the loss without you having to diagnose it directly. And they start building a new sense of what success looks like from where the person actually is, not where you think they should be.

The third question is particularly useful. When someone says "there is nothing we can do," they are telling you their sphere of control has been taken away. That is the specific thing to rebuild over time. Not all at once, not in one conversation.

"Where do you feel like you have the most control right now?" starts that process. Your job then is to help them expand that territory, not to argue that they have more control than they think.

The Stage-by-Stage Diagnostic for Leaders

Here is what you are likely to see in the weeks after a significant change, and what each stage needs from you.

Denial. You will see it as continued focus on the old metrics. Someone tracking defects-per-day in a private spreadsheet even though the company now measures customer-reported quality. Or a quiet assumption that the new goals will change back after the quarter ends.

What they need: acknowledgment that the change is real, and that their previous way of working had real value. Not another explanation of why the change was necessary.

Anger. You will hear it as unfairness. "My performance rating depends on things I cannot control." "Other teams are not being held to the same standard." This is not a conversation about compensation equity.

This is grief that sounds like anger. What they need: to feel heard, not corrected. Ask why it feels unfair. Listen without defending. The anger passes faster when it is acknowledged than when it is argued with.

Bargaining. You will hear it as negotiation. "What if we tracked both the old and new metrics for the first quarter so I can still show individual contribution?" Or: "Can the goals be weighted differently for the first six months?" Bargaining is actually a sign of progress.

It means they are engaging with the new reality rather than denying it. What they need: honest conversation about what is and is not possible, without dismissing the underlying need that the proposal represents.

Acceptance. You will not always hear this one. You will see it. The team member starts talking about the new goals as theirs rather than yours. They bring ideas for how to influence the outcomes they cannot directly control.

They build relationships with the cross-functional teams whose work affects their metrics. They stop tracking the old numbers.

When you see that last one, you have done the right thing. The path to get there was not faster or better explanations. It was space to grieve, and someone who knew how to sit in that room without trying to fix it.

Monday Action

In your next 1-on-1 with someone who has been pushing back after a change, do not defend the decision. Instead, ask: "What was your favorite part about how we used to measure your work?"

Write down what they say. Then ask yourself what they just told you they lost. That is the actual conversation you need to have.
