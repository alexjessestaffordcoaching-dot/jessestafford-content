What You're Calling Resistance Is Actually Grief

---

Quick Bridge: When your team pushes back after a change (a goal change, a team restructure, a new process) you are not dealing with resistance. You are dealing with grief. This post talks about what they lost, why their complaints sound like stubbornness, and what changes when you reframe around grief.

---

When I incorporated a QA team into my reporting structure, I changed their goals on the first day.

They had been measured on individual metrics. How many defects they wrote. Whether the QA duration matched the schedule they controlled. They had built their entire sense of "I am doing my job well" around those numbers.

They were good at hitting them. They knew when they were winning.

I changed the goals to team-level metrics. How many defects customers reported. Whether the entire game team delivered on time. That shift meant they had to change how they worked with the rest of the team, not just manage their own lane.

The complaints started almost immediately. "This is not fair." "There is nothing we can do to make these goals work." "We cannot control what the rest of the team does."

My first instinct was to explain the rationale again. I had already explained it. The same pushback came back the next week, and the week after.

I kept reading it as resistance. What I was watching was grief.

The goals were the vehicle in that case. But I have seen the same response after management changes, team restructures, and process overhauls. The specific change matters less than what the change takes away.

---

What Goals Actually Are

Goals are not just targets. They are how people measure whether they are good at their jobs.

That sounds obvious. But think about what it actually means. When you change a goal, you do not just move the finish line. You rewrite the answer to the question most people ask themselves, often without saying it out loud: am I doing my job well?

The QA team was not complaining about workload. They were not resisting accountability. They were grieving something specific: the ability to self-assess. The old metrics let them see, clearly and on their own, whether they were performing.

The new metrics depended on factors they saw as outside of their individual control. That cut off the signal that tells a competent person they are still doing their job well.

That is the specific loss worth naming. Not "change is hard." The concrete thing they need to find a new answer for is their way of measuring themselves.

It is easy to miss. Not because you do not care, but because the surface complaint sounds like an argument about goal design. "This is not fair" sounds logical. It sounds like feedback on the decision you made.

It is, but it is also a grief response. Those are different problems and require different responses. You need to decide which you are going to respond to.

---

What Grief Looks Like After a Change

The stages of grief are not a straight line. You will see them all after any significant (to them) change, not in a predictable order, and not at the same time for every person on the team.

What shows up most often in the early weeks is the bargaining version. "If you just kept the individual metrics alongside the team goals, we could still show our contribution." Or the anger version: "It is not fair that my rating depends on what the server team does." Or the denial version: a sudden focus on the old metrics, tracking defects written in a personal spreadsheet even though that is no longer what the company measures.

None of this is stubbornness. All of it is people in the middle of a loss they did not choose.

The leader who responds with explanation or defense is responding to the wrong problem. Defending the goal to someone in grief does not get them through it any faster. It just leaves them feeling like no one is acknowledging what they lost.

I have seen this every time I have taken over a team or changed the high-level goals they were working toward. The framing is almost always about fairness or control. The thing underneath is always loss. And the "it's not fair" complaint will keep coming back until someone names what they are actually going through.

---

What Changes in the 1-on-1

The answer here is not complicated. It just requires sitting with discomfort a little longer than you might want to.

Do not solve it in the first conversation.

What they need first is to feel heard, not fixed. If you try to fix before they feel heard, you get more resistance, not less. The instinct to move straight to solutions is usually about reducing your own discomfort, not theirs. [Hey Boss, Stop Solving the Team's Problems!] is worth reading if that pattern sounds familiar.

The questions that work are not questions designed to defend the decision. They are questions designed to name what happened.

"Tell me more about why this feels unfair" opens a door. "Let me explain the rationale again" shuts it.

Listen specifically for the control piece. When someone says "there is nothing we can do," they are telling you the thread between their effort and their outcome has been cut. That is the specific thing to rebuild over time, not in one conversation.

The difference between control and influence matters here. They cannot control whether the entire game team delivers on time. They can influence it: how they communicate blockers, how early they flag risk, the relationships they build with the teams around them. The action is in their hands. The outcome is not.

Helping them find that sphere of influence gives them back some agency over their results. That is not the same as the old metric, but it is a start.

These three questions work well in 1-on-1s after a significant change:

"What was your favorite part about how we used to measure your work?"

"What does doing a good job look like to you under the new goals?"

"Where do you feel like you have the most control right now?"

They do two things. They name the loss without you having to diagnose it directly. And they start building a new sense of what success looks like from where the person actually is, not where you think they should be.

You should not be trying to skip the grief. You are trying to move through it with them. That is a different job than explaining the decision, and it is the one that actually works.

---

Monday Action

Before your next 1-on-1 with someone who has been pushing back after a change, pick one of those three questions. Lead with it. Do not explain the decision first. Ask the question and listen for what they have actually lost, not just what they are saying.
