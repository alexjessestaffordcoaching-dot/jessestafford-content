How Management Roles Actually Open Up (And Why Most Engineers Miss It)

Quick Bridge: You keep waiting for the management role to get posted so you can apply for it. Most of the time, by the moment it shows up, the decision underneath it was already made weeks ago.

I was told growing up: do not do a bad job, because you never know when your next boss is watching. That has held true for my entire career. Every job change, every internal promotion, someone came to me and said, you are the best person for this.

I tell college students the same thing every year. The interview started the first day of freshman year. Not after you have the diploma. Not when you finally apply for the internship. Someone you know is going to have influence over your next role at some point, and you need to show up accordingly.

It works exactly the same way inside a company. When a management seat opens, the leaders who control it usually already know who they want before it's ever posted. Is that fair? No. Is it real? Yes.

I have lost count of the engineers I have mentored who wanted to move into a management role on another team, but had never spoken to a single person who worked there. They had not done the work to understand what the team actually did or why it mattered to the business. Then they could not figure out why they didn't get picked.

At that point, they are no better than an external hire. They are an unknown quantity, and the gamble on an unknown quantity is always the bigger one.

The Seat Doesn't Open the Way You Think

A management seat only gets created three ways. The team grows and needs a new layer of leadership. Someone leaves the current seat, through promotion, transfer, or exit. Or a reorg draws a new line and a gap appears where a leader has to sit.

That's the whole list. Notice what isn't on it: "a spot opened up on the careers page." By the time a req gets posted, one of those three things already happened, usually weeks or months earlier. The posting is a formality. It is not the decision point.

Fewer seats are opening, and the ones that do open get filled faster by whoever the team already trusts. The window to position yourself before a role gets posted is shrinking every year.

I watch engineers treat "apply when something comes up" as a strategy. It isn't one. It's a bet that you'll be the known name in the room when the growth, departure, or reorg happens, instead of competing against someone who already is.

Positioning and People Skills Compound. They Do Not Compete.

Most advice here tells you to pick a lane. Either get better at leading people, or get better at being visible. Pick one and go all in.

Project leadership and people leadership are different skills. You can be excellent at one and never touch the other. Most people leaders proved themselves in a project role first, then used the relationships they built there to grow the people side.

That is the part that compounds. Positioning without a real proof point gets you a friendly conversation and nothing else. People skills without positioning means the team that would trust you with a seat has never heard your name.

How to Apply It

Talk to the team before the role exists. If you want to lead a specific team, go learn what they actually do and why it matters, before there is anything to apply for. Do this now, not the week a posting appears.

Share the goal past your own manager. Your manager is not the only person deciding. If they are the only one who knows what you want, you are betting your entire case on one advocate. Tell the people above your manager, and your manager's peers, what you are working toward.

Train your replacement before you need to. If you are the only person who can do your current job well, you are not a candidate for the next one. You are a retention risk the org cannot afford to move. Mentor someone into the parts of your role that matter most, on purpose, before you need that argument to hold up.

Name which mechanism is actually in play. Growth, departure, or reorg. If you can identify which one is closest to happening on the team you want, you know exactly where to spend your time, and who needs to already know your name before it does.

Before you spend the next six months on any of this, ask yourself one honest question. Do you want to lead people, or do you want the title? Wanting to fix the people problems on a team is different from wanting to stop writing code. If it's the code you're tired of, this seat will not fix that.

Monday, Try This

Pick one team you would want to lead. Find one person on it you do not currently talk to. Set up fifteen minutes this week, not to ask for anything, just to understand what they actually do and why. That conversation is worth more than any application you will ever submit.
