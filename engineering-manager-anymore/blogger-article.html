Why You Didn't Get Considered for the Management Role You Wanted

Quick Bridge: You have been doing the work that should put you in line for a management role, and somehow you still weren't in the room when the decision got made.

I was a senior individual contributor when I decided I wanted to move into management. Every one-on-one, I told my manager where I wanted to go and what I could take on to get there. Eventually I interviewed for two management roles outside the company, at the same time I was quietly building toward one inside it. I was offered both.

I wasn't fully sold on either one, so I brought all three options back to my manager. He asked me to talk to his boss, and their boss, before I made a decision. That is when I found out my manager's boss had no idea I wanted to move into leadership at all. That conversation shifted something for me. I had spent a year building visibility with exactly the wrong people.

My manager liked having me as an individual contributor and had quietly put me in for a different promotion instead. Nobody above him even knew there was a decision to make. I had done everything the advice columns tell you to do, and it still almost went nowhere, because I was only telling one person.

The Technical Leader Promotion Decision Happens Before the Posting

I watch engineers wait for a management role to show up on the internal careers page, then apply and hope their resume speaks for itself. That approach assumes the posting is the start of the decision. It almost never is.

A management seat gets created exactly three ways. The team grows and needs a new layer of leadership. Someone leaves the current seat through promotion, transfer, or exit. Or a reorg draws a new line and a gap opens where a leader has to sit. Nothing else creates the seat. Not a budget review, not a wish, not a resume sitting in a talent pool.

By the time a req actually gets posted, one of those three things has usually already happened, weeks or months earlier. The leaders closest to that decision typically already know who they want. The posting is a formality that lets HR run a process. It is not where the real decision gets made.

The Technology Leader Communication Gap That Actually Blocks You

Most advice on this topic tells you to pick a lane. Get better at leading people, or get better at being visible. That framing is wrong, because the two compound instead of competing.

Project leadership and people leadership are genuinely different skills. You can be excellent at running a project and never build the second skill at all. Most people who eventually lead teams proved themselves in a project role first, and used the relationships built there to grow the people side once they had a reason to.

That is the part most engineers miss. Positioning without a real proof point behind it gets you a friendly conversation and nothing else. People skills without positioning means the team that would eventually trust you with a leadership seat has never heard your name in the first place. You need both, and they have to build on each other, not sit in separate boxes.

Why Internal Promotions Outperform External Hires

Wharton professor Matthew Bidwell tracked seven years of employee data across financial services, investment banking, and publishing. External hires scored significantly lower on performance evaluations for their first two years on the job than employees promoted internally into the same kind of role, and were 61 percent more likely to be fired. External hires were also paid 18 percent more than the internal promotes doing the same work (Knowledge at Wharton, "Why External Hires Get Paid More, and Perform Worse, than Internal Staff").

Companies already know this. It is exactly why an internal candidate who is a known quantity has such an advantage over an external one, and exactly why an internal candidate nobody knows is treated like an external hire anyway. You do not get the benefit of being internal just by having a badge. You get it by being known.

What to Do Before There Is Anything to Apply For

Talk to the team before the role exists. If you want to lead a specific team, go learn what they actually do and why it matters to the business, before there is a posting to respond to. Waiting until the role opens means you are starting the relationship at the exact moment you need it to already exist.

Share the goal with more than your direct manager. I only found out my manager's boss didn't know about my goals because I happened to get three offers at once and forced the conversation. Do not wait for that kind of pressure. Tell the people above your manager, and your manager's peers, what you are working toward. If your manager is the only one who knows, you are betting your entire case on a single advocate.

If you are the only person who can do your current job well, you are not a candidate for the next one. You are a retention risk the organization cannot afford to move. Mentor someone into the parts of your role that matter most, on purpose, well before you need that argument to hold up in a promotion conversation.

Name which of the three mechanisms is actually closest. Growth, departure, or reorg. If you can identify which one is nearest on the team you want, you know exactly where to spend your time and exactly whose radar you need to be on before it happens.

The Org Chart Math Is Working Against Anyone Who Waits

This isn't just advice, it's math. Companies like Bayer, Amazon, and Meta have all made public commitments to flattening their management layers, and the data reflects it: the average manager's span of control climbed from 8.2 direct reports in 2013 to 12.1 in 2025 (Forbes, "Management Cuts Today Are Shaping A Leadership Shortage").

Fewer seats are opening every year, and the ones that do open get filled faster, by whoever the team already trusts. Waiting for a posting used to be a slow strategy. Now it is closer to no strategy at all. The math means the identification window before a role posts is shrinking, not growing, which makes being the known quantity more valuable every year this trend continues. None of this guarantees the seat opens when and how you want it to. But it changes what the decision looks like when it does.

On Monday, Look For This

Pick one team you would want to lead someday. Find one person on it you don't currently talk to. Set up fifteen minutes this week, not to ask for anything, just to understand what they actually do and why it matters. That conversation will do more for your case than any application you submit later.
