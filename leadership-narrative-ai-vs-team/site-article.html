How to Talk to Executives About AI When Your Team Disagrees

Quick Bridge: If you have to talk to executives about AI and your team's experience does not match theirs, you can keep their credibility and still give them the real picture in two sentences.

I lead teams, and I also coach other leaders. A technology leader I coach sat through a quarterly update where the savings from AI were announced as settled. The slide had one big number on it. Everyone in the room nodded, and she nodded too.

She told me afterward that she knew her team's version did not match, and she did not know how to say it without sounding negative. Nobody had asked her to speak. She was afraid that if she did, the first thing the room would hear was a no.

That moment is common. What most leaders are missing in it is a sentence that can carry two true things at once.

What Each Side Is Actually Measuring

Executives and teams are usually not disagreeing about AI. They are describing different work.

A survey of 5,000 white-collar workers in the US, UK and Canada by Section, reported by Computerworld in January 2026 and citing the Wall Street Journal, found that over 40% of C-level executives say AI saves them more than eight hours a week. Two-thirds of non-managerial employees say it saves them less than two hours a week, or no time at all.

Both groups can be telling the truth. From what I see, a leader's week is mostly drafting, summarizing and framing, which is work AI handles well. A team's week runs through a legal review, a budget sign-off, another group's priorities and decisions that sit with other people, and those do not get faster because a tool writes faster.

It is the same shift I wrote about in Stop solving problems that don't exist yet: AI makes trying things cheap, while the decisions around them stay slow.

BCG's CEO Insomnia Index from October 2026 found that 40% of employees say their CEO has not been transparent enough about how AI will affect their roles, while only 15% of CEOs worry their approach to AI could erode trust.

Gallup's AI indicator reports that employees who strongly agree their manager supports their team's AI use are 1.7 times as likely to use AI a few times a week or more.

You are the one person who has heard both sides. That makes the update yours to give, whether or not anyone asked for it.

How to Talk to Executives About AI Without Sounding Like a Skeptic

The order of your sentences decides how you are heard. In a room that has already decided AI is working, whatever you say first is taken as your position on AI. If it is a problem, you get filed as the person who is against it.

Start with what is true about the gain. Then say where the rest of the time goes. Then give the date or the number you can stand behind. Those are three short steps, and they fit in well under a minute.

It is a translation, not a correction. You are not telling an executive they are mistaken about their own experience. You are adding the part of the picture that only you can see. It is the same habit of giving people context instead of a rule that I wrote about in When Following the Process Means Missing the Goal.

When two people look at the same thing and see different things, I ask one question: what would have to be true for them to reach that conclusion? When an executive says AI saved them a month, the answer is easy. For their own work, it did.

What to Say to an Executive Who Thinks AI Is Working

"You are right that the drafting and summarizing got faster, and my team feels it too. What has not sped up is the decisions and the sign-offs from other groups, so the date I can commit to today is the one we already have."

I used a version of this recently with my own team, which had just finished its part of a project and was waiting for a customer to confirm what they wanted. When the date came up, I could say yes to how fast the team had moved and still point at where the project was waiting. You can agree with the gain and be honest about the date in the same breath.

When the date cannot hold, I keep the first sentence and change only the last part: "I can pull in the parts of the work that are drafting, and the date for the rest stays where it is until the decisions move."

When you do not know yet, the honest thing is to say so and to say when you will: "I do not know yet how much of this will carry into the delivery date. I will bring you a number after the next release." Naming the date you will know keeps that answer from sounding like hedging.

I run every update through one test, which is whether the executive would walk away knowing what I need them to know if they only held on to the first thirty seconds.

This does not work every time. Leading with a yes lowers the chance of being heard as a blocker without removing it, and some rooms will still take the second sentence as pushback.

Questions Executives Ask Next

The first answer usually brings a second question.

"So why is it not faster?" The part that got faster is not the part that sets the date. The approvals and decisions set it, and that is where I am working.

"Is your team using the tools?" Yes, and the gain is real for the drafting and summarizing. The rest of the project is waiting on other people, and I can show you where.

"Can you give me a number?" Give the one you can stand behind today, or the date you will have one.

"What if the executive's number looks off?" Usually it is right about their own work. Ask what it measures before you offer a different one.

What Not to Do

There are three things I avoid. I do not bring a counter-statistic, because a competing number turns a shared picture into a debate. I do not open with the problem, because even a careful, fair problem statement is heard first as a position. I do not save it for later either, because the longer a story sits unchallenged, the more it turns into a commitment.

The same facts land differently when they come after a yes, and a date that got pulled in on a good morning is hard to pull back. The other direction, carrying a decision from above down to your team, is the subject of You Just Got a Decision from Your Boss.

Where the Gain Is Landing on Your Team

A general update lands better when it says who the gain is helping. On my teams the more senior people are energized, because decisions that used to take them a long time are turning into results faster. The more junior people are spending more time and energy with the same tools, because they are still learning what good work looks like on this kind of project.

Saying so gives the executive a specific place where the work is paying off and a specific place where it is not yet. That is easier to hear than a general "it is mixed."

What to Do Monday Before Your Next AI Update

Write the three steps on one page, in your own words and with your team's real answers. Step one is what is true about the gain. Step two is where the rest of the time goes. Step three is the date or number you can stand behind, or the date you will know.

Then hand the page to one person on your team and ask what is missing. Listen to the answer before you change anything. Their version is the part of the picture the executive has not heard yet.

You do not need a better argument. You need two true sentences in the right order.
