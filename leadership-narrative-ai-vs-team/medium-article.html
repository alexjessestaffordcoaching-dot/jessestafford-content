What Do You Say When Leadership Thinks AI Is Working and Your Team Sees Something Different?

Quick Bridge: You are the one between what leadership says about AI and what your team sees. You can bring both into the room in two sentences, without sounding like the person who is against AI.

A director told the room AI had written his whole planning memo in an hour. Everyone smiled. Then he looked at me and asked if my team could pull our delivery date in.

I had two answers ready and neither fit. "Sure" was not true. The sentence on the tip of my tongue was "it does not quite work that way," and I could already hear how it would land. It would sound like the person who is against it.

The room was still smiling about the hour, and I was the only one holding both pictures.

What I worked out afterward was why the director and my team were both telling the truth, why my honest answer sounded like pushback, and what I say now. Then one thing to try before your next update.

What was he actually describing, and what was my team?

The Director and My Team Were Both Telling the Truth

He was describing drafting, summarizing and framing. That fills much of a director's day, and from what I see it is the kind of work AI does well. The memo really did take an hour. Nobody in that room was making it up.

My team was describing something else. The productivity on my teams has gone up drastically with these tools, and I will say that to anyone who asks.

What I have also seen is that with more teams moving faster, they reach decision points and organizational hurdles faster. A team finishes its work and then waits on a sign-off from another group that has its own priorities. More of my own time now goes to helping them work through the corporate side of a project.

When a team can finish its work faster than a decision can be made, the date gets set by decisions and reviews, meaning the checks and approvals from other people, not by how fast the work gets done. The director's hour was real, and it landed in a part of the work that was never holding the date.

Each picture was accurate for the work it described. Saying that out loud still felt like a risk.

Why the Honest Answer Sounds Like Pushback

It felt like a risk because the room had already settled the story. The facts were not the danger. The first sentence was.

Part of me did not want to be the person who says no in a room that was having a good morning. I tried the direct way first. "It is not really working out for the team. We are stuck waiting on decisions and review."

The director's smile went flat. He asked whether the team had actually been using the tools.

Same facts, and the room heard something different from what I said. They heard a position on AI. When a story is settled, whatever you say first gets read as your stance on it. Open with the problem and the room files you as the blocker, however carefully you say the rest.

I keep a rule for moments like this. When two people look at the same thing and see different things, one of them knows something the other does not. Ask what would have to be true for them to reach that conclusion.

For the director the answer was simple, because his own work really had gotten faster. The gap was about which work, not about who was right.

That left the question I could not answer in the room. What do you actually say, and in what order?

Say Yes First, Then Say Where the Time Goes

Recently the delivery date came up again, and I gave my answer the other way around.

"Yes, the drafting is faster, and my team feels it. The rest of our time goes to decisions and review, and those have not sped up, so the date I can stand behind is the one we already have."

I let the yes sit for a second before I added the rest, and I watched his face the whole time. He did not smile this time. He leaned in.

Saying yes first had felt like giving up the point I came to make. It turned out the point was still there.

Then he asked a different question. "What is holding the date?" We were talking about the real constraint instead of whether my team was using the tools.

He followed with the one that had been hiding under the first. "So why is it not faster?" I told him the answer was in the sign-offs and approvals, and that is where I am working.

That is the whole order, and it fits in a single breath. Say yes to what is true. Say where the rest of the time goes. Give the date you can stand behind.

Some days you cannot give a date yet, and the honest thing is to say that plainly.

"I do not know yet how much of this will show up in the delivery date. I will bring you a number after the next release."

Saying that is not hedging, because it comes with a date for when you will know.

None of this works every time. Saying yes first lowers the odds that the room files you as the blocker, and it does not remove them. Some rooms will still hear the second sentence as pushback.

I check every update against one test. If my boss only remembers the first thirty seconds, is that what I need them to know? Two true sentences and a date pass. A paragraph about how the reviews pile up does not.

You will probably have to say some version of this in your next meeting.

Monday Try This

Before your next update, write two sentences on a page.

"Yes, [what AI sped up], and my team feels it."

"The rest of our time goes to [decisions, reviews, waiting on other groups], which have not sped up, so [the date or number you can stand behind]."

Read them out loud. Could your boss repeat them in thirty seconds? If not, cut until they could. Then ask someone on your team where their time went this month, and listen before you change a word.
