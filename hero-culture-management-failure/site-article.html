Hero Engineer Culture: How to Stop Relying on One Engineer

Quick Bridge: If you are trying to understand hero engineer culture on your own team, start with the part most write-ups skip: how comfortable it is for the leader. By the end you can spot who your team leans on and take the first small step to spread what that person knows.

Count the times this week someone on your team said "just ask Alex." Not "ask the team." Alex.

If the count is more than two, one person holds something the rest of your group needs. Your group has learned to work around that fact instead of changing it.

What Hero Engineer Culture Looks Like From the Manager's Chair

From the outside, hero engineer culture looks like a great engineer. They answer fast, they take the hard request, and the work gets done. Questions that would stall a team of five stop at their desk and come back solved.

From the manager's chair it feels even better. Releases hold, escalations go quiet, and you are rarely surprised. Nobody calls it a risk, because on any one day it is not one.

I have been in that chair. On one of my teams a single engineer was the only person who understood a piece of shared code that every product headed to one market depended on. Each time that code needed work, launches slipped, and every other team waited in one line behind one person.

That is the pattern: a single point of failure that the leader has stopped seeing.

Why Single Points of Failure Form by Default

Nobody decides to build one. It forms because the best person gets the hardest request, and the hardest request teaches them something nobody else gets to learn. Do that for a year and the knowledge sits in one head.

The trap closes on the manager next. Training someone else means taking hours from the one person who is already behind, so the hours never get spent. The person is too busy to teach. Nobody else learns. The person stays too busy.

The risk has a blunt name in software: the number of people who would have to disappear before a project stalls is called the bus factor. A bus factor of one is not a personality type. It is a staffing fact that someone with authority let build up.

LeadDev, a publication for technology leaders, describes how being the go-to engineer starts as a compliment and can drift into silos and unclear ownership. The manager's side of that slide is the one almost nobody writes about.

What It Costs the Person Everyone Relies On

The cost to the team is easy to count: delays, a queue, a scramble on the day that person leaves. The cost to the engineer is harder to see, because it looks like a reward.

Being the only one who knows feels like job security. You also look good as their manager, because they are efficient. Both of those are true, and both are why the arrangement lasts. What it takes away is range. A person who is known for one thing never gets to build a well-rounded career.

If that engineer also works long hours to keep up, there is a second cost. Economist John Pencavel studied munition workers and found that output rises at a decreasing rate once hours pass a threshold, so more hours stop buying proportionally more work. It is one factory era, so read it as a direction, not a rule for your team.

There is a career consequence as well. I wrote about the individual contributor's side of it in why you cannot get promoted if you are irreplaceable. The same trait that makes someone hard to replace makes them hard to promote.

How to Spread What One Engineer Knows

Most advice here says to document, pair and rotate. Those all help. What they leave out is who schedules them, and the answer is you.

Assign work across projects on purpose. Give the next piece of the specialized work to someone else, and give the person who owned it something new. Decide before the next request arrives. A busy week will always win the argument against training.

Move people between teams. When someone new has to learn the work, cross training stops being optional. It works better than a document nobody opens.

Have the career conversation first. I sit Alex down and talk about where the team and the company are going, usually before I mention the work at all. Then I talk about their career, and I tell Alex softly and plainly that staying tied to this one area is not where their next step is.

After that, the work moves slowly. A colleague sits in for a few weeks, takes the first small piece, and the original owner starts on the new area. It is a handoff in stages, not a switch.

I am not sure how fast the stages should go on your team. I only know the harder case is the opposite one: a sudden move made when the person has already given notice.

When Heroics Are Fine

I am not asking you to remove your best people from hard problems. Some days you need someone who can jump in and solve what nobody else can. A team made of every type of person is stronger than one made of the same type.

The line is between using someone for different problems and letting them become the only person who can solve one. A real emergency is one where speed matters more than spreading knowledge. A false emergency is the one that exists because nobody planned a team that can cover for itself.

Watch for the second kind. If the same person is called in for the same fire every quarter, you have a schedule.

My own test is short. Can Alex take two weeks off right now? If the honest answer is "we cannot afford that," you have your single point. That is a dependency you built.

Questions Leaders Ask Next

What if the person does not want to share? For many people being the only one feels safe. That is why the conversation about their path comes before the handoff, so moving off the work reads as a step forward and not a loss.

What if they leave anyway? Then you are glad you started. In my experience someone always steps in and figures it out, but it takes longer and costs more stress when nobody saw it coming. A leader who has already spread the work gets to treat a departure as an opening for someone else. I wrote about planning for the day someone leaves in an earlier post.

Do I move the person or the work? Move the work first, before the next request arrives. Move the person second, and only when the knowledge stays behind. When I moved one engineer to a different department to limit delays, they kept working on the same codebase rather than starting fresh, and the team they had come from still scrambled when that person eventually left the company.

Monday Try This

Pick one engineer your team leans on. Write the sentence "If this person were out for two weeks, the first thing that stops is ___." Fill the blank with a real piece of work, not a general area.

Then tell the engineer you need an hour with <colleague name> sitting in. That is the whole first step. You do not need a plan for the rest of the quarter.

If your go-to engineer gave notice today, which piece of work would you not know how to cover?

You're great at the work. Let's make you impossible to ignore.
