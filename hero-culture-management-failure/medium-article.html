How to Stop Relying on One Engineer Who Knows Everything

Quick Bridge: One engineer holds what the rest of your team cannot cover. That is not a staffing fact. It is a decision you are still making.

"Just ask Alex."

I heard that phrase in planning meetings for months. One engineer on one of my teams was the only person who understood a shared library that every game headed to one market depended on.

When that code needed work, launches slipped. Every team with a request waited in the same line behind one person. Any question that touched that library came back to Alex, regardless of who else was available.

I noticed the dependency quickly. What I did not do was fix it.

The engineer was always available. Every question came back answered. When you have that, it is easy to decide it is not the right week to slow things down. It is easy to decide you need that person on the next request and you can address it next month.

Why You Do Not Fix It

There is always a good reason to wait.

Every hour I took from that engineer to train someone else was an hour the delays got worse. The training never started.

The person was too busy to teach. Nobody else learned. The person stayed too busy.

It was an uncomfortable spot. I could see the risk, and I still needed that engineer on the next request.

What made it harder to change anything was that it did not feel like a problem on any given day. It felt like having a very good engineer. Every question got an answer, and each time Alex said they could take it, I was relieved.

Relying on one engineer is a decision you make by default. For Alex it feels like job security. For you it looks efficient. In the end you are both worse off: the team has a single point, and Alex never builds a well-rounded career.

Eventually I had to move that engineer to another department to limit the delays to their work. They stayed on the same product. Later they left the company, and the other department had to scramble to get someone ramped up on what they had known.

The knowledge still lived in one head. When the head left, so did the knowledge.

That is what it looks like when you do not fix it. And the reason most managers do not fix it faster is not that they do not see it. It is that on the day it needs to change, there is always a good reason to wait.

How to Move the Work Off One Person

The option that is easy to look past is who schedules the work. The answer is you.

The mistake is waiting for a quiet week. You will wait a long time. The work moves when you decide before the next request arrives, not after it lands.

When the next piece of that specialized work comes in, decide who does it. Not Alex. Someone who does not know it yet.

That means two things in practice. Assign work across projects on purpose. Say it to Alex in one line: "The next piece of that work goes to the newer engineer, and you are on the new project."

You can also move people between teams. When someone new has to learn the work, the cross-training stops being optional.

Training time never shows up on its own, so you are the one who puts it on the calendar.

The harder part is the conversation, because Alex may like being the only one who knows. I sit Alex down and talk about where the team and the company are headed first. Then I talk about their career: "Here is where I see you in that. Staying tied to this work is not where your next step is. I want you to have more than one thing you are known for."

I say it softly, and I say it plainly. Sometimes that is the harder part: Alex may not see what staying in this role costs their career. The arrangement looks like security, but from the outside it looks more like a ceiling.

The engineer has a side of this too, and I wrote about it in why you cannot get promoted if you are irreplaceable.

Then I talk to the engineer taking over the work. I go slowly: "Over the next few weeks, Alex will sit in on this with you and take the first piece. You will start on the new work." One piece at a time, not a switch. The goal is not to remove someone from the work they know. It is to make sure someone else knows it too.

Keep Heroics for Real Emergencies

I am not against heroes. A team needs people of all types, and some days you need someone who can jump in and solve a problem nobody else can. Use your heroes for different problems. Do not let them become the only person who can do one thing.

Here is a test I use: Can Alex take two weeks off right now? If your honest answer is "we cannot afford that," you have your answer. That is a dependency you built.

The emergencies to watch are the false ones, and the ones you created because you did not plan a sustainable team. If the fire exists because only one person knows the code, it is not an emergency. It is a schedule you chose.

I have seen someone leave or be out more times than I can count. Someone always steps in and figures it out. It always takes longer and costs more stress than it had to. The times it went smoother were the times someone had already started learning that work.

What to Do Monday

Write down one name. Then write one sentence: "If ___ were out for two weeks, the first thing that stops is ___."

Name who needs to sit in on that work. Tell Alex directly: "We need <colleague name> to sit in with you on this."

Do not announce a program or build a skills matrix. One name, one sentence, one conversation.

Who is the person your team would struggle most without, and what is the first piece of work you could start moving?

You're great at the work. Let's make you impossible to ignore.
