How to Stay Clear-Headed When Everything at Work Is Falling Apart

I was in high school, running cross country, and I was deep enough into a race that everything hurt. My legs were burning. My lungs were telling me I had hit my limit. Every physical signal I could feel was sending the same message: stop.

And that message was getting pretty convincing.

But somewhere in the middle of that race, I started paying attention to what I was actually telling myself. My body was telling me I could not keep going. And I realized I had not actually decided whether that was true. I had just started believing it because it arrived with a lot of conviction.

So I slowed my breathing deliberately, on purpose, in a race where slowing anything felt like falling behind. I pushed past what every signal said was the limit. I kept moving.

That was not willpower in the motivational-poster sense. It was something more specific than that. I was learning to notice the conclusion I had already reached.

"I cannot keep going" was an interpretation, not a fact. That is a different thing. And once I saw that difference, I never really stopped looking for it.

I carried that into everything that came after.

The Window Between What Happened and What You Decide

A project gets cancelled. A team misses a deadline. You get passed over for a promotion someone else was handed.

Something like that happens, and something else follows right behind it. An internal reaction you did not choose. Maybe you absorb it and keep moving. Maybe it sits with you for the rest of the week.

But you probably did not decide how to respond. It just happened, before you had a chance to intervene.

What you missed is the window that exists between what happened and how you interpreted it. That window is where leadership actually happens.

If you interpret a missed deadline as a sign your team cannot be trusted, you may start to track more closely and micromanage. If you interpret it as information about a process gap, you will ask different questions in the debrief.

The situation was identical. The frame was different. The decisions that followed were completely different.

The frame you land on shapes every decision that follows. Take the "who is to blame" path and you spend the next months eroding trust with a team that was doing what they believed was best. Take the "what do we need to change" path and you fix the process instead.

Mindset Is Not an Attitude. It Is a Practice.

Here is where I usually lose patience with leadership content.

The word "mindset" has been overused to the point of meaninglessness. It shows up on motivational slides. It gets invoked when someone does not want to address a real structural problem. "You just need a better mindset" has become a way to dismiss legitimate concerns rather than engage with them.

That is not what I am describing.

I noticed the same pattern watching athletes I respected under competitive pressure. The ones who performed best were not the ones who felt less. They had practiced what to do with what they felt.

A Harvard Kennedy School field study of over 4,000 workers found the same pattern. Leaders who practiced reappraising the situation, not just the emotion, showed lasting improvements in performance and lower stress. The effects lasted a full month after the study ended. That persistence is the mark of a trained skill, not a personality trait you either have or do not.

You can practice this. That is the whole point.

The Learner Question vs. the Judger Question

Marilee Adams wrote a book called Change Your Questions, Change Your Life. The core idea is a Choice Map: in any situation, you are operating from either a Learner frame or a Judger frame.

Learner frame: open, curious, asking what happened and what you can do about it.

Judger frame: closed, critical, asking who is to blame and what is wrong with everything.

I came across this after I had already been doing something like it without the vocabulary. The cross country reframe was Learner mode before I had a name for it.

"Can I keep going?" produces different results than "This hurts too much to continue." One opens options. One closes them.

Here is what this looks like at work.

The team misses a deadline. Judger question: "What is wrong with this team?" Learner question: "What did we not see coming, and what do we need to change?"

The Judger question puts you in fault-finding mode. The Learner question puts you where you can actually do something about it.

You get passed over for a promotion. "Why does this always happen to me?" leaves you stuck for weeks. "What do the people who made this decision not know about my work?" gives you something to do Monday morning.

The reframe does not change the facts. It changes which facts feel relevant and which actions feel possible.

What I Changed About My Language

Years ago, I stopped telling people I was busy. Not because I was not busy, but because I noticed what saying it actually accomplished. I convinced people I had no time for them. And I increased my own stress by framing my situation in a way that made it feel worse.

I started saying things like "I have been involved in a lot of great conversations today" or "I have not gotten as much done as I expected today." The language was more accurate and less loaded.

This sounds trivial. It was not. After doing it consistently, my own stress responses shifted. I was practicing the reframe in the smallest possible unit.

A sentence. A word choice. A conclusion I did not have to hold.

That discipline, built in small moments, is what made it available in bigger ones. When a project went sideways, I already had the habit of asking which conclusion I was holding and whether I had chosen it deliberately.

---

How to Build the Habit

The reframe is a skill, which means it gets better with deliberate practice, not with motivation.

Start by noticing when you are in Judger mode. It has a recognizable feeling: certainty. You are certain who is to blame, certain why something will not work, certain the situation is fixed and bad. That certainty is the signal.

When you notice it, do not push it away. Sit with it for a moment. Then ask it one question: "Is this the only conclusion available?"

Most of the time, it is not.

You do not have to abandon the first conclusion entirely. Sometimes you land in Judger mode because something genuinely went wrong and someone genuinely should be accountable. But even then, the move to Learner mode helps you handle it more effectively.

The difference between a leader who handles hard situations well and one who gets stuck in them is rarely the situation itself. It is the question they are asking about it.

---

Monday Action

This week, when something at work goes sideways, catch the first conclusion your brain lands on. Write it down. Then ask: what would I need to believe for a different conclusion to be available?

You do not have to take the different conclusion. Just notice whether it exists.

That noticing is the beginning of the practice. I started it on a cross country course in high school, trying to slow my heart rate. Twenty years later, it is still one of the most useful things I know how to do in a hard moment.
