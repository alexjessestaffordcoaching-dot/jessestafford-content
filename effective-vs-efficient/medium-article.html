Quick Bridge: Your team hit an efficiency target and nobody outside your department is celebrating. The work you removed from your step did not disappear. It moved, and whoever is holding it now did not volunteer for it.

The Efficiency Win Nobody Else Is Celebrating

A team I worked with automated their intake process. Requests that used to take three days to route by hand now moved in under an hour. Leadership mentioned it in the year-end review.

The team that built the form was proud of it, and they should have been. It was genuinely faster.

Three people in finance used to read every request before it went anywhere. They caught the weird ones.

The requests missing a field. The ones using the wrong category code. The edge cases the form categories never quite captured, the situations where someone needed to use judgment about whether this request fit the standard template or needed a different path entirely.

When the automated form replaced that manual step, those three reviewers moved on to other work.

The judgment calls they used to make did not move on with them. The work moved upstream.

Every team submitting requests through finance, all thirty of them, now had to do that screening themselves. Before submitting anything, they had to figure out whether their request fit the template, catch their own exceptions, and decide what to do with the edge cases that fell outside the dropdown options. What three people in finance used to absorb quietly, as part of their job, was now distributed across thirty departments.

Invisibly. Without announcement. Without anyone agreeing to it.

Nobody called a meeting about this. Nobody sent a note saying, "Hey, finance used to handle the gray areas and now you will." The thirty teams just started noticing that their submissions took longer to prepare.

That there were questions they did not know how to answer on the form. That requests occasionally bounced back for reasons they did not anticipate.

Finance was faster. The total work in the organization was not. It just got harder to see, because it was spread thin across people who had no idea the job had moved to their desk.

Work Is Like Energy

Work is like energy. It does not just go away. You need to know where you are moving it to.

Speed up your step and the friction has to land somewhere. Time, cost, and quality are always in relationship with each other. Push on one and the other two absorb the pressure.

If your step gets faster, something else in the system got harder or more expensive somewhere. That trade does not disappear because you did not see it happen.

Think about how a racing team handles this. They do not assume the drag disappears. They plan who carries it.

One car drafts another in a race. The car in front breaks the wind. The car behind it gets to run more efficiently, with less drag and lower fuel burn.

That works. It is real physics.

But the car in front is working harder because of it. Somebody has to run less efficiently so the team wins. That is not a flaw in the design. It is the design.

Everyone who agreed to draft knows the lead car is paying the tab for the group.

That is a planned trade. The team decided before the race who absorbs the drag and why.

The finance story ran the same physics without the plan. Thirty teams absorbed more friction. Finance ran faster.

Nobody decided that. Nobody announced it. Nobody told those teams they were now carrying what three people in finance used to handle.

They found out gradually, as the prep work for each submission started taking longer than it used to, without anyone being able to explain exactly why.

When that kind of trade is designed on purpose, it is strategy. When it just happens, it is a tax you passed on to people who had no vote.

Processes can be efficient. Individual steps cannot. A step is only efficient when you know where the friction it removed actually went, and when whoever is carrying it knew it was coming.

How to Catch It Before Someone Else Pays For It

You do not need a new system for this. You need three questions, asked before the launch.

Ask where the friction went, not just where it left.

"We saved three days on this step" is the first half of the sentence. The second half is: and here is where those three days of judgment work ended up instead. If you cannot finish that sentence, you measured your own step. You did not measure the process.

Knowing how much faster your step got is not the same as knowing what it cost the people around you. Those are different questions, and most teams only measure the first one.

Ask who is now making the calls your process used to make.

In the finance intake process, the three reviewers knew which requests were missing something important and which ones just looked different from the usual format. That judgment lived in their heads, not in the system. Once they moved on to other work, nobody was left who could tell the difference.

Before you automate or streamline a manual step, find out what your people were actually deciding when they slowed down. Then make sure someone is still deciding it. And if the job moved, tell the people who now hold it before they find out on their own.

Ask whether anyone agreed to carry the drag.

Sometimes the right move is for one team to absorb more work so the whole system runs faster. That is fine. The lead car in a draft is not losing. It is making a known trade for the benefit of the group.

What is not fine is that team discovering the trade themselves, weeks after it happened, when they start wondering why their workload got heavier without anyone saying anything. If you are making a trade, name it. Say who is taking on what and why. That is the difference between a decision and an unmarked bill.

The teams that avoid this pattern give the people around them a heads-up before the faster version goes live, not after the friction shows up on someone else's plate. It is the same discipline: context beats process every time.

Your Monday Action

Find one thing your team sped up or automated in the last six months. Find the team one step upstream from where you sit. Ask them one question:

"Since we made that change, has anything gotten harder on your end that we have not heard about yet?"

You might get an answer you were not expecting. And that answer will tell you whether the win was a process win or just a step win.
