Quick Bridge: Your team made their step faster, and somewhere upstream, someone's week got harder. This post explains why that happens, what the research on automation says about it, and three questions to ask before you call an efficiency gain a win.

When Making Your Process Faster Just Moves the Work Somewhere Else

The question I come back to every time a team announces an efficiency gain is not "how much faster?"

It is "where did the friction go?"

The answer is almost always a long pause.

That pause is the problem. The gain was real. The routing time dropped, the handoffs accelerated, the metric improved. But nobody traced where the friction that used to live in that step actually landed. And once you stop looking for it, it becomes someone else's Tuesday.

I worked with a team that automated their intake process. Three people in finance used to read every request before it went anywhere. They were the ones catching the weird ones: the requests missing a field, the submissions that used the wrong category code, the edge cases that needed a judgment call about whether this fit the standard path or needed to go somewhere else.

The automated form cut routing time from three days to under an hour. Leadership called it a win.

What they did not measure was what happened upstream.

Every team submitting requests through finance, all thirty of them, was now doing that screening themselves. Before they could submit anything, they had to figure out whether their request fit the template, catch their own exceptions, and decide what to do with the situations that fell outside the dropdown options. What three people in finance used to absorb quietly, as part of their role, was now distributed across thirty departments.

Invisibly. Without announcement. Without anyone agreeing to it.

The thirty teams just started noticing that their submissions took longer to prepare. That requests occasionally bounced back for reasons they could not trace. Finance was faster. The total work in the organization was not.

Why Engineering Team Efficiency Gains Don't Always Reduce Organizational Work

Here is the part that does not show up in any metric: local efficiency improvements do not automatically produce system efficiency. They redistribute work.

Work behaves more like energy than a task list. When you speed up a step in a process, you have not removed the friction that step used to absorb. You have moved it to wherever the next constraint is, and it shows up there as someone else's time, judgment call, or exception to handle.

A team that cuts their processing time in half has not saved the organization half the time. They have saved themselves that time. The question is whether they also created a corresponding cost somewhere else, and if so, whether anyone knew that was coming.

The iron triangle is the underlying physics here: time, cost, and quality are always in relationship with each other. Push on one and the other two absorb the pressure.

This is not a bug in how processes work. It is a property of any system where resources are finite. Speed something up and the friction has to land somewhere. The only real question is whether anyone made a deliberate choice about where.

How Process Automation Creates Invisible Work in Other Teams

Automation is where this pattern shows up fastest, because it does not just speed up a human decision. It removes one entirely.

Lisanne Bainbridge described this mechanism in her foundational paper "Ironies of Automation," and the finding has held up for decades. When you automate the routine, predictable parts of a process, you do not eliminate the human workload. You concentrate it into the hardest, least predictable parts (doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-890046-8)).

Exception handling. Judgment calls. Catching the things the system was never built to recognize.

In the finance intake story: the three reviewers were doing exactly that work. They were not processing standard requests. They were handling the ones that fell outside the standard. The form removed that capacity. The edge cases did not go away. They moved upstream, to teams who never asked for the job and were never told it had moved.

The form had no person reading anything. It routed whatever was typed into it. The judgment work that used to happen inside finance now happened before finance, in the prep work of thirty different teams, each independently learning the same hard lesson about what the form would and would not accept.

What Happens to Upstream Teams When Nobody Tells Them the Job Moved

This is the part that never makes it into the efficiency report.

The upstream teams do not know why their submissions take longer now. They notice it gradually. The prep work that used to feel routine starts feeling like more. They build workarounds. They start keeping notes about which request types tend to bounce back. They develop informal expertise in navigating a process that was never explained to them.

They do not connect it to the intake automation three months ago, because nobody told them those were related. They assume their own process is the problem.

Someone writes a process doc. Someone else schedules a meeting. The actual root cause, that judgment work moved to their desk without notice, stays invisible for months.

This is what unplanned friction redistribution looks like from the inside: a slow, diffuse increase in prep time that nobody can explain. The official efficiency metric shows the intake team getting faster. The unofficial cost shows up as overhead in thirty different places, tracked in none of them.

Three Questions Engineering Leaders Should Ask Before Calling a Process Change a Win

You do not need a new process for this. You need three questions, asked before the launch.

Ask where the friction went, not just where it left. "We cut three days off this step" is the first half of the sentence. The second half is where those three days of judgment work ended up instead. If you cannot answer that, you have measured your own step. You have not measured the process.

Ask who is now making the calls your process used to make. Manual steps are usually catching things nobody wrote down. Before you automate or streamline a manual step, find out what your people were actually deciding when they slowed down.

In the finance story: the three reviewers knew which requests were missing something important and which ones just looked different from the usual format. That judgment lived in their heads. Once they moved on, nobody was left who could tell the difference. Find out what that is in your process before you eliminate the position that held it.

Ask whether anyone agreed to carry the drag. Sometimes the right move is for one team to absorb more work so the whole system runs faster. That is fine.

What is not fine is that team discovering the trade themselves, weeks after it happened, when they start wondering why their workload got heavier without anyone saying anything. If you are making a trade, name it. Say who is taking on what and why. That is the difference between a decision and an unmarked bill.

Your Next Move This Week

Pick one thing your team automated or sped up in the last six months. Find the team that was one step upstream of that change. Ask them directly: "Since we changed that, has anything gotten harder on your end that we have not heard about?"

You will probably learn something you were not expecting. And you will have caught it before it became a pattern that nobody could trace back to where it started.
