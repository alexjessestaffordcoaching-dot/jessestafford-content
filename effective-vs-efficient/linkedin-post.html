Automation does not remove human decisions. It relocates them.

A finance team I worked with automated their intake process. Three people in finance used to read every request before it went anywhere. They were not processing standard submissions. They were handling the ones that fell outside the standard: missing fields, wrong category codes, edge cases that needed a judgment call.

When the automated form replaced them, the judgment work moved upstream. Thirty teams who submitted through finance now had to do that screening themselves. They never knew the job had changed.

Lisanne Bainbridge described this mechanism in 1983. When you automate the routine parts of a process, you do not eliminate the human workload. You concentrate it into the hardest, least predictable parts. Exception handling. Judgment calls. Catching the things the system was never built to recognize.

Efficiency gains do not make decisions disappear. They move them. The question is whether the people who now hold them know it.
