## Shared Elements

**Core situation:** A process gets faster in one place, and thirty teams upstream find themselves doing judgment work that three people used to handle — without being told the job moved.
**Context box text:** WORK DOESN'T DISAPPEAR. IT MOVES.
**Emotional tone:** Direct and systems-minded — not alarmed, not a failure story. Jesse's expression should read as clear-eyed and observational: this is what happens, here is the physics of it. The images should carry the post's tone, which is sobering but not accusatory.

## Concept 1 — Three and Thirty

**Visual metaphor:** A small, clean processing station on the right side of the frame (representing the automated form — sleek, compact, humming) feeds into a single open channel. But to its left, spreading out upstream, thirty smaller stations are all running in parallel, each with its own small stack of papers being handled independently. Three of them are slightly taller/larger than the rest — ghost-form or lightly sketched, as if they used to exist in a different role. Jesse stands at the junction, looking toward the thirty scattered stations rather than the efficient box.
**First-second read:** One clean box on the right; thirty busy workstations spreading left and back across the frame. The contrast reads before the text: the fast part and the distributed cost are both visible in the same image.
**Compositional approach:** Jesse occupies the middle ground at the junction point, smaller than the overall composition but clearly the focal observer. His gaze traces the same direction as the thirty stations, not back at the intake box.

### Full Expanded Prompt

COMPOSITION — most important: Wide shot, 16:9. The frame is organized from right to left. In the right quarter, a compact, clean automated intake station sits small and orderly, suggesting smooth processing. Jesse stands at the center of the frame, body turned toward the left, facing outward toward a spread of thirty smaller workstations arranged in a loose grid that fills the left two-thirds of the frame. The thirty stations are rendered slightly smaller than Jesse, suggesting scale-at-a-distance. Three of the stations are rendered as faint ghost outlines rather than solid forms, positioned slightly apart from the others. Generous white negative space fills the area between Jesse and the spread of stations, emphasizing the scale of the distribution. Jesse does not look at the viewer.

ART STYLE — Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision — not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching. Lines should suggest a technical patent drawing or a well-crafted indie comic panel. Loose, organic watercolor fills — visibly hand-painted. Color intentionally bleeds slightly outside the ink lines or leaves small white gaps at boundaries. Wet-edge textures and transparency variations are required — these are features, not errors. No digital gradients. No glow effects. No perfectly aligned fills. Background: clean white paper texture (#ffffff) with steampunk mechanical elements — objects have exposed mechanics — rendered in charcoal linework as abstract environmental texture. Mechanical background elements suggest systems and structure without being literal or dominant. 16:9 aspect ratio, single panel.

COLOR PALETTE — Primary watercolor wash: #100299 (deep blue) on Jesse's shirt. Canvas and negative space: #ffffff. Linework and shadows: #333333. Shadows are a semi-transparent charcoal watercolor wash, never solid black fill. The thirty workstations carry a warm, muted supporting tone (dusty amber or rust), making them visually distinct from the clean blue-and-white intake side. The three ghost stations are rendered in a lighter, more transparent version of the charcoal ink, suggesting they were once solid but are no longer the primary mechanism.

PRIMARY CHARACTER — Man in his early 40s. Short buzz-cut hair. Neatly trimmed short beard and mustache. Brown eyes. V-neck long sleeve shirt in deep blue (#100299) watercolor wash. Charcoal pants. Standing at the junction, weight settled, arms loose at his sides. Expression: direct and focused, someone observing the physics of a system rather than reacting to a failure.

SCENE DESCRIPTION — A quiet, compact automated intake station sits in the right portion of the frame, running cleanly with no visible strain. Spreading upstream to the left, thirty smaller workstations extend across the frame, each with a small paper stack being worked independently. Three faint ghost-form stations are visible among the thirty, slightly elevated — suggesting a prior role that no longer exists. No other people in the frame. Background steampunk texture (exposed gears, pipe segments) stays minimal, concentrating near the two main areas and fading into white between them.

CONTEXT BOX — Uppercase sans-serif text inside a solid white (#ffffff) rectangle with a sharp charcoal (#333333) border. Placed at the extreme bottom of the panel. Text: WORK DOESN'T DISAPPEAR. IT MOVES.

TONE GUARDRAILS — This image should feel like two people talking through a real problem in plain language. Not a motivational poster. Not corporate (no handshakes, whiteboards, or team huddles). Not inspirational abstraction. Grounded, human, and specific. The mechanical background supports the scene — it does not compete with it.

## Concept 2 — The Planned Trade

**Visual metaphor:** Two cars in a drafting formation: one leads, one follows. The lead car is rendered with slightly more visual strain — faint lines suggesting wind resistance or extra effort, perhaps slightly larger in the frame. The following car runs clean. Jesse stands trackside, watching the formation, clipboard or nothing in his hands, observing the physics of the trade.
**First-second read:** One car is working harder so the other can run more efficiently. Both are visible, both are moving, and the transfer of effort between them is made legible by the contrast in their rendering.
**Why it works:** The NASCAR analogy is the post's central mechanism for the planned vs. unplanned trade. An image that shows the drafted formation makes the "strategy vs. tax" distinction visible without words — the lead car is absorbing the drag on purpose, for the group.

## Concept 3 — The Invisible Tax

**Unexpected angle:** Ironic juxtaposition. A prominent, well-lit small "win" — a process dashboard or metric display showing a dramatically faster routing time — sits in the foreground, well-framed and celebrating. In the far background, spread across the frame and easy to miss on first look, thirty small, faint submission forms are spread across the floor and walls — each one representing work the thirty upstream teams are now handling without knowing why. Jesse stands between the two, looking toward the background.
**First-second read:** Eye goes to the dashboard first — a clean, unambiguous metric improvement. Second look catches the spread of background forms. The win and the hidden cost exist in the same frame. Scale and placement determine which gets seen first.
**Why it works:** The post's core irony — "Finance was faster. The total work in the organization was not." — is staged visually. The efficiency win is visible, prominent, easy to celebrate. The redistribution is in the frame, but smaller and harder to see without looking.

### Full Expanded Prompt

Jesse stands just right of center, body turned slightly toward the background, between the dashboard and the back wall. The back wall and floor of the far background are covered with scattered, faint submission forms — small paper slips rendered in a lighter charcoal wash, spread across the background area. They are present and readable at a second look but do not dominate the foreground.

ART STYLE — Crisp, uniform charcoal ink outlines (#333333), medium line weight throughout. Comic book precision — not sterile flat-art, not heavy superhero cross-hatching.
