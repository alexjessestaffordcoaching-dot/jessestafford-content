How to Make Your Work Visible to a Manager Who Is Too Busy to Notice

Quick Bridge: Your manager has gone quiet, and you cannot tell if that means trust or that they are so busy they are not noticing your work. You can read the quiet more kindly, and write updates your manager takes in within thirty seconds and can repeat to their own boss.

A client of mine read me a message she had not sent. "I cannot tell if he trusts me or forgot I exist," she said. Her director had not asked one question about her biggest project in two months. She had written a check-in four different ways and deleted every version, and each one ended with "just checking this is on your radar."

She knew how that sounded. It asked him to reassure her, and he already had a full week.

You may know the other kind of boss, the one who wants a status update every morning. This is the opposite: a boss who asks for nothing. It is the same visibility question, aimed at someone with almost no attention to spare.

You can read the quiet before you start hunting for problems in it, write an update your manager takes in within thirty seconds, and send a plan they can approve by doing nothing. There is a Monday action at the end.

When Your Manager Goes Quiet, Your Work Probably Looks Fine

She was trying to work out what the silence meant, and the reading she feared most was the plainest one, that her director was not thinking about her at all. How you read the quiet decides what you send next.

I used to read it her way. When a manager was too busy for me, I took it to mean that I, or my work, was not a priority. It was an uncomfortable way to spend a week, rereading my last few messages and waiting for some sign either way. Today I hear the same quiet differently. If I could listen in on a manager who has gone silent, I expect I would hear something like this: "I am not focused on this right now, and that is because I believe it is going well."

It looks different from the manager's chair. When I am not paying attention to a team, it is because things are going well and I do not need to make them a priority. When I am deeply involved, something important has changed. My attention goes to what is a priority and to what my own boss is watching, and a team that looks fine gets neither.

A manager of twelve is holding about 37 topics at once, by my count: each person brings more than one project, question or problem, and on top of that comes the manager's own work and a layer above asking for a summary by end of day. Your work is one of those 37.

The quiet you are reading as a signal is, most likely, arithmetic.

I cannot always tell from the outside whether a particular quiet is trust or a lack of time. When I am unsure, I make it explicit, either by asking or by saying what I am assuming. Start from the kinder reading, because if you go looking for reasons your manager is unhappy with you, you will find them.

If you are honest about it, most of us are running a quiet plan here: do good work and wait to be noticed. It feels fair. With a stretched manager, it is the plan that leaves you invisible.

But looking fine is not the same as being seen, and when your manager does glance at your work, they will not stay long.

Write Your Update for the First Thirty Seconds

Before you send any update, ask what your manager is reading in the first thirty seconds, and whether that is what they need to know.

Managers only know what their team tells them, and then they have to carry that message up to their own boss, so what you send has to be something they can repeat between two meetings without opening your update again (I know, that is a lot to ask of an update, but it is how a message gets up the chain). If the one thing they need is buried in line five, they will not have it.

Most updates open with context, what happened this week and what is going well, and the ask comes last, after your reader has moved on. Cut the context down and put the ask on line one. You probably already know the one thing your manager must hear. Say it first.

My client tried this on her own last update. She read it the way her director would, between two meetings, and found the decision he had to make at the very bottom. "I would have skimmed it too," she said, and laughed.

Her first version read like this:

Before: Weekly update. The schedule rollout is 70% done and ahead of plan. Two hiccups this week, both fixed. Hiring is underway. One supplier question is still open. We need a decision on the start date by Thursday, and if we wait another week the rollout slips two weeks.

The version she sent read like this:

After: One decision from you by Thursday: the start date. If we wait another week, the rollout slips two weeks. Everything else is on track: the rollout is 70% done and ahead of plan, and this week's two hiccups are fixed.

The facts did not change. Only the order did.

If your manager cannot repeat your update without calling you, it is not finished.

If your manager always skims, tell more people. Send it to their peers and to yours, because the message travels further through several inboxes than through one. In my experience the cost of sending an update to one more person is small, and the cost of a manager never seeing it is not.

An update still only tells your manager where you are. What if you also gave them something to say yes to?

Send a Plan That Takes Something Off Their Plate

An overloaded manager is always hoping someone will take something off their plate without being asked: a worry they can drop, a task they do not have to own, a decision that simply goes away. A plan does that.

I learned this under a manager who only met with me when they needed something. Our one-on-ones did not exist. So I started emailing my priorities and what I planned to do next. Once, a team announcement needed to go out, and I told my manager they had one hour to stop me. I do not know what that manager was thinking, because we never talked about it. What I know is that I stopped waiting for permission and gave them an easy way to say no.

My client wrote her director one short plan: "I am going to move the rollout start to the weekend. Tell me by Thursday if you want me to stop."

The reply came that afternoon: "Looks right. Go ahead." She read it twice. For two months she had expected to defend that plan, and it was approved in two short sentences.

Give them a date to say no, and their silence becomes a yes.

It will not land with every manager. Some will not answer at all, and I do not have a clean answer for that case. I would still send the plan, because then at least the date is on record.

If this starts to feel like you are doing your manager's job, three things are worth a thought. You may not know all that your manager actually carries. You are also building the skill of taking a decision off someone's plate before they ask, which I picked up under that manager and which many managers struggle to build. And a manager who keeps handing you more is telling you something about how they see you.

Monday Try This

Take the next update you were already going to send. Read only the first three lines. If your manager's one decision is not in them, move it to line one.

Then add one sentence: "I am going to do X. Tell me by Y if you want me to stop."

You do not need your manager to change anything first.

What does your manager read in the first thirty seconds of your update, and is that the thing you need them to know?
