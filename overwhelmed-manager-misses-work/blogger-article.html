What to Do When Your Manager Is Too Busy to Notice Your Work

Quick Bridge: When you have a manager too busy to notice your work, the fix is smaller than a new job or a better boss. You can start this week by changing what your manager reads first in your next update.

For years, when a manager went quiet on me, I took it to mean my work was not a priority, and I spent those weeks going back over my last emails looking for a sign either way. If you have a manager too busy to notice your work, you may be reading the same silence the same way, and it is an easy read to carry around for months.

The pattern is common. You do good work, nobody says much about it, and the person who writes your review has seen only a slice of it. The rest of this is what to do about it, in order.

Why a Manager Too Busy to Notice Your Work Is Not a Verdict

Sit in your manager's chair for a minute. When I am not paying attention to a team, it is usually because things are going well and nothing there needs me. When I am deeply involved, something important has changed. Attention goes to whatever is a priority and whatever my own boss is watching.

That makes a quiet manager a very different thing from a hostile one. Gallup's U.S. data found that the average manager's number of direct reports rose from 10.9 in 2024 to 12.1 in 2025. More people means more of everything. By my count, a manager of twelve is carrying about 37 topics at any moment, counting each person's projects and problems, their own work, and whatever the layer above is asking for.

The quiet you are reading as a signal is, most likely, arithmetic.

A client once described how this felt: she could not tell whether her director trusted her or had forgotten about her. He had not raised a single question about her biggest project, a schedule rollout, in two months. The Muse lists a busy boss as one reason good work goes unnoticed.

I cannot always tell from the outside which kind of quiet I am looking at. When I am unsure I make it explicit, by asking or by saying what I am assuming. Start from the kinder reading, which is also the more useful one, because you will write a calmer note when you are not trying to win back someone's attention. If you go looking for reasons your manager is unhappy with you, you will find them.

Be honest about the plan you have been running: do the work well and hope someone notices. It feels fair to me too, and with a stretched manager it keeps you invisible.

How to Write the Top of Your Update So It Gets Read

Your manager can only pass along what is easy to pass along, and whatever you send gets carried up the chain (I know that is a lot to ask of a note), so the test is simple: if they read only the top half minute, would they have what they need?

Most people lead with what happened and save the request for the end. Flip it. Take a note about a staffing change, the way it often goes out:

Before: Weekly note. The shift change is mostly in place. Two people missed their sign-ups and it is fixed. We talked to three candidates. Overtime is trending down. The scheduling supplier has not confirmed pricing. Can you approve the extra budget by Friday?

The same note with the ask first reads like this:

After: One approval needed by Friday: extra budget for a second shift. Without it, weekend coverage stays thin. The rest is on track: the shift change is mostly in place, and overtime is trending down.

Every fact that matters is still there. The difference is that a person with thirty seconds between meetings can act on the second one. For a busy reader, the top of the note is the whole note.

If your manager always skims, widen the circle. Copy their peers and your own peers on the summary. A message that lives in three inboxes survives a bad week in one of them, and in my experience the cost of sending it to one more person is small.

How to Send a Plan Your Manager Approves by Doing Nothing

An update tells your manager where you are. A plan gives them something to say yes to, and a stretched manager is quietly hoping someone will take something off their plate without being asked.

I once had a manager who only met with me when they needed something. Our one-on-ones simply did not exist. So I started emailing my priorities and what I planned to do next. Once, when a team announcement had to go out, I told them they had one hour to stop me. I do not know what that manager was thinking, because we never talked about it. I do know I had stopped waiting for permission.

The same shape worked for my client. She sent her director one short note: "I am going to move the rollout start to the weekend. Tell me by Thursday if you want me to stop." The reply came that afternoon: "Looks right. Go ahead." For two months she had expected to have to defend a plan, and it was approved in two short sentences.

A date on the note does two things. It gives your manager a clear way to say no, and it puts on record that you asked. If they say nothing, you move ahead, and nobody was surprised.

A date on the note turns silence into a yes.

Some of this will feel like doing your manager's job. It helps to remember that you may not see everything they carry, that you are building the skill of taking a decision off someone's plate before they ask, and that a manager who keeps handing you more is telling you something about how they see you.

What If Your Manager Has Checked Out?

It is a fair question, and the honest answer is that quiet can mean one of two things. It either means they are in a great place and believe you and they are aligned, or it means they are actively disengaged.

From where you sit, the two can look identical. The good news is that the habits above hold either way, because both reward the same thing: putting your work where more than one person can see it. If you have sent a note with a date a few times and heard nothing, that tells you something, and it is a good time to bring your manager's peers into the loop. I do not have a clean test beyond that, and I would rather say so than invent one.

This is the habit of showing your work proactively, aimed at an audience with almost no attention to spare.

Monday Action: Fix the First Three Lines

Pull up the next update you plan to send. Read the first three lines and nothing else. If the one decision you need from your manager is not there, move it up.

Then add a single line near the top: "I am going to do X. Tell me by Y if you want me to stop."

You do not need your manager to change anything first.
