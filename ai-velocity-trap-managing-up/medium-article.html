Where Are the AI Savings? What to Tell Leadership When Your Team Does More

Quick Bridge: Your team is faster with AI, and leadership is still asking where the savings are. You can give an honest answer and propose the measures that show what the extra speed is buying.

Leadership asked if we are really seeing gains that justify the AI spend, and I heard myself say no.

It came out before I had decided how to say it. The answer is true, and it made me sound like the tools had done nothing, when they have clearly helped my team get more done.

The time AI gave back went somewhere, leadership cannot see where, and nobody agreed on what it should buy. Sorting that out means looking at where the time went, why leadership cannot see a gain, and what to agree on before they ask again.

The Time Did Not Disappear. It Got Filled.

The tools work. They have increased my team's productivity a lot, and the work has moved. People spend more time planning the details of how a feature should work and less time writing code.

What I told leadership is that we are not seeing savings, not because the tools are not helping but because we are just doing more. Where we used to create 3 concepts to choose from, meaning three different ideas for what to build, we now create 15. My team is filling the time we gave it for the work.

Nobody had to tell them to. Give a capable team an hour back and they find something worth doing with it. That is a good instinct. It is also why the hour never shows up on a spreadsheet.

Fifteen options also take more deciding than three, so some weeks my team is stuck on what to build instead of how to build it. That is part of where the time goes.

I do not know that 15 concepts is worse than 3. It may be better. What I do know is that nobody agreed what the extra time should buy, so there is nothing to compare it against.

The team did get more done. Then why does leadership see no payoff for the spend?

Why Leadership Never Sees the Gain

Because leadership was waiting for savings. When a company buys AI licenses, the expectation behind the purchase is less time, lower cost, the same work done faster. The follow-up to my answer is usually some version of the same question: what did we get for it? Without an agreed measure, all I have to offer is a list of things the team did, and that is an awkward thing to hand over when the team is clearly getting more done and the proof of value is not there.

When I am the one asking, I am not trying to catch anyone out. Someone above me wants to know it worked, and I need something to say upward, and "the team is busy" is not it. In my experience the gain never really gets seen, because the team is producing more and nothing leadership watches counts that as value. The measure was never built to see what the tool delivers. A count of what got done cannot show whether the extra work moved a goal, helped a customer, or took cost out.

If the old numbers cannot show the gain, what goes in front of leadership instead?

Agree on the Measure Before They Ask Again

Start from what the business wanted when it bought the tools.

The gain shows up in goal progress, outcomes delivered, customer value delivered, and true savings measured and locked in. Each one asks what the extra time bought, not how much more the team made.

Locked in means the saved hours or dollars come out of the plan instead of being left open for the team to fill again.

I did something like this once with a team that works in short work cycles called sprints. I calculated what the team cost for one sprint, which came to $25,000, and at the review at the end of each sprint I asked: did we produce $25,000 worth of value to the company? I would use it again for this conversation.

The same question applies to my team's 15 concepts: which of them moved a goal or reached a customer? That answer is the gain, and it is the part leadership has not seen yet.

With leadership I would say it this way: "I would like us to agree on how we measure the gain. Can we start with goal progress and customer value, instead of how much the team made?"

A goal the team already owns is the easiest place to start. If the team owns a release date, goal progress is how much of the plan is finished against it. If it owns a customer problem, customer value is whether that problem got smaller.

Four at once is too many. One measure everyone agrees on is worth more than four on a slide.

Those measures only work if leadership agrees they are the right ones, and you cannot guess that. The next step is to ask them.

What to Do Monday

Ask your director one question: "What would you need to see to say the AI spend paid for itself?" Write down the answer in their words.

Then match it to one measure for your team's next sprint. If they say they want faster answers for customers, the measure is days from first concept to customer decision, tracked each sprint next to what the sprint costs. That is an example, not a benchmark. Use whatever words they gave you.

Do not bring a dashboard to that conversation. Bring the question and a pen.

If your leadership asked tomorrow whether AI was paying off, what is the one number you could put in front of them?
