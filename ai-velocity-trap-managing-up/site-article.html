AI Productivity Expectations: What to Tell Your Director

Quick Bridge: If you are trying to reset AI productivity expectations with your director, you do not need a better dashboard first. You can have an honest answer ready and a short list of outcome measures to propose.

The quarterly review is nearly over when finance asks one more question: what did we get for the AI licenses? Everyone turns to you.

You know the team is doing more than it did a year ago. You also know that "more" is not an answer to a question about money, and that whatever you say in the next ten seconds will set the tone for the next budget cycle.

What Leadership Means When They Ask About AI Productivity

The question sounds like a request for a report. Underneath it is a purchase decision. I have been on both sides of that question. When I am the one asking, someone approved the licenses and I want to hear that the money was well spent, because I have to carry that answer to the people above me.

What I am usually listening for is savings: fewer hours on the same work, lower cost, or the same output with a smaller team. That is a reasonable thing to expect from a tool that writes code and drafts documents in seconds.

When the answer comes back as "the team is busy" or "people like the tools," I hear a gap. I have nothing to carry upward.

Where the Saved Time Actually Goes

The last time leadership asked me whether we were really seeing gains that justify the spend, my answer was no. We are not really seeing savings, not because the tools are not helping but because we are just doing more. If we would have created 3 concepts to choose from before now we create 15.

By concept I mean an idea for what to build, worked out enough to choose between. Producing options got cheap, so the team produces more of them and fills the time it was given.

DX, a company that sells developer analytics, looked at a random sample drawn from 400 companies between November 2024 and February 2026. Average AI usage rose 65%, and pull request throughput rose 7.76%, which DX's own headline rounds to 10%. A pull request is a proposed code change, so this is a count of one kind of finished work. Usage climbed far faster than output. It is one data point from one vendor and one narrow way to count, so read it as a direction.

There is also a gap between how fast work feels and how fast it is. A 2025 randomized trial by the research group METR, with experienced open-source developers working on their own projects, found they took 19% longer with AI tools while believing they had gone faster. METR now labels those early-2025 results out of date, so treat it as a reminder that speed is easy to feel and hard to measure.

Resetting AI Productivity Expectations Before the Next Review

The reset is a short conversation, and it works best before anyone is asking.

First, say plainly what is true. You are not defending the tools or apologizing for them. You are describing where the time went: "We are not seeing savings. The tools are helping, and the team is using the time to do more of the work, not to spend less of it." Saying it before anyone asks is the habit I described in an earlier post on showing your work before anyone asks.

Second, name the gap. Nobody agreed ahead of time what the extra time should buy, so there is nothing to compare it with. This is close to the idea in Stop solving problems that don't exist yet, where AI made experiments cheap and teams still plan as if each one is costly.

Third, ask what they would need to see before they would say the spend paid for itself. Their answer tells you which measure to use, and it makes them a partner in the definition of success instead of the audience for your report. I wrote about asking what success looks like before you assume in another post, and the same question applies here.

Four Outcome Measures to Put in Front of Leadership

The gain shows up in goal progress, outcomes delivered, customer value delivered, and true savings measured and locked in. Here is what each one looks like.

Goal progress is how much of a plan the team has finished against a date or target it already owns. If the goal is a release by the end of the quarter, ask how much closer the AI work moved you to it.

Outcomes delivered are results a person outside the team would notice: a problem fixed, a request closed, a decision made faster. Finished tasks are activity, and the outcome is what changed because of them.

Customer value delivered asks whether a customer's problem got smaller, which is the hardest to measure and the easiest for a director to repeat upward. Savings measured and locked in means the hours or dollars saved come out of the plan. If the freed time is simply left for the team to fill, it will be filled, which is exactly what happened to the time we gave my team.

Cost per sprint turns this into numbers. A sprint is a short, repeating cycle of work that many software teams use.

I once worked out what a team cost for one sprint, and at the end of each one I asked whether we had produced that much value for the company. With made-up numbers: if one sprint costs $20,000, the question is whether the sprint produced $20,000 worth of value.

I would use that frame again for the AI conversation. It also shows which of those concepts earned their place.

What to Say When Leadership Expects a Multiplier

The conversation usually arrives with a number attached, and a few follow-up questions come with it.

"Why has our output not doubled or tripled?" What I say is that output did go up, the extra work went to more options, and the thing that did not go up is the value we agreed to look for, so I offer to agree on that value now.

"Are the tools not working?" They are. On my teams the work has moved toward planning how things should work and away from typing them, and I say so, along with what I am measuring to see the effect.

"Can you give me a number by next month?" Give them one that exists today: cost per sprint, and which concepts moved a goal. Promise only a savings figure you have locked in.

"What if the extra options are better?" They may be. I do not know that 15 is worse than 3, and I will not know until I can see which of them moved a goal or reached a customer.

"Where else is the team stuck?" Sometimes it is working faster than the process around it, which is the pattern in Stop Optimizing for The Past, and sometimes it is stuck deciding what to build. Neither of those shows up in an output count, and both are worth saying out loud to your director, because they point to where the next gain is.

Monday Try This

Choose one conversation you can have this week, with your director or your finance partner, and ask the single question: "What would you need to see to say the AI spend paid for itself?" Write their answer down in their words, not yours.

Then look at the four measures above and find the one that is closest to what they said. If they care about speed to customers, you might track the days from a first idea to a customer decision. If they care about cost, start with cost per sprint. Bring the measure to your next review as the first slide, ahead of any list of what the team did. It is a relief to stop handing over a list of activity and hoping it reads as value.

You do not need new tooling for this. You need one answer from one person and one measure you both agreed on.
