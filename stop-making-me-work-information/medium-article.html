Stop Making Me Work for Your Information

Quick Bridge: Your boss is not going to build the story out of your charts. That is not what they asked for. What they keep noticing is that they asked for your interpretation and got the raw material instead. The fix is putting your interpretation first, with the detail underneath. Start with your next update.

The presentation was all charts. No titles that said anything, no sentences, nothing that told me what to take from any of them.

A team member had sent it as their end-of-year performance data. I was busy, so I scanned it the way I scan most things on a full day. I found numbers. I did not find the point.

Then came the thought I knew was coming. I would have to explain these charts to my own leadership and peers, and I could not do that yet.

The cost of a data dump is bigger than it looks, the reasons people send one are not laziness, and the fix is about order.

What a Data Dump Costs the Person Who Asked

The first cost is time. When I talk with this person, they either read the data to me or explain where the numbers came from. Either way, I have to become more of an expert on their work before I can turn it into a message for anyone else.

I asked this person because they know these numbers better than I do. A pile of charts hands the figuring-out back to the one with less context. The work I delegated came back to my desk.

When my car is broken, I ask the service advisor to figure out what is wrong. I do not want a stack of diagrams to sort through. I want their answer, and enough of the story to check that it makes sense.

The rest of the cost shows up in other places. If I do not have time to read it, I may not read it at all, and that limits the recognition for this person and their team. If I read it fast, I may assume something and report the results as better or worse than they are.

It can make their performance look bad when I am only trying to show their best results. And the answer comes later, because I have to schedule time to read it properly.

I do not skip these emails. I scan when I am busy, and it feels like this: I open the deck hoping the point is in there somewhere, and I am not sure I am reading it right. If the subject line and the first two or three sentences do not get to the point, I have to set time aside before I can respond. Then I either put it off or guess.

Many managers I know read only the first part and either move on or answer with bad information. That is what I have seen, not a study.

I put it this way once in a thread about senior leaders: getting an update is not a waste of time. Making me read through an email history to get the information I want is a waste of my time.

The person who sent the charts knows the work. So why send it this way?

Why Smart People Send Them

Nobody sets out to hand their boss homework. I see three reasons.

Two of them are easy to see. School taught many of us creative writing, where you build to a climax, and very few of us were taught technical or business writing, where the point comes first. And pride: they want you to see how much work they did, and they are probably proud of it.

The third is the one people rarely say out loud. Making a decision is uncomfortable. If you front-load the details, someone else has to decide. Sharing detail feels safe, and committing to a view that someone might disagree with does not.

If you recognize yourself in any of those, you are in good company. The work behind the charts is real. It just needs to be easier to find.

Put Your Point First and the Detail Underneath

Order matters more than length. I will stop reading when I have what I need.

So the fix is not a shorter email, it is a different one: start with where things stand and what you think, and put the supporting detail underneath for the person who wants to dig in. Keep the raw data available for anyone who wants to check it.

There is a name for this, bottom line up front, but you do not need the name to use it.

I am coaching my team member to tell me stories about their data. Instead of "our hours per defect is 37," I want to hear this:

"Our hours per defect moved from 42 to 37 over the last year. That shows we are getting better at knowing where to look for defects, and we still have work to do to reach our goal of 10."

The first version is a number. The second is a status, an interpretation of what it means, and what is still open.

In an email, that looks like this.

Before. Subject: "Year-end results." Body: "See attached."

After. Subject: "Year-end results: hours per defect down to 37, goal is 10." Body: the two sentences above, then a third: "I am planning to keep looking where we are looking for another quarter." Then one more line: "Charts and source numbers are below if you want to dig in."

A test I use: what does the reader see in the first 30 seconds, and is that what you need them to know?

I can only think of one exception. If the task was only to give me data so I could synthesize it myself, then the data is the job. Even then, share what you made of it. That is how you show your thinking and the way you work.

Monday Try This

Before you send your next update, write three sentences at the top, in this order: where things stand, what it means to you, and what you need from the reader or what you recommend. Then add one line: "Detail below if you want it."

Read only your subject line and those three sentences. Could your reader act on them? If yes, send it.

Think about the last update you sent. If your reader only read the subject line and the first three sentences, would they know what you think?

You're great at the work. Let's make you impossible to ignore.
