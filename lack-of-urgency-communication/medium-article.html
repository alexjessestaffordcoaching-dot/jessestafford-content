Why Your Team Doesn't Feel the Urgency You Do

Quick Bridge: If you keep saying something is urgent and your team keeps treating it like it isn't, the problem is not their motivation. You handed them the deadline and skipped the reason. Share the why the same way you share the what, and urgency stops being something you have to manufacture every week.

I told my team a date mattered. I said it twice, actually. Once in standup, once in a follow-up message with the deadline bolded.

Two weeks later, the work was moving at the same pace as everything else on the board. Nobody had pushed back. Nobody had said the date was wrong. They just were not treating it like I was.

I got frustrated before I got curious. My first read was that they did not share my sense of urgency, that I was the only one who understood how much this mattered.

I have seen this exact assumption wreck things before, on my own team. They found a fast workaround to hit a deadline, executed it cleanly, and only found out two months later that no one had ever asked if the date could move. They assumed the deadline was the goal. It was just a date someone had picked.

That is what was happening with my own team, from the other side. I had handed them a date. I had never handed them a reason.

The Gap Between Telling and Explaining

Here is the pattern once I actually looked at it: I had told them what to do and when. I had not told them why it mattered, what would happen if it slipped, or who was waiting on the other end of it.

To me, the stakes were obvious. I had been in the room where the priority got set. I knew the customer commitment behind it, the domino it would knock over if we missed it, the conversation I would have to have with my own boss if the date passed.

My team knew none of that. They had the instruction. They did not have the context that made the instruction feel different from every other task already on their plate.

This is not a rare failure. Something close to a third of employees say they are regularly guessing at their real priorities at work, and the majority of employees do not fully understand the strategy their daily tasks are supposed to serve. Urgency does not fail to spread because people do not care. It fails to spread because the reason never left the leader's head.

Urgency is not something you announce. It is something you hand someone, in full, or you do not hand it at all.

The Shift

The reframe that changed how I assign anything: if my team is not treating something with the weight I think it deserves, that is not a read on their character. It is a read on how much of the actual stakes I gave them.

I stopped asking "why isn't this moving faster" and started asking myself "did I actually explain why this matters, or did I just say it was important." Almost every time, the honest answer was that I had skipped straight to the ask.

This is a leadership admission, not a team diagnosis. It is a smaller shift than it sounds like, and it is uncomfortable the first few times, because it means owning that the slow pace was something you built, not something they chose.

I saw this play out again later, in a completely different situation, when an individual contributor kept blocking a launch over a documentation policy. Instead of arguing the policy, I went to their manager and said: "Help me understand what you need to make this work. Here is the goal I need to achieve and why it matters." The moment the why was on the table, the resistance was not really resistance anymore. It was just a person who had never been given a reason to bend.

How to Apply It

1. Before you assign anything as urgent, say the why out loud first, to yourself. If you cannot answer "why does this date matter" in one sentence, you are not ready to hand it to your team yet. Figure that out before you open your mouth.

2. Replace "this is urgent" with the actual consequence.** Not "this needs to happen by Friday." Try: "If this slips past Friday, the customer walks into the renewal conversation with the wrong numbers, and that call is one I am having, not you." **Specific stakes travel. A label does not.

3. Use the direct version of the ask. Something close to: "Here is the goal I need to achieve, and here is why it matters." Say it plainly, without softening it into a suggestion.

4. Do not turn every task into an emergency. Sharing the why is not permission to make everything urgent. If three tasks are all "critical" this week, none of them are, and your team will learn to discount the next one too. Save the full explanation for what is actually time-sensitive, and be honest when something is not.

One more thing worth naming. Sharing the why is not a fix for a deadline that was never realistic to begin with. If the plan was wrong, no amount of explaining the stakes will make the math work. This is specifically for the priorities that are legitimate but under-explained, not a patch for bad planning.

Monday Action

Pick one thing currently sitting on your team's board that you consider urgent but that is not moving like it. Before you follow up on it again, write down the actual consequence of it slipping, in one sentence, and say that sentence to the person doing the work instead of repeating the deadline.

What is one thing on your team's plate right now where you have said "urgent" but never said why?
