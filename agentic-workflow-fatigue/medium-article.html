How to Keep AI From Turning Your Best Engineers Into Tired Coordinators

Quick Bridge: Your strongest engineers are faster than ever and quietly flat. They are steering AI work most of the day and finishing fewer things they can point to and call their own. You may be feeling the same shift in your own week.

When a run of meetings with other leaders wears me down, I go find a tedious task to climb back out. Lately that has been data.

The last time, it was our quality metrics. I spent a while learning the difference between leading and lagging indicators. I set goals for what the data should actually tell us. Then I spent a couple of hours a day in the reports and the databases, writing scripts to automate the numbers so the team could tell a clear story with them.

Nobody assigned me that. I used it as reset time. A few hours of building something that had a start and a finish, and I came back steadier than I left.

There are newer studies on burnout that say it is less about being overworked and more about not feeling progress. That matches what I see in myself. When I am scraped out, the fix is usually finding something I can make progress on and staying with it. Building does that. Steering does not.

A while back I wrote about why the workday got denser after teams picked up AI tools. The short version: they cleared out the low-focus work that used to pace the hard work, so the hours left run heavier per hour. That piece was about the shape of the workday.

This one is about the person working inside it, and it is narrower than burnout. Your fast, capable engineer says the tools are great and is still hollowed out by three in the afternoon, and cannot say why, because every visible measure says the day was easier. Time off does not fix it, because the cause is what the work is made of now, not how much of it there is.

Building pays you back. Steering just spends you down.

Making something gives you a finish line you can see. A test that was red goes green, or a knot you have been picking at for an hour comes loose, and you can point at it and say you built it. The workday used to be paced by a steady supply of those small wins, and they added up to more than they looked like.

Two researchers, Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer, spent years collecting daily work diaries from hundreds of professionals. Their finding: the single biggest driver of a good day at work is making progress on something that matters, and small wins count for more than their size. In a survey alongside the study, managers ranked support for progress dead last out of five motivators. The thing that most protects your team is the thing we most reliably miss.

Agent work removes most of those finish lines and leaves a different kind of effort in their place. You are holding three or four agent tasks in your head at once, judging whether output you did not write is good enough, tracing which inputs produced it, and keeping track of what is actually done versus what only looks done. Then you have to assemble a shippable whole out of the pieces.

The brain is not built to run that mode for long. Watching for the rare costly error in a stream of output that is mostly fine is one of the hardest things to keep doing well, and attention slides off it fast. AI output is a near-perfect version of that problem: fluent, well formatted, mostly right, with the one expensive error buried in a lot of plausible material.

Software runs on thought energy, and there are only so many hours a day a person can hold high focus before it costs them. That ceiling did not move when the tools showed up. What changed is what refills the budget. Building work used to top it back up. Steering work draws it down and gives nothing back.

It is not the model. It is the operating model.

The common explanation is that the AI is not good enough yet, that people need to prompt better or wait for the next version. Set that aside. Even when the output is solid, the mode wears people down, for two reasons that stack on top of each other.

The first is the switching. Running several agent sessions at once means kicking one off, tabbing away while it works, picking up the next, then coming back to the first one half done. Every one of those switches leaves part of your attention stuck on the thing you just left. It is worst when the task you left was unfinished, and with parallel agent tasks every one of them is.

The second is the management work nobody named. Running a set of agent tasks is a project-management job in everything but title: break the work down, hand each piece to the right tool, decide whether what comes back clears the bar, trace what produced it, track real progress against the look of it. Then pull it together into something shippable.

That work used to belong to managers and project managers. Now it sits on every engineer, and nobody scheduled the training for it or built in the recovery time around it.

Underneath both is the same catch. You are handing work to something you do not fully trust, so you follow up on everything it gives back. That is the load of running a team without the earned trust that lets a manager stop checking every line. You get the oversight cost and none of the relief.

Around the same time I was hiding in those quality metrics, I spent an afternoon steering an AI through an analysis of survey data. It locked onto my first reaction to one bad number and kept circling how to avoid it, and I had to keep redirecting it until it was useful. That loop, prompt and watch it drift and pull it back, took more out of me than doing the analysis by hand would have.

One more note, and I hold this one lightly. The people feeling it worst are often the ones with the least room to spare: junior developers, pushed into defining and judging software before they have the mental models to move quickly. The seniors look energized on the surface, and some genuinely are. Do not read that as the whole story.

What a leader actually does about it

I am not going to hand you a structural fix here. The workday redesign is the companion piece. This part is about helping each person carry the new mode, and it looks different for different people.

1. Make unproductive time OK during work hours. The gap while an agent runs, or the ten minutes after a hard call, is recovery doing real work. People will not take it if they expect to be asked why they were away from the keyboard. To me, that question is most of what people are resisting about return-to-office. They do not want to account for every hour. I tell my team plainly that time between tasks is expected, and they do not need to explain it to me.

2. Teach people how to use that time. Left alone, people tend to fill the gap with another agent task or a scroll, and neither one is recovery. The skill worth coaching is noticing what you actually need in that moment, whether that is a walk, a hard problem worked by hand, or ten quiet minutes. It is personal, so you cannot mandate it. You can model it and talk about it in one-on-ones.

3. Make sure everyone still owns something end to end. When the coordinating work drained me, I went and built something with my name on it and it reset me. If someone steers all day and never finishes anything recognizably theirs, that is the thing to fix. Give them a piece of work they carry start to finish, even a small one.

4. Run fewer agent tasks at once, where the work allows. The switching is the core of the drain. Two sessions run well beats five run badly, and it costs the person less. I have landed at two or three myself before the day blurs. Offer it as a habit to try, not a rule.

5. Ask about the feeling, because the dashboard will not. The visible numbers after an AI rollout tend to look good across the board, so I stopped reading them for this. Ask in one-on-ones whether the work still feels like theirs, and when they last finished something and felt it. Treat a blank answer as a load problem to solve together, not a performance issue to raise.

Monday, Try This

At your next one-on-one, ask each person one question: what did you build this week that felt like yours? Push past what shipped and get to the thing they made and can point at. If the answer comes back empty, do not turn it into a performance conversation. Spend the rest of the hour working out where the building went, and how to get a piece of it back.
