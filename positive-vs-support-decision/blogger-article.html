Toxic Positivity at Work: Why Your Team Doesn't Need You to Fake Enthusiasm

Quick Bridge: If you have ever stood in front of your team after a layoff or a disappointing raise cycle and forced a smile you did not feel, this post is for you. Toxic positivity is a named, measured problem, not just a personality quirk, and the fix is not "be more honest." It is learning the specific difference between liking a decision and supporting it, and the exact language that lets you do the second without faking the first.

A round of layoffs had just gone through, and I had a team of people left standing in the wreckage of it, wondering if they were next.

I had two instincts fighting each other. One told me to keep morale up, stay upbeat, remind everyone the company still had a bright future. The other told me to be real with them, which in the moment felt like it meant venting my own doubts about the decision out loud.

Neither instinct was right. What I actually needed to do was something in between that I had never been taught: stay steady, acknowledge the decision hurt, and still stand behind it, without pretending I liked it.

The Toxic Positivity Problem Leaders Don't Realize They Have

Toxic positivity is not a personality quirk. iHire's 2026 Toxic Workplace Trends Report found that roughly 68% of employees report experiencing toxic positivity from someone at work recently, and about 75% admit they suppress genuine negative feelings just to conform to a forced sense of optimism (iHire, Toxic Workplace Trends Report 2026).

SHRM has published explicit guidance on identifying and preventing toxic positivity in the workplace, treating it as a recognized management failure mode rather than a personality trait some managers happen to have (SHRM, Identifying and Preventing Toxic Positivity). That matters, because the leaders I have coached through this are rarely faking it on purpose. They think forced cheer is the kind thing to do. It is usually the opposite.

The failure mode that gets less attention is the mirror image of it: the leader who swings the other way and openly commiserates with the team, "yeah, this is dumb, I don't get it either." That version feels honest in the moment too. It just quietly tells the team their own manager does not believe in the decision either, which is a worse problem than a little forced cheer.

Liking a Decision and Supporting It Are Two Separate Jobs

Here is the reframe that actually changed how I handle these conversations. Nobody on my team was ever asking me to feel good about a shrinking raise pool or a round of layoffs. They were asking whether I would stand behind the outcome, or quietly signal it wasn't worth respecting.

Those are two different questions, and most advice about delivering bad news treats them as one. "Be honest with your team" is true, but it does not tell you which honesty you owe them: honesty about your feelings, or honesty about your alignment. You actually owe them the second one. The first one is optional, and performing it when you do not feel it is where toxic positivity starts.

There is an idea about confronting brutal facts while keeping faith you will get through them. What I am describing here is smaller than that. This is about one conversation, one decision you did not make and cannot undo.

The Sourced Fact: Transparency Beats Forced Optimism

The research backs up which side of this actually earns trust. O.C. Tanner's 2026 Global Culture Report found that employees who see their employer as transparent report meaningfully lower job-security anxiety than those who don't, even when the news itself is not good (O.C. Tanner, 2026 Global Culture Report: Transparency Revisited). Gallup's research adds a second data point: 29% of employees say they lack clear, honest, or consistent communication from leaders, and overall trust that employers "do what is right" slipped further in 2025 (Great Place to Work, How to Reverse New Record Decline in Employee Trust).

Put those together and the picture is clear. Employees are not asking their leaders to feel good about hard decisions. They are asking to be able to trust what their leader says, and forced positivity is one of the fastest ways to burn that trust down.

What to Actually Say When You Don't Agree With the Decision

Once I separated liking from supporting, the script got simpler.

Say both halves as separate sentences, not one blended one. "I do not love this number. I do support it, and here is why." Not "this is a tough one, but it is what it is," which hides both halves and commits to neither.

Give the real reason, not the corporate one. With comp decisions specifically, that meant explaining the actual constraint out loud: I was handed a fixed pool, I had to balance skill, contribution, and pay equity across the whole team, and going over budget meant the business would choose who got zero instead of me. That is not a comfortable reason. It lands better than a smooth one anyway.

Stop talking sooner than feels natural. People need a real conversation where they can say what they actually think, not a monologue where you explain yourself twice hoping it will land better the second time. You do not need to fix how they feel about it. You need them to feel heard.

Name what you see them doing. A number on a page can say "not enough." A person looking someone in the eye and naming their specific contribution says something different, and it costs the company nothing to do it.

What Not to Do: Overcorrecting Into Flat Honesty

There is a real trap on the other side of this too, and it is worth naming directly. Some leaders hear "don't fake positivity" and swing all the way into flat, grim delivery, treating any warmth as dishonest.

That overcorrection misses the point just as badly. Your team takes real cues from your energy, and a leader who loses their composure can tank morale faster than the original decision did. You can still be calm.

You can still tell your team you believe in them and mean it. What you cannot honestly do is pretend you liked the outcome itself. Steady is not the same as cheerful, and most teams can tell the difference between the two without being told.

This Week's Move

Before your next conversation about something you do not personally love, write two separate sentences on paper first. One: what you do not love about it. Two: why you support it anyway.

If you can write both sentences honestly, you are ready for the room. If you get stuck on the second one, that is real information. It means you need to go ask more questions above you before you deliver anything below you, not fake the rest with tone.
