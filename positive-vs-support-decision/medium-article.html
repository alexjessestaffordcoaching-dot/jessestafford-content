How to Tell Your Team About a Decision You Don't Like

Quick Bridge: Your boss and the company is not asking you to be happy about a layoff or a disappointing raise. They are asking whether you will stand behind the decision or quietly undermine it while pretending to be fine. This post gives you the exact split, liking a decision versus supporting it, and the language to hold that line in the room.

I used to get handed a number every year. A total dollar amount, split across my whole team, that was never enough to cover what everyone actually deserved.

Some people got a real increase. Some got a token bump that barely covered inflation. A few, the ones I fought hardest for and still lost, got nothing, not because they had not earned it, but because the math above me did not leave room. If I went over budget, the business would choose who got zero for me.

Then I had to sit across from each person and explain it.

I have walked through both moves myself, more than once. There were years I smiled through a number I did not believe in, telling someone the company valued them while handing them a figure that said otherwise. Other rooms, I let my own frustration in instead. Told someone honestly that I thought the call was unfair, and watched them walk out believing their own manager did not back it either.

Neither one worked. Both left the same residue: people stopped trusting what came out of my mouth the next time I needed them to believe me.

The Two Doors Leaders Keep Choosing Between

It is the same choice every time. Perform enthusiasm you do not feel, or let your own doubt leak out and undercut the decision in front of your team.

Both feel honest in the moment, but neither is. The first one lies about how you feel. The second one lies about whether you actually stand behind the call. That lie costs more, because your team is watching you for the signal to keep pulling in the same direction.

You Don't Have to Like a Decision to Support It

Here is the split I eventually landed on. Liking a decision and supporting it are two different jobs.

Nobody on my team was ever asking me to feel good about a shrinking raise pool. They were asking whether I would stand behind the outcome once I walked out of that room, or whether I would keep venting about it to anyone who would listen. Those are not the same question, and treating them as the same question is what puts leaders on one of the two bad doors.

The question is not whether you believe in the company's long-term direction. The question is whether you can support this specific call, in this specific room, to the person sitting across from you. That is a narrower target than it looks.

None of this means walking in flat or grim. Your team reads your energy and takes real cues from it. I could still be calm, tell my team I believed in them, and mean it. What I could not honestly do was pretend I liked the number sitting in front of both of us, and your team can usually tell the difference between steady and cheerful.

What I Actually Started Saying

Once I separated the two, the conversation got simpler, not harder.

I stopped blending "I do not love this" and "I support this" into one mushy line. I said them as two separate, plain statements: "I do not love this number." I do support it, and here is why. Folding them together into something like "this is a tough one, but it is what it is" hides both halves and lands as neither honest nor aligned.

I gave the real reason, not a corporate one. With comp, that meant explaining the actual constraint: I was handed a fixed pool, I had to balance skill, contribution, and pay equity across the whole team, and going over budget meant the business would choose who got zero instead of me.

The corporate version of that explanation is "budget was tight this year." Almost every manager says something like this. It answers the question technically while telling the person nothing about why it happened to them specifically.

The real version is: I had a number. I had to split it. I made a call across the whole team about who needed it most this cycle, and that call is why we are sitting here. That is uncomfortable to say. It is also the only explanation that actually closes the information gap for the person across from you. They were not asking to feel better. They were asking why. The real reason is the answer to the actual question.

Then I stopped talking and started listening. This is the same thing I do with a team after a layoff: people need a real 1-on-1 where they can say what they actually think, not a monologue where I explain myself twice. I do not need to fix the number. I need them to feel heard about it.

The instinct is to keep talking. You want them to understand. You want them to agree. You want to feel like the conversation landed before you walk out. That instinct is usually backwards. The person across from you does not need more explanation. They need space to say the news was disappointing, or that they are not sure what to make of it. Most of the time, that is the whole job.

And I said the thing out loud that is easy to skip: I saw what they were doing, and I valued it. A number on a page can say "not enough." A person looking you in the eye and naming your specific contribution says something different, and it does not cost the company a dollar to do it.

Your Monday Action

Before your next conversation about something you do not personally love, write two separate sentences on paper first. One: what you do not love about it. Two: why you support it anyway.

If you can write both sentences honestly, you are ready for the room. If you get stuck on the second one, that is real information. It means you need to go ask more questions above you before you deliver anything below you, not fake the rest with tone.
