Stop Handing Your Team a Category. Give Them a Behavior.

Quick Bridge: If you've ever told someone to "communicate better" or "be more strategic," you handed them a category, not an instruction. A four-part formula, situation, behavior, impact, better, turns that category into something they can actually act on Monday morning, and it takes the same ten seconds to say.

The first time I coached a new manager through their first round of real feedback, my instinct was to hand them the easy version. Tell the person to communicate better going forward. It would have taken five seconds and meant nothing to anyone.

So instead I gave them a formula. It's called SBI: situation, behavior, impact, plus what better looks like. A specific situation, time and place. What the person did or said.

The impact it had on the person, the team, the project. What would have been better instead.

Here's the example I used: "Yesterday in our team meeting, you challenged Sam in a combative and confrontational way, by saying X. This caused the meeting to go off topic and others stopped contributing. It would have been better to say something like Y. That would have encouraged conversation instead of shutting it down."

Most managers never get anywhere close to that. They stop at the category and call it a day.

The Feedback That Isn't Feedback

"Communicate better."

"Be more strategic."

"Show more ownership."

These get said in performance reviews and 1-on-1s constantly. They sound like feedback. They get delivered with the tone of feedback, the eye contact of feedback, sometimes even a follow-up email confirming it was said.

None of it is feedback. It's a category label with nothing inside it.

"Communicate better" doesn't tell someone what to do differently on Monday. It tells them there's a problem and leaves them to guess the shape of it.

Which meeting? Which message? Better how, faster, slower, shorter, more context, less jargon, sent to different people?

Vague feedback like this doesn't fail because the manager didn't care. It fails because a category is faster to say than a full, specific statement. "Communicate better" takes two seconds. Describing the actual situation, the actual behavior, and the actual impact takes more thought than most people put into a 1-on-1 on a Tuesday afternoon.

The Shift: Name the Behavior, Not the Trait

Here's what changes the moment you stop handing someone a category and start handing them a behavior.

A trait sounds like an identity. "You're not strategic" is a judgment about who someone is. It's almost impossible to act on, because there's no specific thing to start or stop doing. The person walks away with a bruise and no instructions.

A behavior is something that happened. It has a time, a place, and words that were said. It can be repeated or avoided. That's the entire difference between feedback that lands and feedback that gets nodded at and forgotten by Thursday.

The formula for getting there: name the situation, name the behavior, name the impact, name what better looks like.

Situation is one specific moment, not "you always" or "you tend to." When and where did this actually happen?

Behavior is the observable thing they said or did, not your interpretation of their intent or a summary of a pattern.

Impact is what happened because of it. On the meeting, the project, another person, the outcome.

And better is what you would have wanted instead, said in language they could actually use next time.

None of the four are optional. Skip the situation and it becomes a pattern accusation. Skip the impact and it becomes nitpicking. Skip "better" and you've just criticized without giving them anywhere to go.

How to Apply It

1. Catch yourself before you say the category. The moment "communicate better" or "be more strategic" is about to come out of your mouth, stop. Ask yourself: what specific thing happened that made me want to say this? If you can't answer that in one sentence, you're not ready to give this feedback yet.

2. Turn "communicate better" into a situation. Instead of the category, try: "In yesterday's stakeholder update, you sent the technical details first and the recommendation last. Two people asked what you actually wanted from them. Leading with the ask next time would get you a faster answer."

3. Turn "be more strategic" into a situation. Instead of: "In the roadmap review, you proposed the fix without saying what business problem it solved. Leadership asked you to justify it on the spot. Next time, open with the business reason before the technical plan."

4. Turn "show more ownership" into a situation. Instead of: "When the deploy broke Tuesday, you flagged it in the team channel and waited for someone to respond. An hour passed before anyone saw it. Next time, call the on-call engineer directly and update the channel after."

None of these take longer to say than the vague version once you've built the habit. The work isn't in the delivery. It's in the ten seconds beforehand where you make yourself specific.

That specificity is also why this lands even when it's hard to hear. Gallup has found that only 16% of employees say their most recent conversation with a manager felt deeply meaningful, even though 80% of people who get feedback that actually feels meaningful stay fully engaged. The problem isn't that managers aren't giving feedback. It's that most of what gets called feedback is too vague to be meaningful to the person receiving it.

Why the Category Feels Easier

I get why the shortcut is tempting. Naming a category feels like you've said something true without having to defend a specific claim. "Be more strategic" is hard to argue with because it's hard to even pin down.

But that's exactly the problem. If the person on the receiving end can't argue with it, they also can't act on it.

Staying vague is easier on you too. You never have to point at the exact moment, the exact words, the exact impact, and defend that you read it right. Specific feedback is more work, and it's also the only version that actually changes what happens next time.

Before your next 1-on-1 with them, write down the actual situation, the specific behavior, the impact it had, and what better looks like. Say that instead.
