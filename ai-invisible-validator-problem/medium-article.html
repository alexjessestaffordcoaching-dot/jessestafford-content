How to Get Credit for Catching What AI Gets Wrong

Quick Bridge: You checked the work, you found what was wrong, and you fixed it before anyone used it. Nobody outside your team knows, because from the outside nothing happened.

I was reading the statements of work for a contract. I read the whole thing, because I had some extra unallocated moments. A teammate had not read them. They had produced an AI summary instead.

The summary had hallucinations and errors that would have set back our negotiations.

Because I had read the source, the negotiations were never set back. That is the entire result. No crisis, no recovery plan, no story to tell at the next team meeting. And no one reacted, because from the outside nothing had happened.

I keep seeing the same shape on my teams right now.

The Work That Only Shows Up as Nothing Happening

On my teams, bug bashes before release are one version of it. We built them to get the most eyes possible on a project before it shipped. They turned up several problems that would have been major after release. We managed them early, and almost nobody noticed.

When you handle a problem well, it stays invisible. QA and engineering teams do this many times over. AI tools are now adding to the pile, because a model can hand you something that looks finished and is not.

Some of the engineers I read online complain about spending more time reviewing AI output. That is not quite what I see on my teams. What I see is the harder question: how do we keep quality high when people who are not yet experienced at driving AI to good results are the ones submitting work?

The checking is real work, and it can feel thankless. You read the whole thing while everyone else moves on, and then you have to decide whether to say anything, because saying it can feel like pointing at a teammate. A lot of people quietly fix it and keep going. Then the week ends, and the "what did you do" update has nothing to say about it.

A September 2025 study from BetterUp Labs and Stanford's Social Media Lab found that sorting out one low-quality AI deliverable took the person receiving it close to two hours (HBR). Generating something is cheap. Catching what is wrong with it is not.

And the checking leaves nothing behind. No pull request, no feature, no demo, just an absence where the problem would have been. A good catch looks exactly like a quiet week.

Whose Job Is It to Say So?

When I asked myself if making this visible should be more of a manager responsibility or an individual responsibility, I landed on both. The person who did the checking, and the manager who could be asking.

Managers only know what their team tells them. Then they have to amplify that message to the people above them. People tend to believe their manager knows and understands everything they are doing, and it just is not true. Do you know everything your manager does? Nope.

If you caught something and said nothing, your manager has no idea it happened. They cannot repeat what they never heard.

Quality is everyone's problem. Anyone who is working to only solve their own task is not paying attention to what the product and the business really need.

The first line of defense belongs to whoever brings the tool in. If you are the one promoting an AI tool on your team, you own the first check. After that, everyone needs to be involved.

I have spent years telling people to show their work. That advice assumes you built something to point at. The person doing the checking did not build the thing. Their value is what did not happen, and you cannot share a plan for work that was never needed.

Give Your Manager Something to Repeat

I do not have a good answer to this yet. What I keep coming back to is this: make it about business impact, not technical challenge.

Tie it up for your manager and give them something they can repeat with minimal translation. If they have to decode it first, it stops with them.

Your manager carries your work into rooms you are not in, so use three parts in one or two sentences: what you caught, what it would have cost the business, and what you did about it.

"I read the full statements of work and found errors in the AI summary. If we had worked from it, our negotiations would have been set back. I corrected it before anyone used it."

It works for team-level catches too: "Our pre-release review turned up problems that would have been major after launch. We fixed them before customers saw anything."

Compare either one to "I reviewed the contract summary." Your manager can say the catch out loud in a meeting. "I reviewed it" only tells them you were busy.

Process fixes help too. A rotating quality check, or a short written reason when someone approves AI-assisted work, are worth having. They still do not tell your manager anything until someone says it out loud.

If you lead a team, there is one more move. Change what you ask about. If the only number anyone brings to you is what shipped, you will quietly teach people that checking does not count, and the careful ones will stop bringing it up at all. Ask "what did you catch this week" and then repeat the answer upward. That question changes what people bring you, and it hands you the line your own manager needs to hear.

On Monday

The next time you finish checking something and find a problem, write the sentence before you close the ticket: what you caught, what it would have cost the business, what you did. Send it to your manager that same day as a plain update, written so they could repeat it without changing a word.

If you caught something this week before anyone else saw it, who knows?

You're great at the work. Let's make you impossible to ignore.
