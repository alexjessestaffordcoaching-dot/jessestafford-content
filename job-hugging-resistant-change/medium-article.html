Quick Bridge: If someone who has been excellent at their job for years is dragging their feet on a new way of working, the problem is almost never skill and almost never attitude. It is an unnamed fear of becoming a beginner again in a role where being the expert is most of their identity. The way through it is not pressure. It is four things in the right order: make them feel heard, explain why their role is genuinely changing, help them build the new skills, and find something in the change they can actually get excited about.

What Long-Tenured Employees Resistant to Change Actually Need

I inherited a QA team a few years ago. Not through a reorg I asked for. One day they were just mine.

They had their own way of keeping score. How many defects they wrote. Whether a game reached QA on the day it was supposed to. Their lane, their rules, their sense of what it meant to do the job well.

I changed the goals. New measure: how many defects the customer found after launch. Whether the whole game team hit the release date, not just QA's piece of it.

That meant they had to work differently with everyone else on the team. Not just inside their own lane anymore.

They told me it wasn't fair. That there was nothing they could do to hit numbers that depended on people they didn't control.

I heard resistance. It took me longer than it should have to hear what it actually was: grief.

It Is Not a Motivation Problem

57% of workers now call themselves "job huggers," holding onto their current role out of fear, not love, up sharply from a year earlier. That number is not laziness showing up at scale. It is a rational read on a market where the job-switch pay bump has nearly disappeared and a new hire is the first one cut if a team shrinks.

Your longest-tenured people know that math better than anyone. They also know something else: the way they have always done the job is a big part of why they are good at it. Ask them to work differently and you are not asking them to try harder. You are asking them to become a beginner again, in public, in a role where being the expert is most of their identity.

That is a different problem than a skills gap. A skills gap is what happens when the tools change and someone can no longer do the work. This is what happens when the old way still works fine, and someone is being asked to set down a method they built their whole sense of competence on.

The resistance you are seeing is not "I can't." It is "I don't know who I am here if I'm not the person who does it this way."

The Job Isn't What's Threatened. The Identity Is.

With the QA team, nobody's role was going away. I wasn't cutting headcount or handing their work to someone else. I was changing what counted as good.

That is exactly the situation with a long-tenured employee resisting a new process or tool. The job itself is not in danger. But if all they hear is "change," and nobody tells them the job is safe, they will fill that silence with the worst version of the story they can imagine, and they will not tell you what it is because you are the person asking them to change.

Leaders read this as an attitude problem, which leads to pressure: more oversight, a written warning, a conversation that opens with "you need to be more open." That treats resistance as a will problem, and it isn't one.

Give them the context they're missing instead of a process to comply with. Context lets someone make the judgment call to let go on their own terms. Pressure just teaches them to hide the fear better.

What Actually Works

The first thing is to actually listen to what they're afraid of, and to do it before you say anything about what needs to change. Not listen in order to respond, but listen to understand the specific thing they think they're losing, because the answer they give you first is usually not the real one. "It's not fair" was never about fairness with my QA team. It was about feeling exposed in a role where they had always been the one who had things figured out. What they need first is to feel like you understand what is actually being taken from them.

Once they feel heard, the second thing they need is an honest explanation of why the role is genuinely changing, and "because leadership decided" is not that. The real version is specific: what is different about the work now, why the old way no longer fits, and what happens if nothing changes. People can accept hard things when they understand why the hard thing is necessary, and they cannot accept being told to change for reasons they are not allowed to know.

The third piece is practical: help them build the new skills rather than just assigning them a deadline to have acquired them. A tenured person resisting change is often not actually asking you to slow down. What they are asking is to not look incompetent while they learn, and that is a more solvable problem than most leaders realize. Pair them with someone who is already comfortable with the new way of working, informally, before formalizing any expectation, so there is a low-stakes context to practice in before they have to perform. The goal is to make the new way feel like something they can get good at, not just something they are obligated to accept.

The last piece matters more than most leaders give it credit for: find something in the change that the person can genuinely get excited about, and I mean something specific to them, not something corporate. What does the new way of working let them do that they could not do before? What problem does it solve that they have been quietly frustrated by for years? Every real change has at least one answer to that question. Finding it together with them is different from telling them the answer, and that difference is what separates a conversation that ends in reluctant compliance from one that ends in actual buy-in.

This is not permission to let the work stall. Keep asking them to move, and use these four steps to help them move faster, but through the real obstacle instead of around it.

Monday Action

Pick one long-tenured person on your team who has been slow to adopt something you have asked for. Before your next 1-on-1, write down the specific thing they might be afraid of losing if the change sticks. Not the skill they're missing. The identity piece underneath it. Start there, and see what it changes about the conversation.
