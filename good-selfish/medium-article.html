I Started Mentoring for Selfish Reasons.

Someone was thanking me publicly for the support I give university students in interview prep.

I was taking it in. Feeling genuinely good about it. And thinking about where it actually started.

I began mentoring university students because I kept getting terrible interviews. Candidates who had no idea how to present themselves, talk about their project or experience, what was being assessed and what we were really looking for during interviews.

I went to the source. I started talking to students directly, before they ever sat across from me in an interview.

That is where it came from. Not a calling. Not selfless generosity. I wanted to stop feeling like I was wasting my time in interviews.

Years later, I am standing in a room being recognized for the impact on those students. My honest reaction: if I had tried to start this for the "right" reasons, I do not think it would have lasted.

A five-year study of more than 1,000 employees found that mentors in structured programs were promoted six times more often than employees who did not participate. Mentees were promoted five times more often. Twenty-five percent of mentees received salary increases during the study period, compared to five percent of non-participants.

I was not aware of any of that data when I started. I was just tired of bad interviews. The outcomes showed up anyway.

---

We Are Conditioned to Feel Guilty for This

We are told from childhood that selfishness is wrong. Share. Put others first. Do not act for yourself.

When a selfish motivation shows up at work, the guilt follows automatically. You develop your team partly because it reduces your own burden. You give feedback partly because you want the behavior to change. You build your reputation through relationships you actually value.

That guilt arrives immediately. And we treat it as evidence of something wrong. It is not. It is just guilt doing what guilt does. The question worth asking is not whether the motivation is pure. It is whether what you do after the selfish thought creates something real for someone else.

---

Good Selfish Versus Bad Selfish

There is a version of selfish that is genuinely harmful. You lie for gain. You push the organization toward an outcome that benefits only your own position. You hoard information to protect your standing at someone else's expense.

Some leaders spend their entire careers trying not to become that.

That is not what I am describing.

Good selfish starts from self-interest, but the action creates something real for others. The motivation starts with you. The benefit lands somewhere else.

The line between good selfish and bad selfish is not about the motivation. It is about what you actually do.

---

What the Motivation Actually Looks Like

The examples matter here, and they are not all the same kind of selfish.

I mentor university students because I wanted to stop getting bad interviews. The self-interest was about efficiency. I was wasting time in conversations with candidates who did not understand what the role required or how to show what they actually knew. What they got was genuine preparation: how interviews work, what is actually being evaluated, and how to show what they know. The returns run both directions. Mentors in structured programs are promoted at significantly higher rates than those who do not participate.

I give feedback to my team because I want the behavior to change. That is a different kind of self-interest. I want things to work, and I want the people around me to have the information they need to work differently. What my team gets is honest, specific feedback they can actually act on, rather than the polite vagueness that often substitutes for a real conversation. My goal and what they need happen to be the same thing.

I am a coach because helping other people take control of their own career gives me real personal satisfaction. That is not pragmatism or efficiency. I genuinely enjoy it. What the people I work with get is someone actually invested in their outcome. Not someone performing interest while waiting for the conversation to end.

Three different motivations. Three different forms of self-interest. Three real outcomes for someone else. The common thread is not the type of selfish. It is the direction. The self-interest is pointed outward, toward doing something well enough that the other person gets something real from it.

None of those motivations are selfless. I am not embarrassed by any of them anymore.

If I had been doing all three because it was the right thing to do, I do not think any of them would have lasted. Acting selfless when you do not feel it runs out of fuel. Self-interest does not.

This is not an excuse to stop caring about the people around you. It is permission to stop pretending your motivations are cleaner than they are. The leaders who exhaust themselves fastest are often the ones working hardest to appear selfless. The leaders who sustain their care over time tend to be the ones whose self-interest and their team's benefit are pointing in the same direction.

---

What People Actually See

You are not going to think your way into having purely selfless motivations. Nobody does.

What you can control is what you do after the selfish thought shows up. You can develop your team because it also reduces your burden, and do it well enough that they actually grow. You can give feedback because you want the behavior to change, and make it specific enough that they can act on it. Show up consistently enough in the relationships that matter, and they become real.

Selfishness is a great motivator. What happens after the selfish thought is what people see.

The leaders who build genuinely strong teams are rarely doing it for purely selfless reasons. They are doing it because it works for them, and it also happens to work for everyone else. That is not a compromise. That is how most real things get built.

---

Recognizing the Line

The version of selfish that is actually harmful is not hard to identify. It is the promotion secured by undermining a colleague. It is the budget won by misrepresenting the numbers. It is the project steered toward your own advancement in a way that left the team holding the fallout.

Most of what leaders feel guilty about is nowhere near that line.

It is the honest reality that developing your team also develops you. That the feedback you give also serves your own need for things to work better. That the mentoring relationship you maintain also benefits your career.

If what you are doing creates real value for the people on the receiving end, the fact that it also benefits you is not the problem. It is how this works. The question is not whether your motivation is pure. The question is whether your action created something good.

If the team gets something real, that is good selfish. Keep going without apology.
