Quick Bridge: A lot of leadership advice tells you to be less selfish. This post makes a different argument. When self-interest and team benefit point in the same direction, you get better results over time than anything built on motivation you have to manufacture. Here is the distinction that matters, the data that supports it, and a practical diagnostic to tell which kind of leadership you are actually doing.

---

Selfish Reasons to Mentor and Develop Your Team (And Why That Is Not a Problem)

The leaders who exhaust themselves fastest are often the ones working hardest to have pure motivations.

They develop their team partly because it reduces their own burden and then feel guilty about it. They give feedback because they want the behavior to change and because watching someone grow genuinely makes them happy. Then they wonder if that makes the feedback less real. They mentor people partly to build their own reputation and then quietly question their own integrity.

The guilt is real. But the question it is asking is the wrong one.

---

The Leadership ROI of Self-Interested Behavior

In 2006, Gartner and Capital Analytics tracked more than 1,000 Sun Microsystems employees over five years. The results were significant.

Mentors in the program were promoted six times more often than employees who did not participate. Mentees were promoted five times more often. Twenty-five percent of mentees received salary increases during the study period, compared to five percent of non-participants. (Source: Masterful Mentoring at Sun Microsystems, Talent Management)

There is a concept called "enlightened self-interest." The research on it backs up what effective leaders already know: when your goals and your team's benefit align, the motivation holds. (Keith Edwards PhD, 2025: The Power of Enlightened Self-Interest in Leading Change)

The mechanism is not complicated. Motivation you have to manufacture needs constant renewal. Self-interest has its own fuel.

---

Good Selfish Versus Bad Selfish: The Distinction That Actually Matters for Engineering Managers

This is the distinction worth drawing carefully, because bad selfish is a real thing and it is worth taking seriously.

Bad selfish moves into "what is in it for me" at someone else's expense. You lie to secure a promotion. You push the organization toward outcomes that benefit your position at the team's cost. You hoard information to protect your standing.

Some leaders spend their entire careers trying not to become that. For good reason.

Good selfish is different. Good selfish starts from self-interest, but the action creates something real for others.

I mentor university students because I wanted to stop having bad interviews. The motivation was mine. They got better prepared for the working world.

I give feedback to my team because I want the behavior to change. My direct reports get honest, specific feedback instead of polite noise.

I am a coach because helping other people take control of their own career gives me a lot of personal joy. They get someone genuinely invested in their outcome.

All three started from something I wanted. All three created something for someone else.

The line between good selfish and bad selfish is not about motivation purity. It is about what you actually do.

---

What a 2024 Study Reveals About Self-Interested Technical Leadership

A 2024 study published in PMC examined the relationship between ambition, a form of self-interest, and leadership effectiveness. The finding that matters: self-interest directed outward, toward building teams and developing others, produces observable results that third parties confirm. Self-interest directed inward, toward protecting position and hoarding credit, produces a perception gap between how the leader sees themselves and how others experience them. (A legend in one's own mind: The link between ambition and leadership evaluations, NCBI 2024)

This is the research version of what most effective managers have figured out through experience: the direction of the self-interest is what matters. Not its existence.

Technical leaders who move into management often feel this guilt more sharply than others. Their entire career before management was built on clear, clean solutions. Efficiency. No wasted effort.

The idea that you might be doing something for mixed motivations can feel like a systems integrity error. So they try to purify the motivation rather than look at the outcome.

That is the wrong place to look.

---

The Diagnostic: How to Tell If Your Motivation Is Good Selfish or Bad Selfish

The diagnostic is simple. For each thing you regularly do for your team, run two checks:

What you do: Name the specific behavior. Mentoring. Giving feedback. Developing a skill.

What the person on the receiving end actually gets: Name the specific, observable outcome for them. Better interview readiness. Clearer direction. A skill they did not have.

If the outcome is real and you also benefit from doing it, that is good selfish. Keep going without apology.

If you cannot name a real outcome for the other person, look more carefully. Not at your motivation. At your actual behavior.

In my experience, leaders who run this check find they are further into good selfish territory than they thought. The guilt has been about the motivation. The behavior has been creating real value all along.

The leaders who should worry are the ones who run this check and cannot name a clear benefit for the other person. That is not a motivation problem. That is a behavior problem. And it is the only kind worth correcting.

---

What Sustained Care Actually Looks Like

The final thing worth saying: the leaders who develop people most consistently over ten or twenty years are rarely doing it for purely selfless reasons.

They are doing it because it works for them, and it also happens to work for everyone else. Their self-interest and their team's benefit are pointing in the same direction. That alignment is not a coincidence or a compromise. It is how sustained care actually works.

The leaders who try to be selfless when they are not cycle through burnout and renewal. The leaders who act from honest motivation, directed outward, keep going. The distinction matters over a career.
